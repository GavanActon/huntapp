# Re-anchor Groundwind's mountain heat to treeline

In September and October the research puts moose at Blanchard River and Highland Lake in the subalpine tall-willow belt, from about 250 m below to 50–100 m above the local treeline, plus the valley willow flats. They stay there until snow reaches about 40 cm, which at 60–63° N happens in November, not October. Grizzlies feed at and above treeline on soapberry, blueberry, crowberry and Hedysarum roots, and at Blanchard on salmon reaches; from mid-October they move to steep den slopes. Black bears keep to the forest below and are mostly in their dens by early October. Willow ptarmigan hold the treeline shrub and climb onto boulder fields during the white moult (about 24 September–23 October), with rock and white-tailed ptarmigan above them and spruce grouse in the conifer below. Against that, **Highland Lake's heat is wrong where it matters most**: it scores alpine dwarf shrub under 1 m as prime moose browse, so 32% of its top-decile cells sit at 1,400–1,500 m, above a treeline of about 1,390 m, and, where the Roads and landings weight is on (a fresh phone has it off), its road-based access score tilts the map 1.9× toward lake shores. **Blanchard's moose heat falls in about the right 1,100–1,500 m band only because its alpine is mapped as barren.** Its bear layer scores grizzly country at 0.1, it has no ptarmigan, and it scores deer, which have no season there. Five changes fix most of this: split shrub by height (or by height above treeline), add a treeline-relative elevation term, cost access by the pack-out from the real transport point, use a northern burn curve that holds 1.0 from 11 to 30 years, and gate species and seasons per area. For the friend hunting Blanchard on 12–18 October, the app must also say three things: only spike-fork, tripalm or 10-point bulls are legal; grizzlies cannot be hunted in BC but will come to a kill; and mountain goat closes on 15 October. At Highland Lake it should pass on the request not to hunt Ethel Lake caribou. To work anywhere, each tile needs a profile: species gate, ecoregion, a measured local treeline, climate normals, live snow depth and satellite leaf-off dates. The rules can then key on elevation relative to treeline, snow depth and coat stage instead of Ontario calendar dates.

Evidence tags follow `docs/HUNT-FISH-SCIENCE.md`: **[S]** peer-reviewed or agency science, **[G]** agency guidance or regulation, **[H]** hunter, guide or press consensus. T means local treeline: the 98th percentile of treed-cell elevation in the baked grids, which is **1,389 m at Highland Lake and 1,436 m at Blanchard**. Δz means a cell's elevation minus T.

## Moose hold the treeline willow belt until November snow

Every northern Cordillera study points the same way. In the rut and the weeks after it, moose are at the highest point of their year, in the subalpine shrub just below treeline. GPS-collared moose on the South Canol in south-central Yukon climbed through summer and reached their **highest elevations in the rut and early winter, peaking in November**. Through the year they stayed within a 500 m band between the valley floor and the subalpine [S] ([McCulley et al. 2017](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/174)). An early-winter selection model covering 17,856 km² of the same country put the **preferred elevation at 1,411 m for single moose and 1,314 m for groups**. In the same model, alpine was strongly avoided (β = −2.388, about 0.09×) and conifer was avoided too (β = −1.121, about 0.33×) [S] ([Clarke 2017](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)). The Beaver River model, from the Mayo country and the closest analogue to Highland Lake, **peaks at 1,500 m and falls to 0.70 of peak at ±200 m, 0.45 at ±300 m and 0.11 at ±500 m**. Surveyors there saw most moose "in subalpine shrubs and higher elevation open spruce forest with good willow cover," and few in closed spruce or in lowlands of any kind [S] ([Yukon SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)). In northern BC, collared cows used **1,519 ± 88 m in winter where treeline was 1,450–1,600 m**, and alpine held under 4% of locations in every season [S] ([Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)). The Mayo early-winter census counted **331 moose per 1,000 km² in high subalpine blocks against 75 in low blocks** [S] ([Mayo 2011](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/ead07c60-96c3-3e0b-b561-ab00b9ae1e0f/download/mayoearlywintermoosesurvey_2011_2.pdf)). The Alsek survey along the Haines Road, next to Blanchard, found moose in "subalpine willow flats and creek draws," below dwarf-shrub and lichen tundra that starts at 1,350–1,400 m [S] ([Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)). Converted to Δz, the evidence centres the rut on about T−250 to T+50 m and the post-rut on about T−200 to T+100 m. At both sites that is roughly 1,140–1,490 m.

Snow, not the calendar, sends them down, and it does so late. In the Nelchina Basin (62° N), the autumn migration began "as early as November 1," and most moose did not reach winter range until mid-December or later. Moose stayed on summer range until snow reached about **40 cm** [S] ([Van Ballenberghe 1977](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/migratory_behavior_moose_southcentral_alaska.pdf); [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)). The standard later thresholds are 60 cm (impedes movement), 70 cm (changes habitat use) and 90 cm (high energy cost) [S] ([Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf); [Paragi et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)). Yukon early-winter surveys, flown from late October to mid-November, usually record 15–60 cm, still below the trigger ([SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf); [Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)). Blanchard is the site to watch. The Haines Road funnels Pacific moisture inland, and moose from the Auriol Range move into the Alsek in early winter "presumably in response to deep snow accumulations." The steep subalpine east of the Takhanne rated low for the same reason (same source). **A September–October heat map needs no descent term at either site.** Blanchard will likely descend earlier in November than Highland Lake.

What moose eat up there is tall willow. Willow was 80–85% of the Denali summer diet, with resin birch and alder only "small fractions" [S] ([Van Ballenberghe et al. 1989](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1187)), and 94% of the winter diet [S] ([Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)). In Arctic Alaska, moose spent **80–90% of their winter travel in shrubs taller than 1 m** [S] ([Tape et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4830447/)), and browse surveys count only stems 0.5–3.0 m above the ground [S] ([Julianus et al. 2019](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)). On the Liard at 60° N, February densities were **3.0 moose/km² in subalpine shrubland against 0.1 in upland conifer** [S] ([Goulet 1985](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1389)). At Beaver River, the share of tall shrub carried β = +2.055, about 7.8× selection for a block of all tall shrub [S] ([SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)). Highland Lake's "shrub" cells (median 1,491 m, 0–1 m tall) are alpine dwarf shrub. They fail the 1 m structure test, and once 30–60 cm of snow arrives they also fail the 0.5 m browse floor. **Scoring them at the Ontario 0.9 is the single largest error in either area.** The 0.1 for alpine heath and mat rests on the alpine avoidance coefficients. Dwarf birch's low value rests only on indirect diet evidence, so 0.3 for dwarf-birch shrub under 1 m is inference.

Burns stay useful longer in the north. In interior Alaska, early-winter moose were densest in **burns 11–30 years old** [S] ([Maier et al. 2005](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027971)). At Kanuti, collared moose avoided burns under 11 years, preferred stands over 30, and took the most winter browse in burns about 22 and 40 years old [S] ([Julianus et al. 2019](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)). Beaver River moose selected burns 5–35 years old (β = +1.974, about 7.2× conifer), mostly a 25-year-old burn, and few used burns 0–4 years old [S] ([SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)). The Teslin burn still held 286 moose/1,000 km² at 55 years, half its density of 560 at 24 years [S] ([Teslin 2013](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/08e7dff7-adb6-3063-96f6-2fd7fcf67b99/download/moose_early_winter_teslin_burn_2013_2.pdf)). The Ontario `disturbanceCurve` in `huntRules.ts` (0–4 yr 0.2, 5–9 yr 0.7, 10–20 yr 1.0, 21–30 yr 0.6, 31–60 yr 0.3) peaks a decade early and falls off too fast. For Highland Lake's burns it gets the order backwards:

| Highland Lake burn | Age in fall 2026 | Current score | Northern curve (fix 5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 7 yr | 0.7 | ≈0.45 |
| 2004 | 22 yr | 0.6 | 1.0 |
| 1999 | 27 yr | 0.6 | 1.0 |
| 1984 | 42 yr | 0.3 | ≈0.5 |

The other Ontario terms fit badly too. The 80 m conifer-edge rule (Hamilton et al. 1980) assumes that cover means conifer. In the mountains, moose move away from conifer in fall and early winter and rely instead on tall shrub, open sightlines and groups: on the Kenai, groups above treeline were about twice the size of those below it (12–15 against 6–7) [S] ([Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)). Lakes are rare at treeline, and Denali moose there ate "virtually no aquatic plants" (same source), so the lake-shore bonus has little support in September–October. The water feature that matters is a creek draw lined with tall willow [S] ([Mayo 2011](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/ead07c60-96c3-3e0b-b561-ab00b9ae1e0f/download/mayoearlywintermoosesurvey_2011_2.pdf); [Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)). One conflict needs resolving. South Canol's early-winter model rated valley riparian corridors low ([Clarke 2017](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)), while lowland Alaska moose stayed near rivers ([Paragi et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)). Elevation reconciles them: subalpine creek draws are good in fall, and valley river flats fill later as the snow builds. Terrain matters more than water. Beaver River moose chose slopes under 15°, with β = −0.054 per degree, which gives relative use of **0.58 at 10°, 0.44 at 15° and 0.20 at 30°** [S] ([SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)). South Canol moose that were alone preferred ground with little elevation change within 2 km ([Clarke 2017](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)). No Cordillera study has tested saddles or necks as moose funnels; the corridors in the evidence are valleys and creek draws.

Rut timing hardly moves with latitude. Denali matings ran **24 September–8 October, centred on 1 October, at the same time every year** whatever the weather [S] ([NPS, Rutting Behavior of Moose](https://home.nps.gov/articles/aps-v5-i1-c7.htm)), and over 85% of pregnancies fall within 10 days across North America [S] ([Schwartz 1992](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/alces/6027.pdf)). The Ontario peak of 23 September–8 October therefore stands. The northern shoulders are wider, though. The rut "begins in late August," and cows form stable groups by mid-September [S/G] ([NPS, Moose Rutting in Denali](https://www.nps.gov/articles/denali-moose-rut.htm)). Nelchina cows without calves were solitary 52% of the time before 19 September, 17% in the rut and 47% after it [S] ([Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)). The 14 °C heat trigger is the summer-coat threshold. In winter coat the line is about −5 °C [S] ([van Beest & Milner 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679019/)), so by October 14 °C is too high a trigger. Nothing is needed on the cold side. In September–October, treeline air runs about 6 °C cooler than valleys 1,000 m lower, and even in Old Crow's winter, cold avoidance was "not a strong driver" [S] ([Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)).

For the Blanchard trip on 12–18 October, this means the post-rut transition. Moose are still high in subalpine willow and burns. Bulls feed in secluded spots, sometimes in bachelor groups [H] ([Bailey, Outdoor Canada](https://www.outdoorcanada.ca/?p=3682)), and guides say calling response "abruptly subsides after about Oct. 11" [H] ([Wilson, Guidefitter](https://guidefitter.com/blog/a-guides-guide-to-calling-moose)). The road edge will have been hunted for seven weeks by then. On the Yukon side of the same road, the Alsek survey already put moose "at or near historic recorded lows," with harvest near or over normal limits along the Haines Road [S] ([Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)).

## Grizzlies follow berries upslope while black bears drop into forest

In fall, grizzlies in both areas eat mostly plants, and they find them in the subalpine and alpine. The Yukon government lists soapberry as the fall staple in the Kluane region and blueberry in the central Yukon [G] ([Environment Yukon, archived](https://bac-lac.wayback.archive-it.org/web/20061107092232/http://www.environmentyukon.gov.yk.ca/grizzly.html)). In the upper Alsek valley next to Blanchard, Parks Canada lists soapberry, kinnikinnick berries and Hedysarum ("bearroot") roots as the August–September foods. The roots are "most important when berries are less abundant," and salmon matters only on the lower Alsek [G] ([Parks Canada](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/yt/kluane/activ/alsek/ours-bears)). In the northern Yukon, fall foods were bog blueberry, crowberry, horsetail and bearflower, and bears dug Hedysarum when berries failed [S] ([MacHutchon & Wellwood 2003](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/grizzly-bear-food-habits-in-the-northern-yukon-canada/)). Collared bears there "preferred mountains in fall" [S] ([WMAC 2021](https://wmacns.ca/documents/401/7._Grizzly_Bear_Chapter_edited_final_version_KM_Aug_7_2021.pdf)). BC's provincial account puts it plainly: from August to October "high elevation berries become the major food source." Late fall turns to persistent berries such as kinnikinnick, and to Hedysarum roots [G/S] ([BC Accounts and Measures 2004](https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/identified/iwAMAJB01020.pdf)). The current rule scores barren and alpine at 0.1 and has no elevation term. That is the biggest bear mismatch. At Blanchard, where 62–100% of the land above 1,400 m is mapped barren, it pushes the grizzly score off the slopes the bears use. At Highland Lake the alpine dwarf shrub is coded "shrub" and scores 0.6, close to the 0.85–0.95 that berry heath deserves in early to mid-September. That part is right by accident.

Salmon is a real but fading September food at Blanchard, and absent at Highland Lake. Sonar on the Blanchard River, 175 m above the Tatshenshini, counted **1,408 large chinook in 2019, 90% of them past by 10 August**. Sockeye migrating with them were still passing when counting stopped on 21 August. On the Klukshu, sockeye passed until 10 October, and lake spawners were sampled from 20 September to 4 October [S] ([PSC/DFO 2019](https://www.psc.org/download/606/information/12602/n19-i22-alsek-river-watershed-chinook-and-sockeye-salmon-enumeration-2019-report.pdf)). Coho run into mid-October, and the Klukshu weir is pulled with fish still passing [G/H] ([Yukon News](https://www.yukon-news.com/news/dfo-opens-fishing-for-coho-salmon-on-yukon-portion-of-tatshenshini-river/)). BC Parks says grizzlies are "especially plentiful along the rivers when the salmon are running" [G] ([BC Parks](https://bcparks.ca/tatshenshini-alsek-park/)). Around Highland Lake, the Stewart system carries about 5.4% of the Yukon chinook run, and a 1952 dam cut off the Mayo River run. The nearest catalogued spawning area, Watson Creek, is about 28 km away [S] ([Brown et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/regulations/regprocess/fisheriesboard/pdfs/2022-2023/ayk/rcs/RC019_Brown_et_al_2017_ChinookSpawningAreasYukonRiver.pdf)), and Canadian chinook finish spawning by mid-September [G] ([Yukon River Panel](https://www.yukonriverpanel.com/?p=392)). The salmon weight there should be zero.

Black bears keep a different schedule: lower and earlier. In interior Alaska they moved up to alpine blueberry from mid-July and stayed until frost in mid-September. Then they "move back down into forested areas" for lingonberry and crowberry, and denned at valley-bottom elevations; the last sightings were on 2 and 4 October [S] ([Hatler 1966](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/ADFG/BlackBear/2319911-1965-66.pdf)). Where brown bears share the range, black bears avoided brown-bear areas in summer and were pushed off salmon [S] ([Belant et al. 2010](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70033910)). In Denali they are "typically confined to forested areas" [G] ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-bears.htm)). On the Tanana Flats, the mean den entry date was **1 October**, in willow/alder and black spruce [S] ([Smith et al. 1994](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/black-bear-denning-ecology-in-interior-alaska/)). The Ontario switch in `scoring.ts` (`lateFall: dayOfYear >= 288`, 15 October) sends bears to thick conifer. That direction is about right for black bears, but the switch comes about two weeks late. It also lands in the middle of the Blanchard trip.

Grizzlies keep feeding into October and then move to den terrain, not thick conifer. Yukon grizzlies den "October through April" on "south facing slopes near treeline" [G] ([Environment Yukon, archived](https://bac-lac.wayback.archive-it.org/web/20061107092232/http://www.environmentyukon.gov.yk.ca/grizzly.html)). In southwest Yukon, den sites sit on steeper slopes with more horizontal cover [S] ([Libal et al. 2012](https://bioone.org/journals/ursus/volume-23/issue-2/URSUS-D-12-00002.1/Microscale-den-site-selection-of-grizzly-bears-in-southwestern-Yukon/10.2192/URSUS-D-12-00002.1.full)). In the northern BC mountains, adult females **arrived at den areas on 14 October and entered on 23 October**, in alpine and subalpine-fir habitat at mid-to-upper elevations [S] ([Ciarniello et al. 2005](https://web.unbc.ca/~parsnip-grizzly/reports/Grizzly%20Bear%20Den%20Sites%20Ursus%2016.pdf)). At Lake Clark, females entered on 20 October and males on 28 October [S] ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70203008)). The sources disagree on aspect. BC's account says dens are "usually on steep north-facing slopes," where snow insulates them ([BC Accounts 2004](https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/identified/iwAMAJB01020.pdf)), while the Yukon sources favour south aspects for soil free of permafrost. A rule should therefore prefer south at Highland Lake, which is permafrost country, and stay neutral on aspect at Blanchard. Cold, heavy snow or a poor berry crop push den entry earlier, and bears near their dens "can react quickly if disturbed" even when they seem sluggish [G] ([fRI Research](https://friresearch.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Corrected_HWP_Grizzly_Bear_Den_Brochure_v1-1.pdf)). At both sites, the moose season overlaps with grizzlies heading for dens near treeline. Yukon's regulations note that grizzlies in high alpine areas "are probably females" [G] ([Yukon regulations 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)).

Berries also peak later after fire. In Banff, soapberry began fruiting 5 years after fire and was still productive at 23–25 years where the canopy stayed open; canopy cover explained 70% of the variation in fruit [S] ([Hamer 1996](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/644214)). Bog blueberry reaches its peak cover 50–120 years after fire [S] ([FEIS, V. uliginosum](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vaculi/all.html)). Lingonberry returns within 5–6 years but fruits poorly after severe burns [S] ([FEIS, V. vitis-idaea](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vacvit/all.html)). Alaska's best blueberry patches were "at the edge of a 20 year-old burn" [S] ([Hatler 1966](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/ADFG/BlackBear/2319911-1965-66.pdf)). The current bear rule's 3–20-year window drops Highland Lake's 1999 and 2004 burns just as they reach their best. The Ontario jack-pine bonus has no northern counterpart. The stand labels it reads are also inferred: the Highland bake calls every treed cell black-spruce-led.

## Ptarmigan stack by elevation and climb during the white moult

Willow ptarmigan is the main bird at Blanchard. The best fall study of it in western Canada was run 20 km south at Chilkat Pass, with its barometer standing at Blanchard River itself [S] ([Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)). Kluane, the nearest well-studied Yukon site, "harbors five species of grouse": spruce and ruffed grouse in forest, and willow, white-tailed and rock ptarmigan above treeline. Dusky grouse is not among them [S] ([Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)). BC atlas records place willow ptarmigan at 870–1,410 m in the Northern Boreal Mountains, mostly in the SWB and BAFA zones that make up Blanchard [S] ([BC Atlas, willow ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=WIPT&lang=en)). They put rock ptarmigan at 1,057–2,138 m ([BC Atlas, rock ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=ROPT&lang=en)) and BC's sharp-tailed grouse only around Tagish Lake ([BC Atlas, sharp-tailed grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=STGR&lang=en)). Sooty grouse belong to the coastal side [G] ([ADF&G grouse notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)). At Highland Lake, all seven Yukon species range north past 63° N [S] ([Yukon bird checklist 2017](https://webarchiveweb.wayback.bac-lac.canada.ca/web/20170426194225/http:/www.env.gov.yk.ca/publications-maps/documents/Yukon-bird-checklist-2017.pdf)), but no records from the site itself were found. Spruce grouse, willow ptarmigan and rock ptarmigan are the safe assumptions there. Ruffed and sharp-tailed grouse should follow aspen and burns. White-tailed ptarmigan should be scarce, because the alpine band is only about 250 m tall.

On a single mountain the three ptarmigan sort by height: "willow ptarmigan living closest to timberline, rock ptarmigan on middle slopes and low ridges, and white-tails high among rough rocky screes" [G] ([ADF&G ptarmigan notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)). In the Ruby Range of southern Yukon, mean nest elevations were **1,512 m for willow, 1,728 m for rock and 1,827 m for white-tailed ptarmigan** [S] ([Wilson & Martin 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-008-0308-8)). Willow ptarmigan breed in clumps of willow and birch 0.2–2.5 m tall [S] ([Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)).

The Chilkat Pass timeline is the closest thing to site-calibrated constants for Blanchard ([Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf), all figures in this paragraph). Through brood-rearing, to about 23 September, birds that left their territories "moved uphill but remained within 6 km". During the **winter moult, 24 September–23 October**, 76% of males and 59% of females moved farther. They went uphill, "well above the limit of vegetation into boulder fields" where snow patches lay, in off-territory flocks of up to 200. The first all-white birds appeared on 15 October, nine days before lasting snow. From 24 October males returned to their territories, all of them by 5 November. Birds left for sheltered willow flats and the forest edge only when snow left less than about 1 m of shrub showing: females around 20 November, males around 5 December. Wind decided where they wintered: 3.81 m/s on the breeding flat against 0.52 m/s at the Kelsall Lake wintering site. Territorial calls came at dawn and dusk, and by midday most territorial behaviour had stopped. **The friend's week falls inside the moult, when birds sit high on rock and snow patches**, the cells the current map scores at 0.1.

Spruce grouse are the conifer bird at both sites. Their highest North American densities are in pine plantations 15–25 years old in central Ontario, and forest-grouse density falls as stands age. At Kluane they wintered on white spruce needles [S] ([Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)). From late August they visit "stream sides, lakeshores and roads in early morning to secure grit," and hunters prefer clear, frosty mornings [G] ([ADF&G grouse notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)). That supports the existing roadside-grit bonus. Ruffed grouse at Kluane were "confined to patchily distributed aspen-dominated stands" and made up 9% of forest-grouse detections ([Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)), so they belong only where poplar or aspen is mapped. The Alaskan sharp-tailed grouse is a "fire grouse" of recent burns, open grass and shrub, sparse shrub-spruce at timberline and sedge muskeg, and it winters on birch buds and catkins [G] ([ADF&G grouse notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf); [ADF&G sharp-tailed grouse](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=sharptailedgrouse.main)). That makes Highland Lake's 2019 burn and its muskegs plausible habitat that the current rules score low.

## Mountain hunters glass from above and pack meat downhill

Mountain moose hunting runs on glassing. Guides glass "from a high point overlooking good habitat" and claim to "blind call" moose from two miles all the way to the glassing hill [H] ([Huntin' Fool](https://new.huntinfool.com/trip-details/alaska-yukon-moose-hunt-212)). After the rut, spot-and-stalk is "universally preferred," from a vantage point that "makes use of natural relief" [H] ([Bailey, Outdoor Canada](https://www.outdoorcanada.ca/?p=3682)). Good optics matter most "in areas where regulations dictate antler type" [H] ([Hoffman, BC Outdoors](https://bcoutdoorsmagazine.com/?p=9377)), and Blanchard is one of those areas. In October, moose in subalpine shrub were "readily observable" [S] ([Van Ballenberghe 1977](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/migratory_behavior_moose_southcentral_alaska.pdf)), so the habitat that holds moose is also habitat a hunter can see into. The seat and the moose are in different places, which argues for a separate vantage layer. Fog cuts visibility to "a couple hundred yards" [H] ([Snow, Outdoor Life](https://www.outdoorlife.com/hunting-bull-moose-in-last-frontier.md)), so the vantage layer should switch off in fog.

Calling carries "more than one-and-a-half kilometres" in calm air. Bulls work around to a downwind position, so water or open ground behind the caller forces them to show themselves [H] ([McLennan, Outdoor Canada](https://outdoorcanada.ca/?p=4852); [Wilson, Guidefitter](https://guidefitter.com/blog/a-guides-guide-to-calling-moose)). Wind ruins calling, and response falls away after about 11 October (same sources). For the friend's week, the app should weight glassing and secluded feeding cover above call sites.

Pack-out shapes where to hunt more than anything in the habitat does. A bull yields about 500 lb of boneless meat [G] ([ADF&G](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=moosehunting.main)). The Alaska rule is "don't shoot one a mile from camp." One party that broke it took four days to pack two bulls a mile uphill [H] ([Outdoor Life](https://www.outdoorlife.com/articles/steve-sorensen/2007/09/double-trouble-0/)), and a river party took eight hours to move one bull 500 yards [H] ([Huntin' Fool](https://www.huntinfool.com/articles/type/member-stories/dont-shoot)). The current access score measures distance to a road in steps: 1.0 within 2.5 km, 0.8 out to 4 km, 0.5 beyond, and 0.95 within 120 m of a lake. Highland Lake has no road within 10 km, so 97% of its land gets 0.5 and the 3% beside a lake gets 0.95. That is the 1.9× lean toward shores. The lake itself is the transport, by float plane or boat, so distance should be measured from the shore. The climb back to it should count too, since carrying meat downhill is cheap and carrying it uphill is not. Yukon also restricts off-road vehicles in alpine areas at or above 1,400 m [G] ([Yukon 2026–27 summary](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/yukon-hunting-regulations-summary/2026-27.pdf)), which rules out an ATV above Highland Lake's treeline. Groundwind's route mode already builds a walking-cost grid. The pack-out score could reuse it, with a meat-load factor on the return leg; that is a design suggestion, not a finding.

Hunting pressure pushes moose away, but only locally. Collared Alaska bulls in remote areas avoided ground dense with trails during the season, while bulls in well-trailed country tucked into cover close to the trails [S] ([Brown et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.2405)). Yakutat bulls were displaced out to about 1 km from busy routes [S] ([Shanley 2008](https://scholarworks.alaska.edu/uaf_grad_bio_sciences/440)), and Québec moose avoided roads only out to 100–250 m [S] ([Ecoscience 19(1)](https://bioone.org/journals/ecoscience/volume-19/issue-1/19-1-3461/Interactions-between-a-Large-Herbivore-and-a-Road-Network/10.2980/19-1-3461.full)). Hunters in the Haines corridor say you need to "get away from road activity by hiking into timber or taking a boat down a lake" [H] ([HuntingBC](https://www.huntingbc.ca/forum/archive/index.php/t-85839.html)). Near a highway, access and pressure pull in opposite directions, so they belong in separate factors. Hunters also plan around downslope drainage in the morning, upslope flow by day, and downslope flow again near dark [H] ([Divebomb Industries](https://www.divebombindustries.com/blogs/news/how-to-use-wind-thermals-in-mountain-hunts)). In mid-October the noon sun stands only about 21° above the horizon at Blanchard and 18° at Highland Lake, so shaded north-facing basins may never turn upslope. That comes from sun geometry, not from a hunting source.

## Regulations and safety the app must carry

Blanchard River sits in **MU 6-28**, not 6-29: a point query of BC's management-unit layer returns 6-28, Skeena region [G] ([BC WFS](https://openmaps.gov.bc.ca/geo/pub/wfs?service=WFS&version=2.0.0&request=GetFeature&typeName=WHSE_WILDLIFE_MANAGEMENT.WAA_WILDLIFE_MGMT_UNITS_SVW&outputFormat=json&CQL_FILTER=INTERSECTS%28GEOMETRY,SRID=4326;POINT%28-136.7204%2059.9673%29%29)). The site is inside the **Haines Highway Area Moose Restricted Area** (Map F39). There, any bull is legal from 23 to 31 August, and from **1 September to 31 October only a "spike-fork" bull (no more than two tines on one antler), a "tripalm" bull (at least three tines on one brow palm) or a "10-point" bull (at least ten tines on one antler, brow palm included)** is legal. The antlers must accompany the species licence, and a Mandatory Hunter Report is due by 31 March [G] ([BC Hunting & Trapping Synopsis 2026–2028](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)). The synopsis footnote points to "Map F40," which is a different map; F39 is the right one. The same synopsis states: "Be aware there is no open season on grizzly bear or white (Kermode) or blue (Glacier) colour phases of the black bear." That is the 2017 closure, still in force (same source; [CBC, Dec 2017](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/bc-grizzly-bear-announcement-18-dec-2017-1.4453696)). Tatshenshini-Alsek Park allows hunting during open seasons [G] ([BC Parks](https://bcparks.ca/tatshenshini-alsek-park/)). Inside a BC park, though, hunting or firing is banned within 400 m of either side of a park road or highway centreline. Everywhere in BC, hunting is banned within the 15 m road allowance of a two-lane highway [G] ([BC synopsis](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)).

At Highland Lake, **the Yukon government asks hunters not to take the Ethel Lake caribou herd**. The herd's range includes GMS 4-09, and the 2026–27 summary says: "This herd is open to hunting, but cannot sustain a large harvest." The Mayo District Renewable Resources Council recommends that all hunters avoid the herd until calf survival improves, and the First Nation of Na-Cho Nyäk Dun and the Selkirk First Nation are asking their citizens to do the same [G] ([Yukon 2026–27 summary](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/yukon-hunting-regulations-summary/2026-27.pdf)). Moose in 4-09 is a general season, not the Mayo threshold hunt, which covers 4-04 to 4-06. Hunting on Category A Settlement Land needs written First Nation consent (same source).

**Grizzlies at kill sites are the main safety risk on both hunts.** In Greater Yellowstone an elk kill leaves 30–40 kg of edible offal. After the September elk season opened, grizzlies were 2.3–4.4× more likely to be found outside the park boundaries. Self-defence kills by ungulate hunters were **36.5% of all human-caused grizzly deaths in 1992–2000** [S] ([van Manen et al. 2019](https://bioone.org/journals/ursus/volume-2019/issue-30e1/URSUS-D-18-00018R2/Primarily-resident-grizzly-bears-respond-to-late-season-elk-harvest/10.2192/URSUS-D-18-00018R2.pdf)). One collared Montana grizzly shadowed elk hunters "probably downwind" [H] ([Anchorage Daily News](https://www.adn.com/wildlife/article/alaska-hunters-beware-bears-could-be-following-you/2014/11/27/)). Next door to Kluane, a moose hunter was killed by a grizzly at his kill in Wrangell–St. Elias on 20 September 2020, when he went back to pack meat [H] ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1240782)). In southwest Yukon, carcasses were buried at 68% of bear kill sites and at none of the wolf sites, so a buried carcass means a bear [S] ([Gauthier & Larsen](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/predator-identification-at-moose-calf-mortality-sites-southwest-yukon.pdf)). The agency-backed kill-site card has six points. Move the meat at least 200 m from the gut pile. Hang it at least 3.5 m up and 1.5 m out from the trunk. Come back from upwind, glass the site first, and make noise. If the carcass has been moved or buried, or a bear is on it, leave and don't haze it. Never drag meat to camp. Carry spray in hand while quartering [G] ([Alberta, Bears and hunters](https://www.alberta.ca/index%2Ephp/bears-and-hunters); [IDFG](https://idfg.idaho.gov/press/hunting-bear-country)). Yukon law forbids killing a bear drawn to a kill except in self-defence. BC treats a carcass as a Wildlife Act attractant, with fines up to $50,000, except while lawfully hunting ([Yukon regulations 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf); [BC synopsis](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)). Groundwind's scent cone suggests one more feature, which is inference and untested: once a kill is pinned, draw its downwind plume as the direction a bear will come from, and the upwind side as the safe way back in. Every regulation line should carry a "verified on" date and a "check the current regulations" note, not read as legal advice.

## One profile per tile makes the rules travel

Animals choose habitat differently depending on what else is around them, so weights tuned at 48.9° N will be off elsewhere. A model fitted in one place "is unlikely to capture species responses under different conditions because organisms respond nonlinearly to changes in habitat availability" [S] ([Matthiopoulos et al. 2011](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/generalised-functional-responses-for-species-distributions/)). Models that let the weights change with what is available nearby performed best [S] ([Aarts et al. 2013](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/78763)), and roe deer used the same habitat differently in different landscapes [S] ([Gaudry et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5865119/)). The encouraging result is that Ontario moose models kept their power to rank cells at a new site. They failed only on absolute numbers, with the errors tracking how much forage was available [S] ([Kerckhoff et al., Alces](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/115)). So Groundwind should keep showing relative heat ("the best 10% here"), never densities, and should derive its weights from a per-tile profile. The profile is built in a fixed order: species gate first, then CEC Level III ecoregion, then the provincial climate zone where one exists, then the Δz band, then the vegetation class.

The treeline recipe is cheap, and the bake already holds its inputs. Within each 11 km tile plus a buffer of about 10 km, take the treed cells (NALCMS 1, 2, 5 and 6, SCANFI or VRI crown closure, or LiDAR canopy over 2–3 m). Set T at the 95th–98th percentile of their elevations, separately for north- and south-facing slopes where there are enough samples. Bound the result by the TREELIM climatic line, computed from ClimateNA monthly means: a growing season of at least 94 days with a daily mean above 0.9 °C, and a season mean of at least 6.4 °C ([Paulsen & Körner 2014](https://edoc.unibas.ch/33369)). Where forest is too sparse, fall back to the climatic line. With T in place, each species gets a preferred Δz curve by date. Moose follow fix 2 and slide down with snow (fix 15). Grizzlies centre around T in September and then go to den terrain. Black bears stay below about T−100 after frost. Willow ptarmigan sit around T, with rock ptarmigan above them and white-tailed at the top. In flat boreal tiles, where the land never comes near T, every curve flattens to 1.0 and today's Ontario rules return unchanged. The design therefore extends the current rules rather than forking them.

The climate normals turn the fixed switches into relative ones. The heat trigger follows coat stage by date and compares the live temperature with the local Tave10. The burn plateau stretches as DD5 falls: the north holds 11–30 years against Ontario's 10–20, and assuming the plateau tracks growing-season length is inference. Berry timing follows accumulated degree-days instead of dates; degree-day targets for Vaccinium, Shepherdia and Empetrum still need sourcing. Snow depth replaces the "late fall" date for moose descent and ptarmigan, and the MCD12Q2 dormancy median replaces it for cover value. Calibration can follow the pattern Groundwind's wind checks already use. Sightings and harvest pins act as a local prior per ecoregion that nudges the weights, not a full refit. No competitor publicly does anything like this. onX, Spartan Forge, HuntWise and HuntStand regionalise when deer move, or how suitable a month is for whitetail. Contors markets an assistant that "reads the terrain, the cover, the wind and the season" but publishes no method [H] ([onX](https://www.onxmaps.com/hunt/app/features/deer-movement-forecast); [HuntStand](https://huntinglife.com/huntstand-announces-powerful-new-app-tier-focused-on-whitetail-deer); [Contors](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6790434038)).

## What is still unverified

Nothing in this report comes from the two areas themselves. There are no collar data, surveys or harvest locations for MU 6-28 or GMS 4-09. The moose bands are carried over from South Canol, Beaver River, Besa-Prophet and Nelchina, and no Yukon study gives elevation week by week through 15 September–15 October. Fall moose diet in the Cordillera has not been measured, so the low value given to dwarf birch rests on indirect evidence. Every burn result comes from early winter, late winter or summer, not the rut. No study has tested saddles, aspect in the rut, distance to conifer, or kettle ponds as rut sites. For bears, several things are unknown: Yukon den elevations and entry dates, Kluane diet percentages, the black-bear displacement factor, whether sockeye and coho use the Blanchard itself, where its barriers are, and how far the salmon reaches are from the site. Which drainage Highland Lake feeds was not confirmed either. For birds, Weeden's 1960 species list for Chilkat Pass was not obtained, and there is no dusky grouse record on the Haines Road and no bird record at all from Highland Lake.

Most numbers in the fix tables are reasoned starting points that have not been field-checked or tested against harvest locations. That covers the Δz band edges, the pack-out curve (built on the practitioners' "one mile" and "two miles"), the vantage distances, the pressure ramp, the salmon weights, the grizzly and black bear class values, the ptarmigan offsets from treeline, and the autumn heat-threshold interpolation. The Highland stand labels are inferred from SCANFI. No October snow records were found for either site, so nobody knows when the 40 cm trigger usually arrives, and Blanchard's maritime snow may bring it earlier.

Several regulation details should be checked before the app relies on them. Highland Lake's placement in GMS 4-09 matches the Ethel Lake subzone list but was not checked against a GMS polygon. Nobody checked whether Tatshenshini-Alsek Park comes within 400 m of the Haines Highway near the site, or whether the Blanchard River can be boated. The Yukon game-bird closure west of the Haines Highway was read from a garbled table. BC's in-season corrections PDF would not parse. The 2026 dates for the Tatshenshini sheep draw are assumed from the 2027 listing. Data licences need checking too: NALCMS terms on the CEC page, the current BEC version, the Canadian live snow products (HRDPS, CaLDAS), and the terms for IUCN and NatureServe ranges.

## Conclusion

The Ontario rules answer one question: where do browse and cover meet near water? In the northern mountains the question that matters is vertical and structural: how far is this cell from treeline, and will its shrubs stand above the coming snow? Once that axis is in, most of the Ontario terms survive as tie-breakers within the band. The bake already holds the key input, the treed-cell elevation percentiles that put treeline at 1,389 m and 1,436 m. The same axis is the backbone of the location-aware design. In flat boreal tiles every cell sits far below treeline and the curves collapse to today's behaviour, so the change extends the rules rather than splitting them into a separate mountain branch.

Blanchard's moose map looking right for the wrong reason is the more useful warning. A heat map can pass an eyeball test because an upstream class, here barren, happens to hide a missing rule. A cheap guard would sit beside `area_checklist.py`: compare the share of top-decile cells in each Δz band with the band expected for that species and date. Highland's 32% of top cells at T+10 to T+110 would have failed that check on day one. In the mountains, the hunter's layers also part company with the animal's. Where to sit, how far and which way the meat must travel, and which bull is legal decide a hunt more than the gap between a 0.8 and a 0.9 willow cell. Those layers deserve the same care as the habitat rules.
