# Fall habitat use by moose in the northern Cordillera (NW BC, Yukon, Alaska mountains): rule constants for Groundwind

Evidence tags: [S] peer-reviewed or agency science (including Yukon/ADF&G survey reports), [G] agency guidance or regulation, [H] hunter, First Nation press or forum consensus. "Inference" bullets are my reasoning from the cited numbers, not findings. Unless stated, findings come from the northern Cordillera (BC north of 56°, Yukon, interior/southcentral Alaska); anything from elsewhere is flagged.

Study-area reference points used below:
- Highland Lake (Et'aghro), YT: 63.36° N, treeline ~1,350–1,430 m, relief 690–1,680 m.
- Blanchard River, BC: 59.97° N, treeline ~1,300–1,450 m, relief 830–2,000 m, Haines Road corridor.

## 1. Seasonal elevation use relative to treeline: rut (mid-Sept to mid-Oct) and post-rut (mid-Oct to Nov)

### Takeaway
Across Yukon, northern BC and Alaska, moose climb through summer and the rut and reach their highest elevations of the year in November (the post-rut / early-winter period), concentrating in the subalpine tall-shrub (willow/birch) band at or just below treeline. They avoid true alpine and also avoid closed lowland conifer at that time. Early-winter selection peaks at about 1,310–1,500 m in Yukon studies and at 1,519 ± 88 m (treeline 1,450–1,600 m) in northern BC. They descend from December to February as snow deepens.

### Cited Findings
- [S] **South Canol, south-central Yukon (GPS collars, McCulley, Parker & Gillingham 2017, Alces 53:137–157).** Seasons were defined as Rut = 15 Aug–31 Oct and Early Winter = 1 Nov–28 Feb. "Both sexes used lower elevations in Late Winter and Calving and highest elevations during Rut and Early Winter, peaking in November. Moose gradually descended until the end of Late Winter." Moose were "generally found within a 500-m elevational range from valley bottoms to subalpine areas." In Early Winter "moose maximized their use of Upland Shrub [cover class defined as ≥1,300 m a.s.l.] while reducing use of Conifer." Local knowledge says "moose typically remain in small groups in subalpine areas after Rut until snow depth forces them to lower elevations." Elevation use did not differ by sex or by calf status; variation among individuals was least in October–November. Males moved fastest during the rut (136 ± 9 m/h). — [McCulley et al. 2017, Alces](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/174)
- [S] **South Canol early-winter RSF from aerial stratification flights, late Oct to early Jan, 1994–2007, 17,856 km² (Clarke 2017, Yukon MR-17-03).** The most preferred elevation was **1,411 m for single moose** and **1,314 m for groups**. Single moose selected against Alpine (β = −2.388) and Conifer (β = −1.121) and preferred low variation in elevation within a 2 km window (β = −0.085). Groups selected Deciduous (β = +2.222) and Upland shrub (β = +1.225). "High alpine areas and valleys appear to be avoided"; "low-elevation conifer forest habitats tend to be avoided"; "riparian corridors represent a low suitability habitat" in early winter. The report notes that "moose tend to migrate up to higher elevations following the fall rut and remain in these regions throughout early winter (LeResche 1974, Johnston et al. 1984)." — [Clarke 2017, MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)
- [S] **Beaver River watershed, NE of Mayo, YT (includes parts of GMS 4-05 and 4-07; survey 31 Oct–8 Nov 2019).** Best RSPF at the 400 m sub-block scale (reference class = conifer): Burns 5–35 yr +1.974; Shrubland +0.592; Deciduous/Mixed +0.318 (P = 0.169); Other −0.846; Elevation (km) +26.923; Elevation² −8.976; TallShrub (proportion) +2.055; Slope (°) −0.054. In text: moose selected "mid- to upper elevations in the survey area (1300 to 1700 meters), average slopes of less than 15 degrees, and sub-blocks with a greater percentage of tall shrub cover." "Highest numbers [were] observed in subalpine shrubs and higher elevation open spruce forest with good willow cover in mountainous terrain... We saw relatively few moose in the 2015 and 2019 burns, in closed mature white and black spruce and aspen forested areas, and in lowland habitats of any kind." The model is "consistent with our observations that most moose move to higher elevation habitats with abundant willows during the early winter." — [Yukon SR-22-08, Beaver River 2019](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] **Mayo Moose Management Unit early-winter census, 2–7 Nov 2011.** "Early winter is the best time of year to estimate abundance of moose because of their concentration in high-altitude open habitats." High-density blocks were "in the mountainous area in the northeastern part of the survey area and on subalpine ridges." "Subalpine willow flats and creek draws with abundant willows generally had good numbers of moose... We saw few moose in forested lowlands and lower-elevation slopes." Observed density was 331 moose/1,000 km² in high blocks and 75/1,000 km² in low blocks. — [Yukon, Mayo early-winter 2011](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/ead07c60-96c3-3e0b-b561-ab00b9ae1e0f/download/mayoearlywintermoosesurvey_2011_2.pdf)
- [S] **Alsek (Haines Road corridor, Haines Junction to the BC border), early winter 5–13 Nov 2008.** "As expected for early winter, shrub dominated communities and subalpine willow flats and creek draws with abundant willows generally had good numbers of moose... forested lowlands and lower-elevation slopes typically had few moose in them." Vegetation grades from valley-bottom open conifer "to shrub willow or birch in the subalpine and dwarf shrub and lichen tundra at elevations 1350 to 1400 m." Kluane NP flies early-November trend surveys "in subalpine habitat where moose are known to aggregate after the rut." — [Yukon, Alsek early-winter 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)
- [S] **Besa-Prophet, northern BC (~57.5° N; 14 GPS-collared cows; Gillingham & Parker 2008, Alces 44:41–63).** Valleys lie at ~800–1,300 m, treeline at ~1,450–1,600 m and alpine above ~1,600 m. Fall was defined as 16 Aug–31 Oct and winter as 1 Nov–28 Feb. Moose used **1,519 ± 88 m in winter (Nov–Feb)**, 1,397 ± 53 m in summer and 1,333 ± 81 m at calving. They selected "Subalpine and Low shrub in fall, Subalpine in winter." Subalpine held 33–39% of locations in fall and winter, Alpine <4% and non-vegetated <1%. Moose "selected for mid-elevations in all seasons but late winter" and avoided steep slopes in calving, summer and fall. Both species "moved to lower elevations from winter to late winter." — [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)
- [S] **Nelchina Basin, southcentral Alaska (62° N; Van Ballenberghe 1977, radio-collared).** Foothills moose used habitats up to 1,400 m; tree density thins between 850 and 1,000 m, with dwarf birch/willow understories in the subalpine. Summer ranges were "normally occupied only during the period June through November." Autumn migration to winter range at **500–700 m** began "as early as November 1"; most moose "did not arrive until mid-December or later," and some not until early February. — [Van Ballenberghe 1977, ADF&G](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/migratory_behavior_moose_southcentral_alaska.pdf)
- [S] **Denali / Alaska treeline review (Van Ballenberghe 1992, Alces Suppl. 1:193–206).** "A similar peak in daily movements at Denali occurs in November as moose use forage supplies at high elevations during the post-rutting period." Near Fairbanks (Gasaway et al. 1983), cows "moved again to adjacent hills and mountains in August–October" and down to lowlands in Feb–Apr. In the Nelchina radio-collar data, moose "occupied treeline habitats during April–January." — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [G] Yukon's Science-based Guidelines: "Typically, moose in Yukon move to higher elevations in the early winter period and then descend to lower elevations in late winter (Johnston et al. 1984, Smits 1991)"; "In locations with low snowfall, moose may use the subalpine shrub zone year-round." — [Environment Yukon 2016](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/fa7d728a-213c-3cb8-bcfa-75d56e278dc8/download/moose-guidelines-2016_2.pdf)
- [S] **Contrast case: far-north Yukon (Old Crow Flats, ~68° N, 19 GPS moose; Cooley et al. 2019).** 17 of 19 moose migrated, leaving the lowland flats between 4 Aug and 30 Nov, with peak movement on 2–17 Sept (9 of 17 migrating). They wintered at high (x̄ 733 m, 588–902 m), intermediate (x̄ 436 m) or low (x̄ 347 m) elevation. At >600 m they "selected for shrubby valley bottoms near lakes and rivers within home ranges dominated by alpine tundra." In each winter range the maximum elevation used by bulls averaged 70–125 m higher than that of cows. — [Cooley et al. 2019, Alces 55](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)
- [S] Interior Alaska (Maier et al. 2005, Can. J. For. Res. 35:2233–2243): the densest early-winter (November) female moose populations were "at moderate elevations, near rivers," and in 11–30-yr burns, avoiding "variable terrain and nonvegetated areas." — [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027971)

### Inferences
- **The model needs a time-varying, treeline-relative elevation term.** The evidence supports these bands, with T = local treeline:
  - **Early–mid Sept:** broad use from upper valley forest to T. The subalpine tall-shrub band is about T−250 m to T.
  - **Rut, 15 Sept–15 Oct:** peak near T−200 m to T+50 m, in open spruce, tall shrub and subalpine willow flats.
  - **Post-rut, 15 Oct–Nov:** highest of the year, peak about T−100 m to T+100 m where tall shrub persists.
  - Valley-bottom conifer is down-weighted throughout, more so in November.
- **Beaver River RSPF elevation multiplier** (relative to its 1,500 m peak): 1,300 m or 1,700 m ≈ 0.70; 1,200 m or 1,800 m ≈ 0.45; 1,000 m or 2,000 m ≈ 0.11; 800 m ≈ 0.01. These values are computed from β = 26.923, −8.976 with elevation in km.
- **Transferring to the study areas.** Treeline there is ~1,300–1,450 m. I would centre the November peak about 0–100 m below the local mapped treeline rather than on absolute 1,500 m. The 1,300 m "upland shrub" class threshold in Yukon reports, and South Canol's preferred 1,314–1,411 m, both sit just below a ~1,350–1,450 m treeline.
- **Highland Lake (treeline 1,350–1,430 m):** November peak ≈ 1,250–1,450 m; rut band ≈ 1,100–1,450 m; terrain above ~1,500 m scores low unless mapped as tall shrub.
- **Blanchard River (treeline 1,300–1,450 m):** a similar band, but see Q2: maritime snow on the Haines Road side probably cuts the high-elevation season short.

### Gaps
- No collar study gives elevation use specifically by week through 15 Sept–15 Oct in Yukon. McCulley 2017 reports only seasonal or monthly means, as figures. I did not extract numeric monthly elevations because they are in figures, not text.
- No GPS data were found from MU 6-28/6-29 (Blanchard) or GMS 4-09 (Highland Lake) themselves.
- Miquelle, Peek & Van Ballenberghe 1992 (Wildlife Monographs 122, Denali sexual segregation by season and elevation) was not retrievable.

## 2. What triggers the descent to valley bottoms: snow depth thresholds and dates at ~60–63° N

### Takeaway
Snow, not the calendar, drives the descent, and at 60–63° N it happens from late November to February, not in October. The thresholds in use are about 40 cm (start of migration, Nelchina), 60 cm (impedes movement), 70 cm (alters habitat use) and 90 cm (high energy cost, calf survival). Snow measured on Yukon early-winter surveys (late Oct to mid Nov) is usually 15–60 cm, below the descent thresholds. Late-winter (March) depths of 53–103 cm put moose in valleys.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Nelchina: "moose appeared to occupy summer-autumn habitats until snow depths of about 40 cm triggered migration. Certain individuals, however, migrated independently of snow conditions." — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Nelchina: some moose "spent most of one winter on their summer range when snow depths were generally less than 40 cm." In heavy winters snow exceeded 70 cm. Migration ran "during a prolonged period of 10 to 12 weeks beginning in early November." — [Van Ballenberghe 1977](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/migratory_behavior_moose_southcentral_alaska.pdf)
- [S] "Snow depth >60 cm impedes movement (Renecker and Schwartz 1998)." Also: "fall migration preceded breeding and was unrelated to snow depth in Alaska (Gasaway et al. 1983)." — [Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)
- [S] Thresholds "affecting moose habitat use (70 cm) and high energy expenditure including possible reduced calf survival (90 cm) (Coady 1974)." — [Paragi et al. 2017, ADF&G WRR-2017-2 (GMU 21E)](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)
- [S] Mayo late-winter survey, March 2014: "Moose typically concentrate in river valleys in the central Yukon during winters of deep snow, moving down from their preferred early-winter subalpine habitats when snow gets too deep." Station snow depths were 53–82 cm, and in the Keno area they exceeded the level "that typically negatively affect[s] moose (above 70 cm; Peek 1997)." — [Yukon, Mayo late-winter 2014](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/92b4c443-ce74-316d-8b3f-fd110f3b4c84/download/mayolatewinterstratsurvey2014_2.pdf)
- [S] Rackla/Beaver late winter 2013: snow at 9 sites was 55–103 cm (mean 77 cm), lowest at lower elevations. Moose were concentrated in river valleys in willow, "with shallower snow depths than in the adjacent subalpine." "Some moose were found in subalpine willow habitats... where snow depths were lower." — [Yukon TR-13-16](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/9f04c437-0c1f-3a55-9717-b38a7965358a/download/racklalatewinterstrat_2013_tr-13-16_2.pdf)
- [S] Early-winter snow on surveys:
  - Beaver River, 31 Oct–8 Nov 2019: "Snow cover was complete and at depths greater than 15 cm." — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
  - Alsek, Nov 2008 (a heavier-than-recent year): "<15 cm to intermediate (30 to 60 cm) in the north half... intermediate in the south." — [Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)
- [S] Teslin burn, 24–28 Nov 2013: tracks were seen "descending from the subalpine"; moose were "likely... pushed to lower elevations due to... deepening snow." "Cow/calf groups are often pushed down into lower elevations earlier than solitary moose, due to the difficulty for the calf to travel through moderately deep snow." — [Yukon, Teslin Burn early-winter 2013](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/08e7dff7-adb6-3063-96f6-2fd7fcf67b99/download/moose_early_winter_teslin_burn_2013_2.pdf)
- [S] **Alsek / Kluane, the Blanchard neighbourhood.** "The moose population that summers in the Auriol Range migrates into the Alsek survey area in early winter, presumably in response to deep snow accumulations in the mountains." "Subalpine areas east of the Takhanne River... were primarily classified as having low expected moose abundance due to the steep terrain and generally deeper snow levels." The Yukon-Stikine Highlands are "close enough to the Pacific Ocean to funnel moderate amounts of precipitation up the Haines Road." — [Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)
- [S] Brooks Range / Kanuti, Alaska (Joly et al. 2016; winter = 16 Dec–14 May). "Moose were 56% less likely to select a location for every 100 m higher during severe winters, but only 6% less likely during mild winters." Males moved lower in winter; females stayed at similar elevations. Winter-severity classes used days of snow ≥30, 60 and 90 cm. — [Joly et al. 2016, Alces 52](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/165)
- [S] Northern BC: south-facing slopes in the Besa-Prophet "are often blown free of deep snows," and "moose and elk may not be as affected by snow depth." — [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)

### Inferences
- **For a hunting-season model (Aug–Oct, with Nov as an extension) there should be no snow-driven descent term before ~mid-November.** The upslope bias should strengthen through October into November.
- If the app ever ingests snow depth, a workable ramp is:
  - <40 cm: no penalty;
  - 40–60 cm: high terrain ×0.7;
  - 60–70 cm: ×0.4;
  - >70–90 cm: valleys and lower slopes preferred, subalpine ×0.2 except wind-scoured or south-facing tall shrub.
  - Cows with calves should shift earlier (one band sooner).
- **Blanchard River (Haines Road maritime snow)** probably has an earlier and stronger November descent than Highland Lake (continental, Mayo-type). Steep, snow-loaded subalpine east of the Takhanne was rated low in early winter even by Yukon biologists.

### Gaps
- I found no Yukon or BC collar dataset that dates the start of descent by snow depth at 60–63° N. Dates are inferred from survey timing and the Nelchina data (62° N, south side of the Alaska Range).
- No climatology of October–November subalpine snow depth for Mayo/Highland Lake or the Haines Road was retrieved. Yukon snow survey bulletins start in February.

## 3. Browse species and structure: tall willow vs dwarf birch vs ericaceous low shrub; is alpine dwarf-shrub tundra used?

### Takeaway
Northern mountain moose live on willow, especially tall willow. Resin/dwarf birch (Betula glandulosa) is a minor diet item, and ericaceous mat or dwarf-shrub alpine tundra is avoided. Usable browse lies about 0.5–3.0 m above ground, and in winter it must stand above the snow. Moose spend 80–90% of their winter travel in shrubs taller than 1 m. Alpine dwarf shrub under 1 m should not score as prime browse; it should score near barren/alpine (≈0.1).

### Cited Findings
- [S] **Denali summer diet (Van Ballenberghe, Miquelle & MacCracken 1989, Alces 25:31–35):** "willows (Salix spp.) comprised about 80–85% of the diet during June, July, and August... About 46% of the summer diet consisted of diamondleaf willow (Salix planifolia pulchra)... resin birch (Betula glandulosa), and green alder (Alnus crispa) contributed small fractions"; herbaceous plants ≈2%. — [Van Ballenberghe et al. 1989](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1187)
- [S] Denali winter diet: "Risenhoover (1987) reported 94% of the winter diet was willow; 2 willow species composed 63% of the diet." Treeline shrub communities are "1 to 2 m in height of resin birch, alder and willows." Alluvial willow stands are "dominated by tall (>1.5 m) feltleaf willow"; "Moose were most able to forage efficiently on feltleaf willow with a distribution in dense stands limited to alluvial sites... about 7% of the landscape in eastern Denali." Treeline has "large areas with a high biomass of less preferred shrubs." Tundra and alpine gross primary productivity is "about one order of magnitude less" than floodplain or valley shrub habitat (Telfer 1984). Winter forage biomass at Denali was 39–111 kg/ha in alluvial willow, 23 kg/ha in spruce, 222 kg/ha in willow riparian-lowland and 44–202 kg/ha across winter habitats. — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] **Arctic Alaska shrub height (Tape et al. 2016, PLoS ONE):** moose spend "80–90% of their tracked distance during winter in habitats with shrubs taller than 1 m." Average snow depth among riparian shrubs is ~1.1 m. Riparian shrubs grew from 1.10 ± 0.07 m (1860) to 1.97 ± 0.21 m (2009), which enabled range expansion. "Northern moose distribution is thought to be limited by forage availability above the snow in late winter." Species named: Salix alaxensis, S. richardsonii. — [Tape et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4830447/)
- [S] Browse-height window used in Alaska browse surveys: stems 0.5–3.0 m above ground. "Shorter vegetation in early seral stands (<11 years old) is often unavailable due to snow depth"; "mature birch and Bebb's willow... are often inaccessible given their height >3.0 m." — [Julianus et al. 2019, Alces 55:67–89](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)
- [S] **Liard River valley, northern BC (60° N; Goulet 1985, Alces 21:103–114, winter).** "Sixteen browse species accounted for 97 percent of all browse utilization by moose, with willows alone contributing 35 percent." "Browse utilization was greatest in sub-alpine and alluvial habitats." February 1981 densities were 3.0 moose/km² in subalpine shrubland, 1.2 in alluvial, 1.1 in burns and 0.1 in upland conifer. Pellet groups/ha: subalpine 44, burns 33, bog lowland 26, alluvial 21, upland deciduous/mixed 10, conifer 4. — [Goulet 1985](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1389)
- [S] Beaver River early winter: TallShrub proportion β = +2.055. That is ~7.8× relative selection for a sub-block that is all tall shrub vs none, from the NASA ABoVE tall-shrub class. Surveyors "did not take time to search for moose in unvegetated alpine habitats." — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] Alpine avoidance:
  - South Canol single moose, Alpine β = −2.388, ≈0.09× (Clarke 2017).
  - Besa-Prophet, Alpine (Dryas, Cassiope, sedge meadow) <4% of locations in all seasons (Gillingham & Parker 2008).
  - Yukon survey "suitable moose habitat" excludes land ≥1,524 m (Mayo 2011, Beaver 2019).
  — [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf); [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)
- [S] Old Crow isotopes: diet "shrub-dominated" year-round. The sampled shrubs were Salix pulchra, S. alaxensis, Alnus viridis and Betula glandulosa, with dwarf birch grouped with feltleaf willow/alder as one source. — [Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)
- [G] Yukon guidelines: preferred dense-shrub habitats are riparian areas, floodplains, "Subalpine climax willow communities" and early-successional burns/cuts. Winter habitats have "high shrub density (primarily willow) and low crown cover when foraging." — [Environment Yukon 2016](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/fa7d728a-213c-3cb8-bcfa-75d56e278dc8/download/moose-guidelines-2016_2.pdf)
- [S] Shade-grown willows at Denali treeline were more palatable than sun-grown (Molvar, Bowyer & Van Ballenberghe 1993, Oecologia), per an abstract summary. — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/647758ecf2e6fe92b365cc6a)

### Inferences
- Proposed cover multipliers for mountain areas in fall and early winter:
  - **subalpine tall willow (>1.5 m) and riparian/alluvial feltleaf willow flats: 1.0**;
  - willow-birch shrub 1–1.5 m: 0.7–0.8;
  - **dwarf birch–dominated shrub <1 m: 0.3**;
  - ericaceous/Dryas/Cassiope mat and alpine dwarf shrub (BAFA, most of Highland Lake's alpine above treeline): **0.1**;
  - open subalpine fir–spruce with willow understorey (SWB): 0.6–0.7;
  - closed spruce: 0.2–0.3.
- **The Ontario "shrub 0.9" class should split by height.** Without a canopy-height layer, a proxy is "shrub above T+100 m = dwarf shrub."
- Highland Lake's "alpine dwarf shrub (mostly <1 m)" fails both the 1 m structural threshold (Tape) and the 0.5 m lower browse bound once 30–60 cm of snow arrives. It is wrong to score it as prime browse.

### Gaps
- No Yukon or BC study gave fall-specific percent diet composition (Sept–Oct). The diet figures are summer and winter.
- No paper quantified the Betula glandulosa share of fall diet in the Cordillera. Evidence for dwarf birch as low value is the "small fraction" of the Denali summer diet, which is indirect.
- No data were found on whether ericaceous shrubs contribute at all in fall.

## 4. Post-fire browse curve in the northern boreal and Cordillera

### Takeaway
In Alaska and Yukon, moose use and density peak in burns about 11–30 years old (Maier et al. 2005; Joly et al. 2016). Yukon early-winter surveys select burns 5–35 years old, mostly a 25-year-old burn, and avoid burns 0–4 years old. Use of 30–45-year-old burns stays high (Kanuti: >30 yr preferred; Mayo: 1970s–2000s burns heavily used). The Ontario curve peaks too early (10–20 yr) and falls too fast (21–30 yr = 0.6).

### Cited Findings
- [S] Maier et al. 2005 (interior Alaska, early winter, female moose): densest populations "in areas where fire occurred between 11 and 30 years ago"; most relationships held "at or below 34 km²." — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027971). Restated: "in November moose preferentially use forest stands where fire occurred 11–30 years ago." — [Julianus et al. 2019](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)
- [S] **Kanuti NWR, interior Alaska (66–67° N; Julianus et al. 2019).** Sampled burns from 2005, 1990 and 1972 plus unburned stands, in 2012–13. "Summer density and biomass of preferred browse was highest at sites in the 1990 burn" (~22–23 yr old). "Radio-collared moose avoided burns <11 years old in summer and had preference for older stands (>30 years old). Winter browse offtake was highest in the 1990 and 1972 burns [~22 and ~40 yr] despite relatively high biomass available in the 2005 burn." — [Julianus et al. 2019](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)
- [S] Joly et al. 2016 (Brooks Range to Kanuti): moose "selected for habitats with high canopy cover and/or that typically have abundant forage such as 11–30 year old burned areas." Females selected burns. — [Joly et al. 2016](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/165)
- [S] Beaver River (Yukon) early winter 2019: "moose selected for shrub habitats and old burns (5 to 35 years old; moose mostly observed in a 25-year-old burn)." RSPF Burns(5–35 yr) β = +1.974, ≈7.2× vs conifer. "Relatively few moose in the 2015 and 2019 burns" (0–4 yr). — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] Mayo late winter 2014: "Areas burned in the 1970s through 2000s with abundant willows were heavily utilised," i.e. burns ~10–44 years old. Rackla 2013: "Lower-altitude areas burned in the 1970s through 1990s with abundant willows were heavily used." — [Mayo 2014](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/92b4c443-ce74-316d-8b3f-fd110f3b4c84/download/mayolatewinterstratsurvey2014_2.pdf); [TR-13-16](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/9f04c437-0c1f-3a55-9717-b38a7965358a/download/racklalatewinterstrat_2013_tr-13-16_2.pdf)
- [S] **Teslin burn, southern Yukon (stand-replacing fire 1958).** Density was 560 moose/1,000 km² in 1982 (24 yr post-fire), 416 in 1984 (26 yr) and 286 in 2013 (55 yr). The report expects a response "11 to 30 years post-fire... when regenerating willow, aspen and birch is most available (Kelsall et al. 1977, Schwartz and Franzmann 1989)." By 56 years the browse "has grown in height over time and is likely considerably less available now than 20–30 years post fire." Harvest and predation are possible confounders the report does not separate. — [Teslin Burn 2013](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/08e7dff7-adb6-3063-96f6-2fd7fcf67b99/download/moose_early_winter_teslin_burn_2013_2.pdf)
- [S] Treeline habitats "typically experience infrequent fires... habitats exploited by moose at treeline are therefore less dynamic but consistently productive." — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Northern BC: the Liard valley winter density in burns was 1.1 moose/km², with 33 pellet groups/ha (Goulet 1985). In the Besa-Prophet, Deciduous burn use was a steady 13–16% of moose locations year-round. — [Goulet 1985](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1389); [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)

### Inferences
- Proposed Cordillera burn-age multipliers (fall and early winter):
  - **0–4 yr: 0.1–0.2**;
  - **5–10 yr: 0.4–0.5** (low stems under snow; Kanuti avoided <11 yr);
  - **11–30 yr: 1.0**;
  - **31–40 yr: 0.8**;
  - **41–60 yr: 0.5**;
  - >60 yr: back to the underlying cover class.
- The plateau is ~10 years longer and the tail heavier than Ontario's. This matches slower post-fire growth at 60–63° N and the Teslin trajectory (still ~0.5× of peak density at 55 yr).
- **Highland Lake burns, scored for the 2026 season:**

  | Burn | Age (yr) | Score |
  |------|----------|-------|
  | 2019 | 7 | ≈0.45 |
  | 2004 | 22 | ≈1.0 |
  | 1999 | 27 | ≈1.0 |
  | 1984 | 42 | ≈0.5 |

  The Ontario curve currently gives 1984 = 0.3 and 1999 = 0.6, both too low.
- Burns above treeline or in alpine shrub are rare and should not get the burn bonus over the alpine cap.

### Gaps
- Maier et al. 2005 full text (effect sizes by fire-age class) was not retrieved, only the abstract.
- No Yukon or BC study tested 20–40-yr burns specifically in the rut period (Sept–Oct). All the burn results are early winter, late winter or summer.
- I did not verify burn-severity effects on browse. ADF&G project 1.74 (Hajdukovich burn) was a progress report without results.

## 5. Rut timing for Alaska-Yukon moose (A. a. gigas) and NW BC

### Takeaway
Breeding is tightly synchronized and day-length-driven: Denali matings ran 24 Sept–8 Oct, centred on 1 Oct, the same every year regardless of weather. That is essentially the Ontario window (23 Sept–8 Oct), so the peak does not need shifting. Northern studies define a long "rut season" (15/16 Aug–31 Oct), with mixed-sex groups forming by mid-September and male movement highest in this period. The pre-peak ramp should therefore start earlier (late Aug to early Sept), and post-rut groups persist into November.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Denali (12-year study, 86 observed matings): "Mating observed from September 24 to October 8"; "Peak mating for moose was centered on October 1"; "The rut occurred at the same time each year, evidently independent of differences in temperature or snowfall." Large males did 88% of mating; yearlings <2%. — [NPS, Rutting Behavior of Moose](https://home.nps.gov/articles/aps-v5-i1-c7.htm)
- [S/G] Denali: "The rut begins in late August"; "By mid-September cows form relatively stable groups that persist until the rut ends. These may contain up to twenty-five females"; "By the end of the first week in October, the rut abruptly ends." Rutting moose are found in "sparse spruce forests and more open shrub tundra." — [NPS, Moose Rutting in Denali](https://www.nps.gov/articles/denali-moose-rut.htm)
- [S] Schwartz 1992 (ADF&G Moose Research Center): "Breeding season is relatively short, with >85% of all pregnancies occurring in <10 days. Peak rut occurs in late-September and early-October. Rutting season is relatively constant across North America." Estrous cycle averages 24 days (22–28). Edwards & Ritcey (1958, BC) found "89% of all females conceived within a 10 day period." — [Schwartz 1992, Alces 28:165–173](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/alces/6027.pdf)
- [S] **Nelchina radio-collared moose (percent of observations solitary).** Females without calves were solitary 52.0% (20 Aug–19 Sept), 17.1% (20 Sept–19 Oct) and 46.8% (20 Oct–19 Nov). Males: 57.5%, 10.2% and 42.3%. Females with calves stayed 90–100% solitary even during rut. Miquelle (1990, Denali): mixed-sex groups were 74.5% of groups in the rut. Denali rutting groups reach up to 40 animals. — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] South Canol, Yukon: Rut season defined 15 Aug–31 Oct; males' highest movement rate was in rut (136 ± 9 m/h). — [McCulley et al. 2017](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/174). Besa-Prophet: fall (16 Aug–31 Oct) is "when... males and females formed mixed sex groups, and females came into estrus." — [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)
- [G] Yukon: higher-density populations use "a communal or harem breeding strategy," whereas some forest-dwelling or overharvested populations use serial "pair mating." — [Environment Yukon 2016](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/fa7d728a-213c-3cb8-bcfa-75d56e278dc8/download/moose-guidelines-2016_2.pdf)
- [S/H] Mayo hunters, 2001–2013, wrote that "The timing of the rut varies from year to year and movement rates of bulls are affected by... temperatures and early snowfalls." One log: "So far we've seen no large bulls or evidence of rutting. Seems like a late, warm year, leaves still on the trees." Of hunter observations from Aug–Oct, 25% were in August, 64% in September and 11% in October. — [Mayo Community Moose Monitoring 2001–2013](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/d6364203-5c83-3bd2-bf5e-ab5b40e39183/download/mayo_community_moose_monitoring_2.pdf)
- [G] BC MU 6-28/6-29 (Blanchard), 2026 synopsis: Haines Highway Moose Restricted Area is "Aug 23 – Aug 31 open for any bull. Sept 1 – Oct 31 open for spike-fork, tri-palm, or 10 point bull only." Bow-only Sept 1–Oct 31 carries the same antler restriction. — [BC Region 6 Synopsis 2026](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)
- [H] Alaska hunter (Chugiak, AK): "The end of Sept is pretty magic in AK, so much so most areas are closed to hunting by that time"; bulls are "harder to call in later in the rut... but they are way more active." Another poster: "cloudy 40 something degree [°F] days in early Oct. are really prime." — [Hunttalk thread](https://www.hunttalk.com/threads/moose-rut-timing.319300/latest)

### Inferences
- Keep the Ontario peak (23 Sept–8 Oct = 1.0) for the Cordillera, but adjust the shoulders:
  - ramp from ~1 Sept (0.4) to 15 Sept (0.7) to 23 Sept (1.0);
  - after 8 Oct, decay to ~0.5 by 20 Oct, with post-rut group behaviour (aggregation in subalpine) to Nov.
- For Highland Lake/Mayo, a warm September can delay visible rut behaviour by about a week (hunter logs). The underlying breeding dates are fixed.

### Gaps
- No published conception-date distribution specific to Yukon or northwestern BC moose was found. A. a. gigas dates come from Denali and the Kenai.
- No Ontario-vs-Alaska direct comparison of conception dates was retrieved. The "relatively constant across North America" statement is Schwartz 1992's.

## 6. Topography: benches, saddles, ridges, valley floors, slope, aspect

### Takeaway
In fall and early winter, mountain moose select gentle terrain at mid-to-upper elevations. Typical places are subalpine willow flats, broad creek draws, benches and rolling plateaus. They avoid steep slopes and rugged, highly variable terrain. The best quantitative slope term is Beaver River's β = −0.054 per degree, which gives relative selection ≈0.44 at 15° and ≈0.20 at 30°. I found no evidence on saddles or necks as funnels for moose.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Beaver River: selected "average slopes of less than 15 degrees" (RSPF slope β = −0.054/°, P < 0.001). Aspect (eastness, northness) models ranked well below the best model. — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] South Canol early winter: single moose "preferred habitat with little variation in elevation (i.e., flatter terrain when using a 2,000 m diameter area)." Groups selected against high TPI1000, i.e. they "prefer flat or slightly lower areas (i.e., tending toward valleys and canyon bottoms) to higher ridgetops and hilltops" at the 1 km scale, while still selecting ~1,314 m elevations. — [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)
- [S] Mayo 2011: high-density blocks were "on subalpine ridges" and in the mountainous NE. Habitat descriptions mention "subalpine willow flats and creek draws." — [Mayo 2011](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/ead07c60-96c3-3e0b-b561-ab00b9ae1e0f/download/mayoearlywintermoosesurvey_2011_2.pdf)
- [S] Alsek 2008: moose were lacking "in the steep terrain and vast snow/ice fields of the Boundary Ranges... between the Takhanne River and Kusawa Lake." — [Alsek 2008](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/83e2c3c9-9f47-324a-99dd-7a2e86b623c8/download/alsek_moose_survey_early_winter_2008_2.pdf)
- [S] Besa-Prophet: moose avoided "Non-vegetated areas and steep slopes" during calving, summer and fall, and "selected for mid-elevations in all seasons but late winter." — [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)
- [S] Maier et al. 2005: moose "avoided variable terrain and nonvegetated areas" in early winter. — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027971)
- [S] Joly et al. 2016: males selected gentler slopes in winter; females "avoided steeper slopes" in summer and selected higher solar radiation in winter. Kanuti terrain classes in the related study: flat, moderate (10–30°), steep (>30°). — [Joly et al. 2016](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/165); [Julianus et al. 2019](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/231)
- [S] Besa-Prophet: south-facing slopes "provide benefits to wintering species because they are often blown free of deep snows." — [Gillingham & Parker 2008](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/36)
- [S] Migration routes and destinations are traditional: "migratory routes, as well as destinations, were traditionally used by individuals." — [Van Ballenberghe 1977](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/migratory_behavior_moose_southcentral_alaska.pdf)

### Inferences
- Slope multiplier from Beaver River, exp(−0.054·s):

  | Slope | Multiplier |
  |-------|------------|
  | 5° | 0.76 |
  | 10° | 0.58 |
  | 15° | 0.44 |
  | 20° | 0.34 |
  | 25° | 0.26 |
  | 30° | 0.20 |
  | 35° | 0.15 |

  A simpler rule: 1.0 under 10°, 0.6 at 10–20°, 0.3 at 20–30°, 0.1 above 30°.
- **Add a terrain-roughness penalty**: SD of elevation within 2 km, or TPI at 1 km.
- **Benches and broad saddles** with willow at T−200 to T+50 m are plausible concentration sites (gentle slope plus subalpine shrub plus elevation). This is inference, and saddles are not specifically documented.
- The current "saddles/necks as funnels" rule should be low-weight for moose in this terrain. Movement corridors in the evidence are valleys and creek draws between seasonal ranges.

### Gaps
- No Cordillera study tested saddles, passes or "necks" as moose funnels.
- No study gave an aspect preference for the rut period. Aspect effects found were for winter (solar radiation, wind-scoured south slopes).

## 7. Hiding cover in open subalpine country: does the 80 m edge-to-conifer rule hold?

### Takeaway
Mostly not. In fall and early winter in the mountains, moose select away from conifer and toward open tall shrub. At treeline they rely on tall shrub, broken terrain, group living and wide spacing rather than conifer edges. Hamilton et al. 1980 (Ontario) should be replaced by a tall-shrub term. A conifer-proximity term is worth keeping only for cows with calves and for the late-winter or deep-snow period.

### Cited Findings
- [S] South Canol early winter: Conifer β = −1.121 for single moose (≈0.33×). Early winter is the only season in which Conifer was not the most-used class: "In Early Winter, moose maximized their use of Upland Shrub while reducing use of Conifer." At home-range scale "moose selected against conifer stands in favour of land-cover classes assumed to have better forage availability." — [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf); [McCulley et al. 2017](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/174)
- [S] Beaver River: reference class conifer; burns ≈7.2× and shrubland ≈1.8×; tall shrub proportion β = +2.055. "Relatively few moose... in closed mature white and black spruce." — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] Treeline: "Because moose habitat is patchy at treeline and tree density is low, moose are often readily visible to predators including wolves and brown bears." On the Kenai (Peek et al. 1974), group sizes above treeline were larger, "about twice as large... (12–15 versus 6–7)." "Open habitat enables animals to observe conspecifics from afar." Wide spacing "seems clearly related to predator avoidance." — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Willow and young deciduous growth on active floodplains "provide concealment cover and winter forage for moose" (interior Alaska lowland). — [Paragi et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)
- [G] Yukon guidelines: moose use "a mosaic of habitat types that provide abundant deciduous browse... interspersed with stands of mature conifer that provide cover from predators as well as thermal and snow interception cover." In winter they select "high shrub density... and low crown cover when foraging," and mature stands matter "for cover from predators... and for relief from deep snow conditions when traveling." — [Environment Yukon 2016](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/fa7d728a-213c-3cb8-bcfa-75d56e278dc8/download/moose-guidelines-2016_2.pdf)
- [S] Groups in early winter may be "indicative of an anti-predator strategy (Molvar and Bowyer 1994)." — [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)

### Inferences
- In mountain areas, replace the 80 m → 400 m conifer-edge decay with a **tall-shrub density term within ~200–400 m** (Beaver used 400 m sub-blocks). Optionally add a weak "within 300 m of any cover (tall shrub ≥1.5 m or open spruce)" factor (1.0 near cover, 0.7 beyond) rather than conifer-only edges.
- The open SWB subalpine fir–spruce at Blanchard counts as both browse and cover. The edge rule penalises its interior wrongly.

### Gaps
- No Cordillera study measured distance-to-conifer selection in the rut.
- No study measured concealment-cover height thresholds (e.g. ≥2 m shrub) for moose in fall.

## 8. Water and wetlands in the rut in mountain country

### Takeaway
At treeline, lakes and ponds are scarce and aquatic feeding is negligible by fall. The Ontario shore bonus (200 m lake / 100 m wetland) has little support for mountain moose in Sept–Nov. What matters is willow along creek draws, alluvial river flats and subalpine willow flats. Valley-bottom river corridors become important later, in deep-snow late winter.

### Cited Findings
- [S] "Lakes and ponds may be rare near treeline; as a result moose must forego highly digestible aquatic plants." In eastern Denali "ponds and lakes were rare... and moose consumed virtually no aquatic plants." By contrast, a coastal lowland wetland held ~2,500 kg/ha aquatic forage, about 10× adjacent shrub biomass. — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Early-winter Yukon surveys: moose were in "subalpine willow flats and creek draws with abundant willows" (Mayo 2011, Alsek 2008). Riparian corridors were "low suitability" in early winter (South Canol RSF). — [Mayo 2011](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/ead07c60-96c3-3e0b-b561-ab00b9ae1e0f/download/mayoearlywintermoosesurvey_2011_2.pdf); [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)
- [S] Interior Alaska lowlands (Innoko/Yukon rivers): moose within 0.8 km of rivers were 50% of locations in Sept, 52% in Oct and 45% in Nov. This is a lowland river system, not mountains. — [Paragi et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)
- [S] Old Crow (68° N): summer home ranges sit in drained lake basins and lakeshores. Winter moose at >600 m selected "shrubby valley bottoms near lakes and rivers." — [Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)
- [S] Maier et al. 2005: early-winter density higher "near rivers." Joly et al. 2016: moose moved closer to rivers in moderate and severe winters, and cows with calves avoided riparian habitat. — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027971); [Joly et al. 2016](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/165)
- [S] Denali rutting habitat is "sparse spruce forests and more open shrub tundra," with no mention of water. — [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/denali-moose-rut.htm)
- [H] "Stay high for glassing and call the creeks and boggy areas" — this poster is from North Idaho (Shiras moose), not northern. — [Hunttalk](https://www.hunttalk.com/threads/moose-rut-timing.319300/latest)

### Inferences
- **For mountain areas in fall:** drop the lake-shore bonus to a small value (×1.1 within 200 m) or zero. Keep a **creek-draw / alluvial willow bonus** (×1.2–1.3 within ~100 m of streams *where tall willow is mapped*, at any elevation up to T+50 m).
- Treat Blanchard's river willow flats as prime, but expect them to fill mainly from mid-November onward as snow builds.
- Kettle and pond wetlands in subalpine benches may serve as rut gathering spots. This is a hunter belief I could not source for the Cordillera.

### Gaps
- No Cordillera study on wallows, ponds or kettle wetlands as rut sites.
- No numeric selection for distance-to-water in the rut in mountain terrain.

## 9. Predators (wolves, grizzlies) and selection of open high ground by cows and calves

### Takeaway
Yukon and Alaska mountain moose live under intact wolf and grizzly predation, with grizzly densities highest near treeline. Bulls and lone cows concentrate in the best shrub/burn/elevation habitat in the rut and early winter. Cows with calves stay solitary, spaced out and nearer forest or mixed cover. In early winter they spread away from the prime high shrub blocks and are pushed down earlier by snow. Calving (May–June) is the season when some moose climb to escape bears; in fall the evidence is that cow-calf pairs do not seek open high ground.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Treeline predator density: brown bears at 32/1,000 km² in Denali (Dean 1987) and 15/1,000 km² in the northern Alaska Range foothills. Biologists saw "only 1 brown bear in 10 years of surveys in adjacent lowlands" (Gasaway et al. 1983). Wolves at treeline have alternate prey (caribou, Dall's sheep). Calf mortality "typically results in 80–90% of the females being without calves during rut" at Denali. — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Females with calves at Denali were "solitary 98.8% of the time except during the breeding season." Nelchina cows with calves were 90–100% solitary in every period including the rut. — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S] Beaver River early winter: "a significantly greater proportion of adult bulls and lone adult cows in survey blocks with more of the most favoured land cover types (shrub habitats and burns) and topography. In contrast, cows with calves tended to space themselves more away from these habitats." — [SR-22-08](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/7833119c-e384-3765-9302-883d9323e5b7/download/sr-22-08-moose-survey-beaver-river-2019.pdf)
- [S] South Canol early winter: the cow & calf model selected deciduous, mixedwood, upland and lowland shrub. "High alpine areas, valleys, and riparian corridors have low suitability." — [MR-17-03](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/e35aea61-fcce-32d6-8c40-9e070bf40802/download/mr-17-03_2.pdf)

- [G] Yukon moose densities generally run 100–250 per 1,000 km² of suitable habitat because of "three relatively intact predator populations (grizzly bears, black bears and wolves)." "Bear predation is most common in the first 2 months of a calf's life, while wolf predation is considered to have a greater influence in the remainder." — [Environment Yukon 2016](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/fa7d728a-213c-3cb8-bcfa-75d56e278dc8/download/moose-guidelines-2016_2.pdf)
- [S/H] Mayo hunter logs record predators at moose in fall: "Wolf pack was around the lake most of September. Moose were scarce"; "Grizzly bear just killed this moose." — [Mayo Community Moose Monitoring](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/d6364203-5c83-3bd2-bf5e-ab5b40e39183/download/mayo_community_moose_monitoring_2.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a hunter (bulls), the prime subalpine shrub/burn/gentle-terrain cells are right. Cow-calf distribution is flatter: more mixedwood/deciduous, fewer alpine-edge cells.
- If the app models "cow-calf" separately, damp the elevation peak (×0.8 at the top band) and add +0.2 for mixed or deciduous forest.

### Gaps
- No fall-specific (Sept–Oct) predation-risk selection coefficients exist for Yukon or NW BC moose.
- The "open high ground by cows and calves" behaviour is documented for calving (May–June), not for fall.

## 11. Temperature: is the 14 °C heat-stress rule sensible here, and is there a cold-side change?

### Takeaway
The 14 °C figure is the summer-coat threshold (Renecker & Hudson 1986). In winter coat the heat-stress threshold is about −5 °C. By the late-September rut and into October, moose are moving toward the winter coat, so 14 °C is too high a trigger for October. On the cold side, deep cold is not a strong driver: moose at high elevation actually sit in warmer inversion air in mid-winter. In fall, standard lapse rates make treeline about 6 °C cooler than valley floors.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Temperature classes used for moose from Renecker & Hudson (1986):
  - low: <−5 °C in winter, <14 °C in summer;
  - moderate: −5 to 0 °C in winter, 14 to 20 °C in summer;
  - high: ≥0 °C in winter, ≥20 °C in summer.
  Southern Norway; categories from the Alberta captive work. — [van Beest & Milner 2013, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679019/)
- [S] "Respiration rate started to increase at 14°C and at 20°C they began open-mouthed panting" (captive moose, Renecker & Hudson), cited in a Swedish study at 63.6° N. — [Græsli et al. 2020, Front. Ecol. Evol.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2020.00107/full)
- [S] Interior Alaska, 15 Aug–15 Oct (rut). Male movement rate vs air temperature (−7 to 24 °C) was significant in 36 of 67 male-years (5 positive, 31 negative), "but the correlations were variable and overall not strongly negative (x̄ r = −0.093)." — [Paragi et al. 2017](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/1.69_wildlife_research_report_2016.pdf)
- [S] Old Crow (68° N): "standard lapse rates in autumn when Ta in September–October was approximately 6°C cooler at 1250 m than 250 m," but in Dec–Feb Ta "averaged 9°C warmer at 1250 m than at 250 m." When air was −30 to −40 °C, high-elevation moose were 3.5 °C warmer on average. Collar temperatures below −40 °C were rare above 800 m (8% of records) and common below 600 m (62%). However, "cold avoidance was not a strong driver of habitat selection." — [Cooley et al. 2019](https://alcesjournal.scholasticahq.com/article/157128-the-seasonality-of-a-migratory-moose-population-in-northern-yukon.pdf)
- [S] Denali treeline: "first frosts in fall are commonly 10–14 days earlier" than 500 m lower; bud-break is two weeks later. Large males lose "about 15–20% of their body weight during rut." "No evidence existed that males selected habitats that provided thermal cover" in winter (Miquelle 1990). — [Van Ballenberghe 1992](https://www.alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/1809)
- [S/H] Mayo hunters: "The moose seemed to be a lot more active in early September, then there were not many sightings during the middle two weeks... Unseasonal warm temperatures especially during the evenings may have contributed." — [Mayo Community Moose Monitoring](https://open.yukon.ca/information/2671194b-faf9-458c-8a9d-f24de5e76a0b/resource/d6364203-5c83-3bd2-bf5e-ab5b40e39183/download/mayo_community_moose_monitoring_2.pdf)
- [H] Alaska hunter: "cloudy 40 something degree [°F, ≈4–9 °C] days in early Oct. are really prime." — [Hunttalk](https://www.hunttalk.com/threads/moose-rut-timing.319300/latest)
- [S] Conifer cover moderates "extremes of heat, cold, wind, and deep snow" (general statement). — [McCulley et al. 2017](https://alcesjournal.org/index.php/alces/article/view/174)

### Inferences
- Make the shade/cover shift date-dependent:
  - Aug to mid-Sept: trigger at **14 °C** (full shift by 20 °C);
  - mid-Sept to mid-Oct: trigger near **8–10 °C**;
  - from late Oct into Nov (winter coat): **~0 °C**, i.e. warm or sunny days push moose to shade, north aspects or higher, cooler terrain.
  This is interpolation between the summer and winter thresholds; no fall measurement exists.
- On warm September days, cooler treeline air (~6 °C below valley at a 1,000 m difference) favours higher, north-facing tall shrub.
- No cold-side penalty is needed for Sept–Nov. Deep cold does not push moose off high ground, and inversions favour it in mid-winter.
- A wind-exposure penalty on ridge crests is plausible, but I found no moose-specific evidence.

### Gaps
- No study measured the transition date or temperature between the summer (14 °C) and winter (−5 °C) heat-stress thresholds in autumn.
- No Cordillera study quantified wind-exposure avoidance.
- Renecker & Hudson 1986 primary text was not retrieved; the thresholds are as restated by van Beest & Milner 2013 and Græsli et al. 2020.

### Cross-cutting: where the Ontario rules are wrong for Blanchard River and Highland Lake (inferences from the findings above)
1. **No elevation term.** Add a treeline-relative band, rising through the rut to a November peak at about treeline −100 m to treeline. Base it on Beaver River (peak ~1,500 m, 0.7 at ±200 m), South Canol (1,314–1,411 m) and Besa-Prophet (1,519 ± 88 m vs treeline 1,450–1,600 m).
2. **Alpine dwarf shrub scored as "shrub 0.9".** Cap it at ~0.1 (alpine β = −2.388; <4% of locations; <1 m shrubs fail the 1 m and above-snow tests). Reserve 0.9–1.0 for tall willow (>1–1.5 m) in subalpine flats, creek draws and alluvial bars.
3. **Burn curve peaks too early and decays too fast.** Use 11–30 yr = 1.0 and 31–40 yr ≈ 0.8; drop 0–4 yr to ~0.1–0.2.
4. **Conifer-edge (Hamilton 1980) rule.** Replace it with tall-shrub density and terrain; conifer is selected against in early winter (β = −1.121).
5. **Shore bonus.** Remove or shrink it for lakes. Keep a willow-riparian/creek-draw bonus.
6. **Slope.** Add exp(−0.054·slope) and a roughness penalty.
7. **Saddle/neck funnels.** Unsupported for moose here; lower their weight.
8. **14 °C shade rule.** Make it season-dependent (14 → ~0 °C from Sept to Nov).
9. **Rut curve.** The peak (23 Sept–8 Oct) is fine; widen the shoulders (start ramp ~1 Sept, post-rut subalpine aggregation into Nov).
10. **Snow term, if added.** Use the 40/60/70/90 cm steps; not needed before mid-November.
