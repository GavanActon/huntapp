# Upland birds of the northern Cordillera: fall (Sept–Oct) grouse and ptarmigan habitat at Blanchard River (BC) and Highland Lake (YT)

Evidence tags: [S] science (peer-reviewed or atlas data), [G] agency guidance or regulation, [H] hunter consensus or popular press.
Site anchors used below: Blanchard River 59.97 N, 136.72 W (BC MU 6-28/6-29; relief 830–2000 m; treeline about 1300–1450 m). Highland Lake 63.36 N, 134.76 W (near Mayo; relief 690–1680 m; treeline about 1350–1430 m; burns 1984, 1999, 2004, 2019, which are 42, 27, 22 and 7 years old in fall 2026).

Key local fact: the best willow ptarmigan fall and winter study in western Canada (Gruys 1993, Chilkat Pass) was run about 20 km south of Blanchard River. Its barometer stood "at Blanchard River, 20 km north of the study area". Its numbers are the closest thing to site-calibrated constants that exist for Blanchard.

## Q0. Which grouse and ptarmigan species occur at each site?

### Takeaway
Blanchard River: willow ptarmigan is certain and is the dominant fall bird. Spruce grouse is very likely in the open white spruce. Rock and white-tailed ptarmigan are likely on the alpine above about 1400 m. Ruffed grouse is possible only in poplar/aspen patches. Dusky grouse is unconfirmed; sooty grouse belongs to the coastal side of the pass. Sharp-tailed grouse has no BC season, and the nearest BC population is around Tagish Lake. Highland Lake: all seven Yukon species range north past 63° N, but I found no site-level records. Assume spruce grouse, willow ptarmigan and rock ptarmigan. Ruffed and sharp-tailed grouse depend on aspen and on burns. White-tailed ptarmigan and dusky grouse are uncertain.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Chilkat Pass habitat. North of the pass the land descends into "an open canopy white spruce (Picea glauca) forest, interspersed with willow and birch". On the Pacific side, the coastal forest has "a dense cover of willow and alder at higher elevations" (Weeden 1959). Ground usually snow-covered October–May, with snow depth about 1–1.5 m in most winters. Willow ptarmigan (L. l. alexandrae) have been studied there since 1979 on a 4.5 km² site; Weeden (1959) and Mossop (1988) studied nearby sites. — [Gruys 1993, Arctic 46:228–239](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Kluane (SW Yukon, Shakwak Valley, about 200 km from Chilkat Pass) "harbors five species of grouse": spruce grouse and ruffed grouse in forest year-round, and willow, white-tailed and rock ptarmigan "above treeline in the Shakwak Valley most of the year". Willow ptarmigan descend into the open forest from October to March. Dusky grouse is not among the five. — [Martin, Doyle, Hannon & Mueller 2001, Kluane book ch. 11](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Yukon Bird Club checklist (May 2017). Ruffed Grouse, Spruce Grouse, Dusky Grouse, Sharp-tailed Grouse, Willow Ptarmigan, Rock Ptarmigan and White-tailed Ptarmigan are each coded "* W" (confirmed breeding; observed annually in winter). None carries the "S" code (S = "south of 63° N (includes Pelly Crossing but not Stewart Crossing)"), which marks species limited to the south. — [Checklist of Yukon Birds 2017](https://webarchiveweb.wayback.bac-lac.canada.ca/web/20170426194225/http:/www.env.gov.yk.ca/publications-maps/documents/Yukon-bird-checklist-2017.pdf)
- [G] White-tailed ptarmigan range "from the Alaska Range and central Yukon southward to Washington and northern New Mexico". Rock ptarmigan occur "in all major treeless areas" of Alaska. — [ADF&G Ptarmigan Wildlife Notebook (Weeden, rev. Paragi 2008)](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] BC willow ptarmigan were found at 870–1,410 m in the Northern Boreal Mountains Ecoprovince. Most Atlas records are in the Spruce-Willow-Birch and Boreal Altai Fescue Alpine BEC zones, which are Blanchard's two zones. — [Martell 2015, BC Breeding Bird Atlas: Willow Ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=WIPT&lang=en)
- [S] BC rock ptarmigan: "locally rare to uncommon"; "Most breeding is from about 1,000–2,000 m"; Atlas elevation range 1,057–2,138 m. The highest likelihood of finding it is on the Stikine and Yukon plateaus and in the Cassiar and northernmost Rocky mountains. — [Martell 2015, BC Atlas: Rock Ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=ROPT&lang=en)
- [S] BC white-tailed ptarmigan: "the widest distribution of all ptarmigan in British Columbia". The Northern Boreal Mountains is one of three ecoprovinces with extensive areas of higher probability of observation. Breeding is at 1,830–2,650 m in the interior (Campbell et al. 1990; mostly southern data). — [BC Atlas: White-tailed Ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=WTPT&lang=en)
- [S] BC spruce grouse: highest probability of observation in the Northern Boreal and Sub-Boreal Interior ecoprovinces, "particularly east of Atlin Lake". Known up to 2,500 m. The northern subspecies is F. c. canadensis. — [Chutter 2015, BC Atlas: Spruce Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=SPGR&lang=en)
- [S] BC ruffed grouse: widespread. "The Atlas surveys have expanded confirmed breeding in the northwest of the province"; important areas include the Stikine drainage and the Liard Plateau. Highest abundance is at 750–1,250 m (records 5–1,697 m). — [Chutter 2015, BC Atlas: Ruffed Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=RUGR&lang=en)
- [S] BC sharp-tailed grouse: the Alaskan subspecies (T. p. caurus) lives "in the northwest corner of the Northern Boreal Mountains Ecoprovince around Tagish Lake". The Atlas found breeding evidence "in all of these areas except the Northern Boreal Mountains and Southern Interior Mountains ecoprovinces". — [Chutter 2015, BC Atlas: Sharp-tailed Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=STGR&lang=en)
- [S] BC dusky grouse: "more sparsely distributed farther north". Its western limit "narrowly overlaps with that of Sooty Grouse along the eastern downslopes of the southern Coast Mountains and along upper reaches of large rivers like the Skeena". — [Starzomski 2015, BC Atlas: Dusky Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=DUGR&lang=en)
- [G] Sooty ("blue") grouse in Alaska are "restricted ... to the southeastern part of the state ... from Glacier Bay southward". They live in dense coastal Sitka spruce–hemlock forest but are "often found near timberline among dwarfed alpine firs". — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook (Ellison, rev. Paragi 2008)](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [G] Ruffed grouse in Alaska occur along the Yukon, Porcupine, Tanana, Kuskokwim and Upper Copper rivers and "in the Taku and Stikine river drainages in southeastern Alaska". Sharp-tailed grouse occur "in the Yukon River Valley from Canada to Holy Cross". — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [S] The Alaskan sharp-tailed grouse ranges from "north-central Alaska east to the southern Yukon territory, northern British Columbia, and northern Alberta" (Johnsgard 1983). — [USFS FEIS: Sharp-tailed grouse](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/animals/bird/typh/all.html)
- [S, secondary] Yukon sharp-tailed grouse: "scattered populations in southern and central Yukon, north to lower Peel River basin and Old Crow" (summary of Yukon bird literature, read through a search summary). — [iNaturalist taxon guide: Sharp-tailed Grouse](https://www.inaturalist.org/guide_taxa/25891)
- [H] A Yukon naturalist photographed willow ptarmigan on 17 May 2015 "near Kelsall Lake, north of Haines Summit on the Haines Road". Kelsall Lake is one of Gruys's wintering sites. — [Sue Thomas, Yukon Sights, Haines Rd 2015-05-17](https://www.suethomas.ca/20150517_HainesRd.html)

### Inferences
- Blanchard River sits in the SWB/BAFA zones, 20 km down the Haines Road from Chilkat Pass. Willow ptarmigan should be the default ptarmigan for every shrub cell from about 830 m to about 1450 m.
- Kluane, the nearest intensively studied Yukon site, lists no dusky grouse. Combined with the sparse northern BC Atlas data, the Blanchard default should be "dusky absent or rare" until a record turns up. Sooty grouse belongs on the Haines (coastal) side of the pass, not at Blanchard.
- Highland Lake is about 40 km north of 63° N. Species without an "S" code (all seven) are not limited to south of 63° N, but that does not prove presence at the lake. Weight ruffed and sharp-tailed grouse by habitat (aspen; burns and shrub openings) rather than assuming them.
- Highland Lake's alpine band (about 1430–1680 m) is only about 250 m high. White-tailed ptarmigan needs steep rock near late snow (Q1), so it is likely scarce there. Rock ptarmigan is the more probable second ptarmigan. Blanchard's alpine reaches 2000 m and has room for all three.

### Gaps
- Weeden's "The Birds of Chilkat Pass, British Columbia" (Canadian Field-Naturalist, 1960) would settle the Blanchard species list, including dusky grouse and the ptarmigan mix. I did not obtain it.
- eBird and Birds of the World range pages could not be read (JavaScript or paywall), so I have no point records for Highland Lake or the Mayo area for ruffed, sharp-tailed or dusky grouse, or for white-tailed ptarmigan.
- Sinclair et al. (2003), Birds of the Yukon Territory, is the authoritative Yukon range source and was not accessible.

## Q1. Willow, rock and white-tailed ptarmigan: fall habitat, elevation vs treeline, flocking, movement and time of day

### Takeaway
On one mountain the three species stack by elevation: willow ptarmigan closest to timberline in willow/scrub birch 0.2–2.5 m tall, rock ptarmigan on middle slopes and dwarf-shrub/sedge meadows, white-tailed ptarmigan highest on steep rock near snow. At Chilkat Pass, willow ptarmigan stayed within about 6 km of their territories through September. They then moved uphill onto boulder fields and snow patches during the white moult (24 Sept–23 Oct) in flocks of up to 200. Males, and about half the females, returned to their territories for an autumn territorial period (late Oct–early Dec). The birds left for sheltered subalpine willow flats and the forest edge only when snow buried the shrubs to below about 1 m: females about 20 Nov, males about 5 Dec.

### Cited Findings
**Altitudinal stacking**
- [G] "willow ptarmigan living closest to timberline, rock ptarmigan on middle slopes and low ridges, and white-tails high among rough rocky screes and boulder-strewn ridges close to glaciers or snowfields". — [ADF&G Ptarmigan Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Southwestern Yukon ptarmigan "are sympatric in a regional sense, but are stratified altitudinally" (Weeden 1963, 1964). Breeding willow ptarmigan use subalpine "clumps of Salix and Betula (0.2–2.5 m high) interspersed with sedge and grass hummocks or deciduous shrubs". — [Martin et al. 2001, Kluane ch. 11](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Ruby Range, southern Yukon (sympatric breeding of all three species). Mean elevation around nests: white-tailed 1,827 m, rock 1,728 m, willow 1,512 m. White-tailed selected steep, rocky slopes at higher elevations, and drier lichen/rock/dwarf-shrub habitat. Rock ptarmigan used lower sedge/graminoid meadows with scattered woody shrubs. Willow ptarmigan nested in dense woody shrubs. (Numbers are from a search summary of the abstract; the full text was not read.) — [Wilson & Martin 2008, J. Ornithol. 149:629–637](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10336-008-0308-8)
- [S] Where rock and white-tailed ptarmigan co-exist, "the former selects lower alpine meadows and the latter favours steep, rocky slopes" (Martin & Wiebe 2004). — [BC Atlas: Rock Ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=ROPT&lang=en)
- [S] Willow ptarmigan in BC prefer subalpine and alpine habitats "normally just above treeline, especially low, moist areas and shrubby habitats where willow or birch shrubs are abundant; it avoids dry, rocky areas". — [BC Atlas: Willow Ptarmigan](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=WIPT&lang=en)
- [G] Rock ptarmigan prefer "high valleys or slopes with low shrubs, herbs and grasses". Willow ptarmigan favour "subalpine areas" and willow-lined waterways. White-tailed breeding habitat is "boulder fields, snowfields, glaciers, cliffs, and rockslides at higher altitudes than other ptarmigan". — [ADF&G Alaska's Grouse & Ptarmigan card (2012)](https://newsrelease.adfg.alaska.gov/static/hunting/smallgamehunting/pdfs/alaska_grouse_ptarmigan.pdf)

**Willow ptarmigan fall sequence at Chilkat Pass (about 20 km south of Blanchard River)**
- [S] Brood rearing (1 Aug to about 23 Sept): birds that left territories "moved uphill but remained within 6 km of their territories". Those leaving were mainly from territories with less cover and lower vegetation. Fewer than 30% remained on territory by the end of brood rearing in 1988. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Winter moult (24 Sept–23 Oct): from mid-September 1988, 76% of 17 males and 59% of 17 females moved farther from their territories. 35% of males and 44% of females went to nearby side valleys. "Movements were uphill from the brood rearing areas, and many birds moved well above the limit of vegetation into boulder fields", where snow patches were already present. Both sexes joined flocks; Table 4 shows off-territory flocks of up to 200 birds. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Moult timing: the first all-white birds were seen on 15 Oct 1988, "nine days before permanent snow cover". Temperatures were about +5 °C at the onset of moult and −5 °C at its end. Gruys argues daylength is the more reliable cue. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Autumn territoriality (24 Oct–10 Dec): 55% of 20 radio-tagged males and 16% of 19 females were back on their spring territories by 26 Oct, and all males by 5 Nov. 53% of females returned at some point, but no more than 21% were on territory at once. All females with known locations stayed within 11 km of their territories. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Departure to wintering grounds: females averaged 20 Nov (SD 10.6 d), males 5 Dec (SD 7.0 d); all had left by 14 Dec 1988. In 1988–89, 20% of males and 67% of 10 females moved past the boreal forest edge, about 20 km N/NE. The longest movement was 90 km, by a male. Spring return: males 21 Mar, females 6 Apr. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Shrub height rule: "Most ptarmigan stayed in vegetation of, on average, 1 m or taller throughout autumn and early winter". They "may have left their territories because height of vegetation decreased below 1 m as snow depth increased". Vegetation height within 10 m of radio-tagged birds ran about 1.0–2.3 m (Table 8, partly garbled in the scan). — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Cover numbers (Table 2): September cover on the windy breeding flat (Haines Rd) was 23% vs 60–66% at the wintering and forest-edge sites. December cover above snow was 1.4% vs 7.7–15.6%. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Mossop (1988) found that "in years with little snow most female willow ptarmigan at Chilkat Pass remained near the breeding grounds". — cited in [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] In Kluane winters, willow ptarmigan descend into "open forest with more cover than the subalpine but still with exposed forage" (Weeden 1964, Gruys 1993, Mossop 1994). Ptarmigan "were present in the valley only in winter". — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)

**General ptarmigan fall pattern**
- [G] "Autumn is a time of restlessness. Flocks form and disperse and form again ... In October ... females tend to form their own flocks and drift lower down into brushy forest openings while cocks stay close to timberline." The longest movements in Alaska are 160–240 km one way. Birds are "nomadic in winter, moving erratically from one sheltered slope or patch of food to another from November to March". — [ADF&G Ptarmigan Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)
- [G] Diet: "By mid-October most ptarmigan (except in coastal areas of Southcentral Alaska) are back to their winter menu". Once snow covers the ground, willow ptarmigan eat willow buds and twigs and a little birch; rock ptarmigan eat birch catkins and buds and a little willow; white-tails mix willow, birch and alder. — [ADF&G Ptarmigan Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)
- [G] Rock ptarmigan in September gather in groups "numbering from 20 to over 250 birds", then "flocks of mostly females move to their low-elevation wintering areas". Diet is mostly dwarf birch buds and catkins October–March, and berries and seeds from mid-August. — [ADF&G Rock Ptarmigan species page](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=rockptarmigan.main)
- [G] Rock ptarmigan "turn white in early October and remain white until early May". In winter "males move to the lower edge of their breeding range; hens move to hills around large valleys in shrubby, open habitat". Willow ptarmigan in winter "remain close to shrubby slopes and valleys at lower elevations than their breeding habitat". White-tailed ptarmigan "usually move lower in late fall, wintering on slopes or in high valleys with alder, willow, birch, and some spruce". — [ADF&G Alaska's Grouse & Ptarmigan card](https://newsrelease.adfg.alaska.gov/static/hunting/smallgamehunting/pdfs/alaska_grouse_ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] White-tailed ptarmigan "remain in the alpine most of the year and descend in winter to willow-dominated basins with considerable snow accumulation" (Braun et al. 1993). — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S, secondary] White-tailed ptarmigan: most move to slightly lower elevation in winter, some as far as 14 miles (about 23 km) from summer to winter range; females move farther than males. — [Center for Biological Diversity natural history page](https://biologicaldiversity.org/species/birds/white-tailed_ptarmigan/natural_history.html)
- [S] White-tailed winter habitat (Colorado; read only through a search summary, and the authors of the linked paper are not confirmed): willow-dominated basins or riparian areas "at or below treeline where snow is available for roosting". Willow canopy cover was higher at feeding sites than at random sites, except in March–April at peak snow. Hen winter home ranges averaged 1.62 ± 0.72 km² (n = 17) vs males 0.44 ± 0.20 km² (n = 2). — [Wilson Bulletin 104(2):263–272](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/wilson/v104n02/p0263-p0272.pdf)
- [S, summary] "In autumn, Willow Ptarmigan in western North America move upslope ... where they molt into their white winter plumage. These areas are drier and stonier than breeding habitats, to which they return after completing molt." — [All About Birds: Willow Ptarmigan life history](https://allaboutbirds.org/guide/willow_ptarmigan/lifehistory)

**Time of day**
- [S] At Chilkat Pass in autumn, territorial calls and lone birds on territory were noted "especially in the mornings". Male territorial calls were "heard at dawn and dusk". By midday "most territorial behaviour had ceased" and males tolerated other birds. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Midwinter willow ptarmigan in Alaska: crop contents rose from small amounts in the morning to "as much as 15 percent of a bird's weight toward evening" (read only through a search summary; the full text was not read). — [Irving, West & Peyton 1967, Condor 69:69–77](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v069n01/p0069-p0077.pdf)
- [H] Ptarmigan often "flush long before an approaching hunter can close to within gunning range". Groups "feeding or loafing in brushy cover during a sunny midday" can be reluctant to flush. — [Outdoor Canada, Skok, ptarmigan in winter](https://www.outdoorcanada.ca/hunting-ptarmigan-in-winter-the-5-things-you-need-to-know/6)

### Inferences
- Willow ptarmigan fall habitat (Blanchard: about 830–1450 m, the SWB willow/scrub-birch band; Highland Lake: the shrub band around 1350–1430 m treeline). A rule could score shrub (willow/birch) 0.2–2.5 m tall from about treeline −150 m to treeline +250 m. Taller shrub (≥1 m) should score highest, because Gruys's birds stayed in vegetation ≥1 m all autumn. The −150/+250 m band is inferred from Atlas elevations and Kluane's "open forest" use, not measured.
- A date-dependent shift fits the Chilkat timeline:
  - Aug 15–about Sept 20 (brood flocks): shrub band; birds from low-cover territories drift uphill.
  - About Sept 20–Oct 25 (moult): boost the upper shrub edge, boulder fields and snow-patch cells up to about 6 km and a few hundred metres above the willow band. Expect flocks of 20–200.
  - Late Oct–Nov after the first lasting snow: back to the shrub band (males especially). Weight toward cells where shrub height exceeds snow depth by about 1 m or more.
  - Once snow buries the shrubs (Chilkat: Nov 20 for females, Dec 5 for males): wind-sheltered willow flats, basins and open spruce at the forest edge.
- Rock ptarmigan: a separate band about 100–200 m above the willow band. Use dwarf birch/low shrub, sedge meadow and rocky ridges, scored highest at treeline +100 to +400 m. Ruby Range nests were about 200 m above willow nests; that site's treeline is not in my sources, so use relative offsets, not absolute elevations.
- White-tailed ptarmigan: the highest band, on steep rocky slopes, scree and snowfield edges. Score it near zero below the top of the rock-ptarmigan band. It barely fits in Highland Lake's 1430–1680 m alpine. In late fall, shift it to willow basins at or just above treeline.
- Time of day: feeding peaks early morning and late afternoon (crop fill rising toward evening). Midday loafing is in brush, or on boulder and snow patches during the moult. Territorial males are most active and audible at dawn in Oct–Nov.

### Gaps
- No source gave a measured aspect preference for fall ptarmigan in the northern Cordillera. Gruys reports only that movement direction followed valley side (west-side birds to western slopes, east-side birds to eastern slopes) and that wintering sites were wind-sheltered.
- Treeline elevation at the Ruby Range site (needed to convert Wilson & Martin's nest elevations into treeline offsets) was not found.
- I found no NW BC or Yukon telemetry on rock or white-tailed ptarmigan fall movements; the figures above come from Alaska, Colorado and general accounts.

## Q2. Spruce grouse in the northern Cordillera: stand age, structure and fall foods

### Takeaway
Spruce grouse are the conifer specialist at both sites. The best evidence points to moderately dense, mid-successional conifer: densities peak in 15–25-year-old pine stands (Ontario plantations), and birds generally favour dense, young trees 6–15 m tall. Grouse density falls with stand age. In fall they eat berries (blueberry, mountain cranberry) and needles, and from late August they visit roads, lakeshores and streams for grit in the early morning. Winter diet is needles only: white spruce at Kluane, lodgepole pine in Alberta.

### Cited Findings
- [S] "Highest spruce grouse densities in North America are in 15- to 25-year-old pine plantations in central Ontario (Boag and Schroeder 1992)". "Population density and recruitment of other forest grouse decreases with increasing age of the forest (Bendell and Elliott 1967, Zwickel and Bendell 1972, 1985)". — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Spring microhabitat: males choose "mature or young forests with sparse canopy cover and openings on the ground for display". Breeding females use "open wet and dry meadows with interspersed trees and a well-developed shrub and herb layer". Hens with broods may use forest with much deadfall. — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] At Kluane in winter (1989–1992), spruce grouse fed "exclusively on needles of white spruce (Picea glauca)" and used particular "feeding trees". These trees had lower camphor, bornyl acetate and resin, and longer needles (Mueller 1993). Forest grouse densities on control plots were 0.28–3.33 birds/10 ha across the hare cycle (spruce grouse 0.28–2.5/10 ha). — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S, summary] Spruce grouse "select fairly dense stands of younger trees (20–50 feet high), often areas that are regenerating after fire or insect infestations". Birds dispersing in autumn "sometimes frequent deciduous forests". — [All About Birds: Spruce Grouse life history](https://allaboutbirds.org/guide/Spruce_Grouse/lifehistory)
- [S] In central Alberta lodgepole pine country, conifer needles make up 79% of the spruce grouse's annual diet by dry weight (read only through a search summary; the full text was not read). — [Pendergast & Boag 1971, Condor 73:437–443](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/condor/v073n04/p0437-p0443.pdf)
- [S] Coastal SE Alaska (not interior): spruce grouse "selected bog and high-volume, old-growth forest habitat and avoided clearcuts". Second growth 15–30 years after clearcutting was used in proportion to availability (read through a search summary). — [Habitat relationships of spruce grouse in southeast Alaska (thesis)](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2346/8906)
- [G] Alaska habitat: "a spruce-birch forest with a lush understory of mountain cranberry, blueberry, crowberry, and spiraea growing on a thick carpet of mosses". Summer–fall foods are flowers, leaves and berries, "particularly blueberries and mountain cranberries". "In late August, they begin frequenting stream sides, lakeshores and roads in early morning to secure grit". At night in winter they roost on the snow under spruce boughs or in snow burrows. — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [G/H] "Spruce grouse hunters generally try to be out on the clear frosty mornings of September and October, when birds are seeking grit at locations where bare soil or gravel is exposed." — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [G] Spruce grouse are found in "white spruce, spruce/birch, black spruce bogs". — [ADF&G Alaska's Grouse & Ptarmigan card](https://newsrelease.adfg.alaska.gov/static/hunting/smallgamehunting/pdfs/alaska_grouse_ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Kluane spruce grouse peaked about a season ahead of the hare peak (1989–90) and crashed through predation in 1990–91. Local survival was 27% in the hare-decline years vs 54% at the hare low. — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Post-fire tree recruitment in Yukon–BC border and interior Alaska burns was highest in the first 5 years, with no net establishment after 10 years. Stand density set early persists for decades. — [Decadal observations of tree regeneration following fire in boreal forests](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/4403)

### Inferences
- The current "young dense conifer 0.7" rule fits the northern Cordillera. For Highland Lake's burns: the 1999 and 2004 burns (22–27 years old) with dense lodgepole pine or spruce regeneration are the closest match to the 15–25-year peak, and probably merit 0.8–1.0 where the conifer is dense. The 1984 burn (42 years) is a good, declining stand at about 0.7. The 2019 burn (7 years) is too young for spruce grouse (score about 0.2) unless unburned conifer patches remain. Because recruitment stops at about 10 years, the regeneration density visible today will hold.
- Blanchard's open, SWB subalpine fir–white spruce is lower-density spruce grouse habitat than dense pine regeneration. Weight closed-canopy conifer patches and wet meadow–conifer edges, and drop the score above about 1400 m.
- Fall overlay: the existing roadside-grit bonus is supported by agency guidance from late August, especially early on clear, frosty mornings. Berry-rich understorey (blueberry, mountain cranberry) in open conifer is the fall feeding cover.

### Gaps
- I found no study of spruce grouse stand-age selection in Yukon or northern BC lodgepole pine, and no years-since-fire curve for spruce grouse in the northern boreal; the stand-age constants are borrowed from Ontario and general accounts.
- The USFS FEIS spruce grouse fire review could not be retrieved (404).

## Q3. Ruffed grouse in the Yukon and NW BC: presence and habitat

### Takeaway
Ruffed grouse are present in the southern and central Yukon and in NW BC, but they are tied to aspen and balsam poplar. At Kluane they were confined to patchy aspen stands and made up 9% of forest-grouse detections. Treat them as present only where Populus is mapped (riparian balsam poplar, aspen on warm slopes and young burns), never as a general forest bird at either site.

### Cited Findings
- [S] At Kluane, "Ruffed grouse ... were confined to patchily distributed aspen (Populus tremuloides)-dominated stands and thus occurred at lower overall densities than spruce grouse". They were mostly in larger aspen stands, but also smaller deciduous stands when numbers were high. They made up 9% of forest-grouse detections. Densities were 0–2.2 birds/10 ha on the aspen-rich plot. "Virtually no ruffed grouse were heard in the Shakwak Valley ... from 1991 to 1993". — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Ruffed grouse occupy all forest stages provided they "include deciduous trees, especially Betula or Populus species". Northern populations show 10-year cycles that peak and decline 1–2 years before hares (Keith & Rusch 1986). — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [G] Alaska: "In summer and fall, these birds are often found in alder thickets and willow bottoms, as well as in spruce-birch forests and aspen groves. In winter, aspen-dominated forests are preferred." Principal fall foods are "blueberries, high bush cranberries, rose hips, and aspen buds". "Males sometimes drum in the autumn also as young males disperse." Late fall birds can be found "'budding' in the tops of aspens and birches". — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [G] Ruffed grouse "prefer young stands of aspen and birch. In fall, birds frequent the forest edge where wild rose, highbush and lowbush cranberry, and blueberry are available." — [ADF&G Alaska's Grouse & Ptarmigan card](https://newsrelease.adfg.alaska.gov/static/hunting/smallgamehunting/pdfs/alaska_grouse_ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] BC ruffed grouse were most abundant at 750–1,250 m and "most common in early successional forests dominated by aspen and poplar". The Atlas extended confirmed breeding into NW BC. — [BC Atlas: Ruffed Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=RUGR&lang=en)
- [S, summary] The subspecies B. u. yukonensis is described from the Yukon and Alaska to NW Saskatchewan (read through a search summary of Birdforum Opus). — [Birdforum Opus: Ruffed Grouse](https://www.birdforum.net/opus/Ruffed_Grouse)
- [S] Yukon status: confirmed breeder, regular in winter, with no restricted-range code. — [Checklist of Yukon Birds 2017](https://webarchiveweb.wayback.bac-lac.canada.ca/web/20170426194225/http:/www.env.gov.yk.ca/publications-maps/documents/Yukon-bird-checklist-2017.pdf)

### Inferences
- Keep "aspen 6–25 yr = 1.0" only where Populus is actually mapped, and cap its effect when ruffed grouse are not confirmed locally. At Blanchard (SWB zone, little aspen), ruffed grouse should be at most a minor bonus on riparian balsam poplar/willow bottoms in the 830–1100 m valley floor. At Highland Lake, aspen or birch regeneration in the 1999/2004 burns on warm aspects is the likeliest ruffed habitat.
- The "shrub/alder 0.8" value for ruffed is supported in fall (alder thickets, willow bottoms) only where aspen or birch is within reach.

### Gaps
- No ruffed grouse record specific to the Mayo/Stewart River area or the Haines Triangle was found.

## Q4. Dusky and sooty grouse: range in NW BC and the southern Yukon; fall movement upslope to conifers

### Takeaway
Sooty grouse is a coastal species (SE Alaska from Glacier Bay south; the BC coast; most common below 750 m) and should be absent on the interior Blanchard side. Dusky grouse breeds in the Yukon (confirmed breeder) and is sparse in northern BC. It is not among Kluane's five grouse, and I found no record from the Haines Road, so treat Blanchard as outside known range until a record turns up. Where present, dusky grouse "migrate" uphill for winter into conifers: in Colorado, males moved a median 488 m up and 10.5 km away and reached winter areas around mid-October.

### Cited Findings
- [G] Sooty grouse in Alaska occur "from Glacier Bay southward". "Dense, coastal forests of tall Sitka spruce and hemlock are the usual haunts ... but they are often found near timberline among dwarfed alpine firs. Muskeg and alpine meadows are important summer and fall feeding areas." In winter they live in conifers on hemlock and spruce needles. — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [S] BC sooty grouse: present through most of the Coast and Mountains ecoprovince and "most common below 750 m", with the core in the Coastal Western Hemlock zone. — [BC Atlas: Sooty Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=SOGR&lang=en)
- [S] BC dusky grouse: "more sparsely distributed farther north"; the centre of breeding is south of 52° N. "Dusky Grouse breeds at higher elevations than Sooty Grouse, with many observations in the 1,500–2,250 m range, although breeding from 500–1,250 m is not uncommon." It is probably under-recorded because the hoot is hard to hear. — [BC Atlas: Dusky Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=DUGR&lang=en)
- [S] Yukon: Dusky Grouse is coded "* W", a confirmed breeder present in winter. — [Checklist of Yukon Birds 2017](https://webarchiveweb.wayback.bac-lac.canada.ca/web/20170426194225/http:/www.env.gov.yk.ca/publications-maps/documents/Yukon-bird-checklist-2017.pdf)
- [S] Kluane's grouse list has five species (spruce, ruffed and three ptarmigan) and no dusky grouse. — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S] Colorado radio-tracking (13 males, 19 females): elevation gained moving to winter areas was a median 488 m for males (range 183–671 m) and 122 m for females (−61 to 760 m). Distances were a median 10.5 km for males (1.0–29.4 km) and 1.0 km for females (0.1–28.0 km). Males left breeding areas around 7 July, females around 11 Aug. Arrival on winter areas was around 14 Oct for males and 23 Oct for females. Once there, birds were sedentary (median 135 m between winter locations). Birds breed in open conifer, aspen and mountain shrub but "are restricted to coniferous forests during winter". — [Cade & Hoffman 1993, Auk 110:70–77 (USGS abstract)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70128724)
- [S, secondary] Dusky grouse "move ... to denser forest areas in winter, with the odd habit of moving to higher altitudes in winter" (Handbook of the Birds of the World, 1994). Breeding range is given as "from southeastern Alaska and Yukon south to New Mexico". — [Wikipedia: Dusky grouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusky_grouse)

### Inferences
- Blanchard: no dusky or sooty rule by default (score 0). If dusky grouse are confirmed, a fall rule would move them from open shrubby slopes and burns (Aug–Sept) to closed subalpine fir/spruce near treeline from about mid-October, scoring conifer from treeline −300 m to treeline.
- Highland Lake: dusky grouse is possible (Yukon breeder, no southern-only code) in open conifer and shrub slopes in Aug–Sept, moving to dense subalpine conifer near treeline by mid-to-late October. Keep the weight low until local presence is confirmed.

### Gaps
- No Haines Triangle, Tatshenshini or Mayo-area dusky grouse record was found. The sooty/dusky boundary through the Chilkat Pass is not mapped in any source I could read.
- No northern (Yukon/BC) telemetry on dusky grouse fall movement; the Colorado figures may not transfer to latitudes with low treelines.

## Q5. Sharp-tailed grouse in the Yukon: burns, shrub openings and presence near Mayo

### Takeaway
The Alaskan sharp-tailed grouse (T. p. caurus) is a "fire grouse" of the Yukon River drainage. It uses recent burns, brushy grass–shrub openings, sparse shrub-spruce at timberline and sedge muskeg, and lives on birch buds and catkins in winter. It ranges into central Yukon, so Highland Lake's 2019 and 1999/2004 burns are plausible habitat, but I found no Mayo-area record. In BC it is confined to the Tagish Lake corner, there is no Region 6 season, and it is effectively absent from Blanchard.

### Cited Findings
- [G] Sharp-tailed grouse "prefer recent burn areas, open grass-shrub habitat, agricultural lands, sparse shrub-spruce at timberline, and sedge muskegs". — [ADF&G Alaska's Grouse & Ptarmigan card](https://newsrelease.adfg.alaska.gov/static/hunting/smallgamehunting/pdfs/alaska_grouse_ptarmigan.pdf)
- [G] "Fire is important in the ecology of the sharp-tail, since fire maintains the brushy grasslands". They also use spruce bogs, scrubby woodlands and birch–aspen parklands, and "abundance may be closely linked to wildfires and the occurrence of early seral plant communities that recolonize and dominate recent burns. Timberline and muskeg habitat are a more stable source of habitat for sharp tailed grouse in Alaska." In early fall, family groups gather into flocks that range widely once snow persists; males stay near leks. Winter staple: paper birch buds and catkins. — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [G] Fall crops typically contain "kinnikinnick berries, lowbush cranberries, blueberries, grains". Winter diet is primarily "dwarf-birch catkins"; "dwarf birch bushes are essential winter range components". — [ADF&G Sharp-tailed Grouse species page](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=sharptailedgrouse.main)
- [S] After boreal fire, "stands of aspen and birch develop on well-drained sites and are occupied by Alaskan sharp-tailed grouse" (Ellison 1975). In Alaska and Yukon, "prescribed burning has helped convert spruce muskeg habitat into more productive sharp-tailed grouse habitat" (Mossop 1979). Northern subspecies "inhabit brushy stages of northern boreal forests" (Prose 1987). FEIS gives no years-since-fire numbers for caurus. — [USFS FEIS: Sharp-tailed grouse](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/animals/bird/typh/all.html)
- [S] BC: caurus occurs only around Tagish Lake, and the Atlas found no breeding evidence in the Northern Boreal Mountains. — [BC Atlas: Sharp-tailed Grouse](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=STGR&lang=en)
- [G] BC Region 6 Schedule 6 has no sharp-tailed grouse entry; open seasons are listed only for blue/spruce/ruffed grouse and ptarmigan. — [BC Reg. 190/84, Schedule 6](https://free.bcpublications.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/190_84_07)
- [S] Yukon: confirmed breeder, present in winter, with no southern-only code. — [Checklist of Yukon Birds 2017](https://webarchiveweb.wayback.bac-lac.canada.ca/web/20170426194225/http:/www.env.gov.yk.ca/publications-maps/documents/Yukon-bird-checklist-2017.pdf)

### Inferences
- Highland Lake sharp-tail rule: open burn and shrub openings 3–25 years post-fire (the 2019 burn now; the 1999/2004 burns where still open), sedge muskeg and sparse shrub-spruce near treeline. Score highest where dwarf birch or paper birch is present for the coming winter. The 3–25-year window is inferred from "recent burns/early seral" wording plus the stated "more stable" timberline and muskeg habitat; no measured curve was found. This is the one species for which the existing "regen 0.7 (5+ yr)" and "open wetland 0.15" values are too low.
- Blanchard: score 0 for sharp-tails (no BC season, outside known BC range).

### Gaps
- No Mayo/Stewart River sharp-tail record or lek location was found. No years-since-fire occupancy curve exists for the Alaskan subspecies in the sources I read.

## Q6. Weather effects: wind, snow, first snow ("white birds on brown ground") and where birds sit

### Takeaway
Wind and snow decide where ptarmigan sit. Chilkat Pass birds left exposed territories during snowstorms and wintered where the wind was about 7× lighter. They abandoned an area once snow left less than about 1 m of shrub showing. During the white moult (late Sept–Oct) they climbed onto boulder fields with snow patches, and they turned white about 9 days before lasting snow. Forest grouse respond more to cold (snow roosts) and to early-morning grit trips than to wind.

### Cited Findings
- [S] "Both in autumn and spring ptarmigan left their territories during snow storms and moved to protected areas". In 1988 birds returned on 18 Oct, when the snow line was about 100 m above most territories, then "left again on 19 October, prior to a large storm". They returned on 23–26 Oct, when territories had 1–5 cm of snow. Departures were not reliably predicted by 24-h barometric pressure change (males r = −0.27, females r = −0.08, not significant). — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] Wind at 2.5 m above the ground or snow averaged 3.81 m/s on the breeding area vs 0.52 m/s at the Kelsall Lake wintering ground. "The difference in wind speed ... explains why in both years large numbers of ptarmigan were seen in the protected parts of Datlasaka Flats, and not closer to the breeding area, 5–10 km away". Wind raises convective heat loss, "compacts the snow, making it more difficult for ptarmigan to dig snow burrows", redistributes snow into the shrubs, and abrades exposed vegetation. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] In 1988, "heavy, wet snow bent most shrubs, which further decreased height of vegetation". Departure "is ultimately caused by lack of cover, which in turn is caused by a combination of snow depth, snow density, and wind speed". — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] During the moult, "many birds moved well above the limit of vegetation onto boulder fields, where patches of snow were already present ... they may select areas that offer protection from predators. Formation of large flocks during this period provides additional protection". Snow patches at higher elevations "offer camouflage to white-tailed ptarmigan" (Braun 1971). — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] First all-white willow ptarmigan were seen 15 Oct, nine days before permanent snow cover (24 Oct 1988). Adult mortality was high during raptor migration in early September (Gruys 1991). — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [S] In mild winters, rock and white-tailed ptarmigan (and sharp-tailed grouse) moved shorter distances than in severe winters (Hoffman & Braun 1977, Bossert 1980, others). In the absence of snow, willow ptarmigan may hold territories all winter. — [Gruys 1993](https://pubs.aina.ucalgary.ca/arctic/Arctic46-3-228.pdf)
- [G] Rock ptarmigan winter flocks "appear to be nomadic, wandering from place to place according to weather, snow conditions, food supply", and spend the day "eating, avoiding predators, snow burrowing, and waiting out storms". — [ADF&G Rock Ptarmigan species page](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=rockptarmigan.main)
- [H] "Late in September, after facing a strong, cold wind for several fruitless hours, you top out on a rocky ridge and suddenly find yourself surrounded by several hundred stretch-necked, pinto-patterned ptarmigan" (the agency notebook's hunting section, written in hunter voice). — [ADF&G Ptarmigan Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf)
- [S, summary] A moulting hen's choice of background depends on plumage state: hens "sit on snow while they are mainly white, but choose the border between bare ground and snow" when darker. This is from a spring study; no autumn equivalent was found. — [Wikipedia: Snow camouflage (summary of willow ptarmigan studies)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_camouflage)
- [G] Spruce grouse roost on the snow under spruce boughs or in snow burrows. Ruffed grouse use snow burrows in extreme cold, and sharp-tails burrow into snow at night. — [ADF&G Grouse Wildlife Notebook](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/grouse.pdf)
- [S] Kluane winter grouse censuses were not run during "falling snow or moderate to high winds", a practical note on detectability. — [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf)

### Inferences
- Wind: in strong wind or on storm days, penalise exposed ridge and flat shrub cells and boost lee-side basins, willow flats and forest-edge cells within about 5–10 km. The app's head-height wind model can drive this directly; Gruys's 3.8 vs 0.5 m/s contrast is a usable calibration pair.
- First snow and mismatch: from about late September until lasting snow, white or whitening ptarmigan sit higher, on boulder and snow-patch cells, in larger flocks. Once lasting snow arrives they return to the shrub band (males) and are better hidden. Early-season white birds on brown ground should be scored as "up and in flocks", not absent.
- Snow depth: once snow depth leaves less than about 1 m of shrub exposed, shift willow ptarmigan downslope to taller willow and the forest edge. A field rule could be: cell scores only if mapped shrub height minus snow depth is at least 1 m.
- The 8–11-year cycle means year-to-year abundance swings dwarf habitat differences. Kluane/Chilkat willow ptarmigan tracked hares (r = 0.84, n = 12 years), but ADF&G calls 10-year cycles "more legend than proven fact in Alaska" (conflict noted below).

### Gaps
- No quantified autumn mismatch behaviour study (ptarmigan on snow-free ground in fall) for North America was found. The background-matching evidence is from spring.
- Cycle evidence conflicts: [Martin et al. 2001](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~krebs/downloads/Kluane%20Book%20-%20Ch11%20-%20Grouse%20and%20Ptarmigan.pdf) and [BC Atlas](https://www.birdatlas.bc.ca/accounts/speciesaccount.jsp?sp=WIPT&lang=en) report 8–11 or 10–11 year cycles in Canada, while [ADF&G](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/education/wns/ptarmigan.pdf) calls them "more legend than proven fact in Alaska".
