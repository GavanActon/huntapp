# Fall (Sept–Oct) habitat of grizzly and black bears in NW BC (Blanchard River) and the central Yukon mountains (Highland Lake)

Evidence tags: **[S]** peer-reviewed or agency science report; **[G]** agency guidance, regulations or fact pages; **[H]** hunter/local consensus, news or field anecdote. "Elsewhere" marks findings from outside the northern Cordillera (Banff, Yellowstone, Alberta foothills, interior/southcentral Alaska). Sites: Blanchard River BC 59.97 N 136.72 W (830–2000 m, treeline ~1300–1450 m, MU 6-28/6-29); Highland Lake YT 63.36 N 134.76 W (690–1680 m, alpine dwarf shrub above ~1400 m, GMS 4-09, burns 1984/1999/2004/2019).

## 1. Grizzly fall diet in the Yukon and NW BC interior: foods, habitat, elevation and months

### Takeaway
Northern interior grizzlies are mainly plant-eaters in fall: soapberry is the staple in the Kluane/Tatshenshini–Alsek country (Blanchard), bog blueberry in the central Yukon (Highland Lake), with crowberry, kinnikinnick/bearberry and lingonberry as late, persistent fruits, Hedysarum alpinum ("bear root") roots dug in spring and again in Aug–Oct when berries are short, and arctic ground squirrels, moose and (near Blanchard) salmon as opportunistic protein. Fall foraging is concentrated in subalpine and alpine terrain and open forest, not closed conifer, so the app's alpine = 0.1 and "no elevation" rules are the biggest mismatch.

### Cited Findings
- [G] Environment Yukon (archived species page): fall foods by region are "soapberries" (Kluane region), "blueberries" (central Yukon) and "ground squirrels" (northern Yukon, some years); "some bears" use salmon in the Tatshenshini/Klukshu and Fishing Branch streams. Soapberries come into use "in mid-July". Summer foods are grasses and horsetails in alpine areas; spring foods are "roots of Eskimo potato" (Hedysarum), overwintered cranberries and bearberries, marmots, ground squirrels, carrion and newborn moose/caribou calves. Bears forage in the "alpine" and "subalpine zone". Territorial population was "6,000–7,000". — [Environment Yukon grizzly page, archived 2006](https://bac-lac.wayback.archive-it.org/web/20061107092232/http://www.environmentyukon.gov.yk.ca/grizzly.html)
  - The same page says bears "consum[e] up to one kilogram per day" in fall; this figure is ambiguous (probably weight gain, not intake) and should not be used as a constant.
- [G] Parks Canada, Alsek River valley (Kluane NP, the upper Alsek valley next to the Tatshenshini, directly analogous to Blanchard). Upstream of Lowell Lake (interior vegetation), the main grizzly foods by month are: **May**: bearroot (Hedysarum alpinum) roots, horsetail leaves, overwintered kinnikinnick berries. **June**: horsetail, locoweed (Oxytropis) flowers. **July**: locoweed, horsetail, soapberry (Shepherdia canadensis). **August/September**: soapberry, kinnikinnick berries, and bearroot roots ("most important when berries are less abundant"). Downstream (wetter coastal vegetation) foods add blueberry, crowberry, raspberry, currant, gooseberry, highbush-cranberry, soapberry, and roots of bearroot, sweet-cicely and angelica. "Less common" items are cow parsnip, grasses, sedges, willow catkins, rosehips, arctic ground squirrels, moose, and "salmon on the lower Alsek". Both species use the valley April–October. — [Parks Canada, Bears in the Alsek River Valley](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/yt/kluane/activ/alsek/ours-bears)
- [S] Northern Yukon (Firth River, Ivvavik NP; Arctic Cordillera, from 176 scats, 222 h of observation and 99 feeding sites, 1993–95): spring = Hedysarum alpinum roots and overwintered crowberry; summer = horsetail (Equisetum arvense) and bearflower (Boykinia richardsonii); **fall = bog blueberry (Vaccinium uliginosum), crowberry, horsetail and bearflower**; "when blueberries were not available, grizzly bears dug for alpine hedysarum roots". Bears killed or scavenged caribou and hunted arctic ground squirrels "when available". Northern food availability is lower than in southern Canada because of the short season and fewer major foods. — [MacHutchon & Wellwood 2003, Ursus 14(2):225–235](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/grizzly-bear-food-habits-in-the-northern-yukon-canada/)
- [S] Yukon North Slope (traditional knowledge plus GPS collars): in fall, grizzlies "feed extensively in areas with berries and roots, specifically bog blueberries, crowberries, horsetails, and bearflowers", and hunt or scavenge caribou, moose, muskox and ground squirrels. Habitat models show both sexes **preferred mountains in fall**: females on shallow-to-moderate slopes, males on increasingly steep slopes, plus berry-producing vegetation near wetlands. Spring use includes "hunting ground squirrels on hillsides". — [WMAC (NS) Grizzly Bear chapter, 2021](https://wmacns.ca/documents/401/7._Grizzly_Bear_Chapter_edited_final_version_KM_Aug_7_2021.pdf)
- [G/S] BC provincial account: early fruits from **mid-July** in riparian and low-elevation seral forest (e.g., "pine-soopolallie terraces"). In **late summer and fall (August–October) "high elevation berries become the major food source, mainly soopolallie (Shepherdia canadensis), blueberries, and huckleberries"**. "Late fall feeding focuses mainly on harder berries such as mountain ash (Sorbus) or kinnikinnick (Arctostaphylos uva-ursi) that persist past the Vaccinium fruiting season, and on the roots of Hedysarum." Interior grizzlies prey on ground squirrels throughout the active season. Forage value by structural stage: herb stage (2) is "very high" on bulbs, horsetails and herbs in wet meadows, avalanche slopes and alpine/subalpine meadows; shrub stages 3a/3b are "very high" in recovering burns or clearcuts with Vaccinium; dense forest stages 4–6 serve mainly as day-bedding and security cover near foraging sites; old forest (7) has value only in its gaps. — [BC Accounts and Measures for Managing Identified Wildlife: Grizzly Bear, 2004](https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/identified/iwAMAJB01020.pdf)
- [S] Elsewhere (Banff Front Ranges): Hedysarum roots have less crude protein and more fibre while flowering, and digging was "minimal during the mid-summer flowering period". In spring, digging moved to north slopes and higher elevations where plant phenology lagged. **The volume of roots in Aug–Oct feces was greater in years of poor buffaloberry (soapberry) crops**, so root digging stands in for berries. Most foods were in dry meadows, shrubfields or open forest; "the alpine zone was unimportant" there, a southern-Rockies result that differs from the northern sources above. — [Hamer & Herrero 1987](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/grizzly-bear-food-and-habitat-in-the-front-ranges-of-banff-national-park-alberta/)
- [S, secondary] Hedysarum alpinum grows in "moist, open woods and slopes, gravelly banks to alpine and sub-alpine elevations". — [Wikipedia: Hedysarum alpinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedysarum_alpinum) (search-result text only; not checked against a primary flora)
- [S] Soapberry ripens June–August across its range and in **July in Alaska**. In Alaska it is "uncommon or locally common in openings and forests of dry uplands and in aspen forests on old burns". Black bears, grizzlies and grouse "make substantial use of them in the fall". — [USFS FEIS, Shepherdia canadensis](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/shecan/all.html)
- [S] Elsewhere (Banff): soapberry fruit production is negatively tied to forest canopy cover, which **explained 70% of the variation** in fruit across 76 plots. — [Hamer 1996, J. Range Manage.](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/644214) (abstract via search and catalogue)
- [S] Arctic ground squirrels (northern Alaska, Toolik/Atigun, 68.5° N): "Females re-enter hibernation in **August**, up to eight weeks before males." Males stay active into late Sept or early Oct, so a fall grizzly digging squirrels mostly targets males or hibernating animals. — [Sheriff et al. 2013, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3720053)
- [S] Elsewhere (Denali, Murie): when autumn snows come, bears "continue for a time to dig roots, to excavate ground squirrels, and, in places, to feed on berries", and the denning period runs from late October/early November to April. Excavating squirrels from burrows is "nearly always" successful. — [Murie, Grizzlies of Mount McKinley, ch. 7](https://npshistory.com/series/science/14/chap7.htm) (quoted from search-result text; page fetch failed)
- [S] Moose, SW Yukon (6,310 km², 1983–85): grizzlies caused **58%** of calf deaths from known causes and wolves 25%. Grizzlies, wolves and licensed hunters caused 50%, 26% and 9% of all adult plus calf mortality. Grizzlies killed more calves before **June 20**. Adult female moose survival was 91% a year, with most deaths May–October. — [Larsen, Gauthier & Markel 1989, JWM 53:548](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/4246)
  - From the same Yukon report series (search-result text; the library PDFs are behind a bot wall): 93% of calf deaths fell between birth and 1 November, **95% of grizzly-caused calf deaths fell within 8 weeks of birth**, and black bears caused 4% of calf deaths. — [Yukon EMR library: moose mortality SW Yukon 1983–85](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/causes-and-rate-of-moose-mortality-in-the-southwest-yukon-1983-1985.pdf)
- [S] Interior Alaska analogue (east-central Alaska, 24 radio-collared grizzlies, Boertje et al. 1988 as reviewed by Ballard): **no moose calves were killed in autumn**. Adult moose were killed at **1 per 106 bear-days in fall (18 Sep–18 Oct 1985)**, and by adult males at 1 per 43 bear-days in autumn (1 per 26 in spring, 1 per 132 in summer). Each adult male killed an estimated 3.3–3.9 adult moose a year. Black bears "are not significant predators of adult moose". — [Ballard 1992, Alces Suppl. 1](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/alces/760.pdf); [Gasaway et al. 1986 ADF&G report](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/federal_aid/86_mo_gmu20e_gasaway_etal.pdf)
- [G] Yukon hunting regulations: "Grizzly bears found in high alpine areas are probably females." — [Yukon Hunting Regulations Summary 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)
- [S, search snippet] Berries, especially blueberries and soapberries, "form the bulk of the diet starting in late July when grizzly bears go into a feeding frenzy called hyperphagia". — [Hinterland Who's Who grizzly fact sheet](https://www.hww.ca/assets/pdfs/factsheets/grizzly-bear-en.pdf) (search-result text)

### Inferences
- **Site split.** Blanchard sits in the Kluane/Tatshenshini–Alsek "soapberry + Hedysarum + some salmon" system. Highland Lake (central Yukon, 63.4° N) is a "bog blueberry + crowberry/lingonberry + ground squirrel" system. The species weights should differ by area, not just by habitat class.
- **Fall timeline for both areas.** Mid-July to August: soapberry in open forest, river terraces and burns. Late Jul to mid-Sep: Vaccinium/crowberry in subalpine and alpine shrub-heath, until frost. Late Aug to Oct: Hedysarum digging on gravel bars, floodplains and alpine/subalpine slopes, plus persistent kinnikinnick, crowberry and lingonberry. Throughout: ground-squirrel digging in alpine meadows, opportunistic moose (adults, gut piles; calves are a spring food only).
- **Proposed fall constants for grizzly, early Sept to ~20 Sept, northern mountain areas (inference; not field-tested):**
  - subalpine shrub/heath and alpine dwarf shrub (Vaccinium/crowberry/bearberry): 0.85–0.95, up from the alpine 0.1
  - open subalpine fir / open spruce with soapberry, and young burns: 0.8–1.0, gated on canopy cover (see section 6)
  - gravel bars, floodplains and outwash (Hedysarum, also travel routes): 0.7, rising to 0.8–0.9 from late Sept
  - alpine meadow with ground squirrels: 0.5–0.6
  - willow/birch shrub (SWB): 0.6, which matches the current "shrub" value
  - closed conifer: 0.2–0.3, valued as bedding cover next to foraging, which justifies keeping the "near hiding cover" bonus
  - bare rock, ice and scree: 0.05–0.1
- **Elevation term.** In the northern Cordillera a grizzly fall score should rise toward and just above treeline: Blanchard ~1200–1700 m; Highland Lake ~1100–1500 m (the alpine dwarf-shrub band from ~1400 m and the subalpine shrub below it). Valley-bottom wetlands score low except along salmon reaches and gravel bars.
- **Hedysarum as a poor-berry switch.** Because root use rises when soapberry fails, a "poor berry year" toggle could move weight from berry habitats to gravel bars and alpine slopes.

## 2. Salmon: Blanchard/Tatshenshini system and the Stewart/Mayo system near Highland Lake

### Takeaway
**Blanchard:** yes. The Blanchard River itself carries chinook (about 1,400 large fish counted in 2019) and co-migrating sockeye. Chinook pass mostly by mid-August and spawn into early September. Sockeye in the Klukshu and Tatshenshini run and spawn into early October, and coho run into mid-October and beyond. Salmon is a real early-September food near the rivers, fading through the hunt, while Klukshu remains a known late-fall bear-fishing site. **Highland Lake:** effectively no. The Stewart system carries only a small chinook run that spawns late July to mid-September, the Mayo River run was cut by a 1952 dam, the nearest catalogued spawning sites are minor and 28–63 km away, and fall chum do not use the Stewart.

### Inferences
- **Blanchard rule.** Add a salmon term for cells within ~200–500 m of the lower Blanchard (below any barrier), the Tatshenshini mainstem, the Takhanne below Million Dollar Falls, and Klukshu Creek and Lake:
  - weight ~0.8 until ~10 Sept (spent chinook and river sockeye)
  - ~0.5 from 10 Sept to 5 Oct (sockeye in the Klukshu and upper Tatshenshini, lake spawners in Klukshu Lake)
  - ~0.3 in October (coho; Klukshu specifically)
  - Salmon pulls grizzlies (and displaces black bears) to river corridors that the berry rules would otherwise score low.
- **Highland Lake.** By my rough calculation from the Brown et al. coordinates, the nearest catalogued spawning areas are Watson Creek ~28 km away, Hess/Upper Stewart/Pleasant Creek ~45 km and the Mayo River ~63 km. All are minor and finish spawning by mid-September, and there is no fall chum. Salmon weight should be 0 for the Highland Lake rules.

## 3. Black bear fall habitat in the north: berries, burns, grizzly avoidance, elevation

### Takeaway
Northern black bears are berry specialists. In interior Alaska they move up to alpine and subalpine blueberry from mid-July to mid-September, then drop back into forest after frost for lingonberry and crowberry, and den in lowland spruce/willow by early October. Where grizzlies are present, black bears keep to forest and avoid prime grizzly sites (salmon, open alpine), so in the two mountain areas black bear fall habitat should be scored lower and more forested than grizzly habitat, and they den earlier and lower.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Interior Alaska black bears: "between mid-July and early August a major shift … with most bears moving from the lowland spring areas to higher country", linked to blueberries. "Until mid-September, [when] frosts reduce blueberry availability … bears remain mostly in alpine situations. After the blueberry season is over … many bears move back down into forested areas where … cranberries and crowberries are utilized. Denning follows"; the dens known were "at low (valley bottom or near valley bottom) elevations". The active season is "early May … ending in early to mid-October" (last sightings 2 Oct 1965, 4 Oct 1964), so 6.5–7 months a year are spent in the den. Blueberries "are by far the most important fall food"; lingonberry gains sugar after frost. Open spruce forest and muskeg was the best blueberry habitat, "however, the most productive patches occurred **at the edge of a 20 year-old burn**". Mature open deciduous forest was best for lowbush cranberry. Litter size is probably tied to fall blueberry abundance, and a 1963 blueberry failure was blamed for a spike in bear problems. — [Hatler 1966, ADF&G black bear report](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/ADFG/BlackBear/2319911-1965-66.pdf)
- [S] Southcentral Alaska (female brown and black bears, May–Sept 2000): black bears selected areas used by brown bears in spring but **avoided brown-bear areas in summer**, using high-berry areas instead. Brown bears selected salmon streams. "Black bears were displaced by brown bears" from salmon; berries are "a less nutritious, but adequate food source". — [Belant et al. 2010, Polar Biology 33:31](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70033910)
- [G] Denali: black bears are "typically confined to forested areas", in habitat that "keeps them away from contact with brown bears". — [NPS Denali, Black bears](https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-bears.htm)
- [S] In the Yukon, "berries provide the major food from midsummer until frost for black bears". Lingonberry fruit persists overwinter and "again assumes importance in black bear diets during the fall"; bog blueberries are "utilized heavily from July to September" in interior Alaska. — [FEIS Shepherdia](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/shecan/all.html); [FEIS V. vitis-idaea](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vacvit/all.html); [FEIS V. uliginosum](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vaculi/all.html)
- [S] Black bears caused about 4% of moose calf deaths in SW Yukon, against 58% for grizzlies (search-result text). They are "not significant predators of adult moose"; calf predation by black bears runs at 1 calf per 11–53 bear-days in early summer. — [Yukon EMR moose mortality report](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/causes-and-rate-of-moose-mortality-in-the-southwest-yukon-1983-1985.pdf); [Ballard 1992](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/research_pdfs/alces/760.pdf)
- [G] Yukon regulations: "Any black bears found together in autumn are likely a female and cubs. A female black bear may hide her cubs in a tree for up to five hours while she feeds." The Southern Lakes have had many black bears killed in defence of life and property. — [Yukon Hunting Regulations Summary 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)
- [G] Elsewhere (west-central Alberta): "black bear dens are typically at lower elevations compared with grizzly bear dens"; black bears also den in hollow logs or under stumps. — [fRI Research grizzly den brochure](https://friresearch.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Corrected_HWP_Grizzly_Bear_Den_Brochure_v1-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Black bear fall rule, northern mountain areas (inference):**
  - Early Sept–~15 Sept: subalpine and alpine Vaccinium heath and burns 3–40 yr at 0.8–1.0. But where a cell is also high-value grizzly habitat (open alpine near treeline, salmon reaches), scale black bear down by ~0.6–0.7 for displacement.
  - After the first hard frost (~mid-Sept at 63° N; somewhat later at Blanchard's lower valley): weight shifts to forest and forest edges with lingonberry/crowberry (open spruce, mixed, deciduous at 0.6–0.7; burn edges at 0.8).
  - From ~1 Oct: denning in lowland black spruce, willow/alder and valley bottoms.
- The current Ontario "after mid-October go to thick conifer" switch is roughly right for **black bears** but about two weeks late for the north. For grizzlies it points to the wrong terrain (see section 4).
- At Blanchard, black bears are likely in the forested Blanchard/Tatshenshini valleys below treeline. Grizzlies span the valley (salmon, gravel bars) and the subalpine and alpine.

### Gaps
- No Yukon or NW BC telemetry study of black bear fall habitat was found. All the quantitative black bear data are from interior or southcentral Alaska.
- No source quantifies how strongly black bears avoid grizzlies at an alpine berry patch (a displacement coefficient); the 0.6–0.7 above is a guess.

## 4. Den entry dates, den sites (elevation, slope, aspect) and when bears stop feeding

### Takeaway
Yukon grizzlies den roughly October to April (6–7 months) on steep slopes near treeline, with south aspects favoured in permafrost country. Mean entry dates across comparable northern and mountain studies cluster in mid-to-late October, with pregnant and mountain females first. Northern black bears den about two weeks earlier (mean ~1 Oct in interior Alaska), at low elevation in spruce/willow. Bears wind down activity for days to weeks before entry, and a poor berry crop or early snow pushes entry earlier.

### Cited Findings
- [G] Yukon grizzlies spend "six to seven months — October through April" in dens, on "south facing slopes near treeline". Dens are about 1 m high and 1.5 m across with 1–2 m tunnels. — [Environment Yukon, archived](https://bac-lac.wayback.archive-it.org/web/20061107092232/http://www.environmentyukon.gov.yk.ca/grizzly.html)
- [S/TK] Yukon North Slope: dens "generally occur on steep hillsides, near a water source and on south-facing slopes"; traditional knowledge describes "top of hill facing south with good dirt (no permafrost)". The den model selects slopes, southern aspects, hillsides and nearness to rivers, avoids tundra, and the habitat lies in the mountains (13% of the area is moderate-value den habitat and 14% high-value). Elders report later entry and earlier emergence with climate change, and permafrost slumping is ruining dens. — [WMAC (NS) 2021](https://wmacns.ca/documents/401/7._Grizzly_Bear_Chapter_edited_final_version_KM_Aug_7_2021.pdf)
- [S] SW Yukon (Southern Lakes, n = 21 dens): grizzlies selected **steeper slopes and more horizontal cover** at the den, read as stability plus security. — [Libal et al. 2012, Ursus 23(2)](https://bioone.org/journals/ursus/volume-23/issue-2/URSUS-D-12-00002.1/Microscale-den-site-selection-of-grizzly-bears-in-southwestern-Yukon/10.2192/URSUS-D-12-00002.1.full). Thesis abstract (Southern Lakes YT plus Denali AK): **females denned at higher elevations and on steeper slopes than males**. — [Libal 2011 thesis](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/4717)
- [S] Northern-central BC (Parsnip River, ~55° N; 61 collared bears 1997–2002): mountain adult females **arrived at den areas 14 Oct and entered 23 Oct**, against 26 Oct and 9 Nov for plateau females. They emerged 11 May (plateau 24 Apr) after an average 200 days (plateau 164). Mountain dens were 74% excavated into slopes and 26% natural caves, with rocks as the main stabiliser (47%), placed in **alpine/true-fir habitat at mid-to-upper elevations with an optimum mid-elevation band**. No dens were in low sub-boreal spruce, early seral or human-altered areas. Successive dens were 2.4 km apart (mountains) and 5.1 km apart (plateau). — [Ciarniello et al. 2005, Ursus 16:47](https://web.unbc.ca/~parsnip-grizzly/reports/Grizzly%20Bear%20Den%20Sites%20Ursus%2016.pdf)
- [G/S] BC account: denning runs "from November through March and usually to mid-April in the northern areas of British Columbia", in high, sloped areas with dry, stable soils. "Dens are usually on steep north-facing slopes" where snow builds up for insulation (a BC-wide, mostly southern generalisation). Wet or seepage areas, shallow soils and boulder fields are avoided. — [BC Accounts and Measures 2004](https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/identified/iwAMAJB01020.pdf). **Conflict:** the Yukon sources favour south aspects (permafrost-free soil); the BC account says north aspects (snow cover).
- [S] Alaska analogues: southcentral Alaska brown bears' mean entry was **14 Oct**, with no difference by sex or reproductive class. Males emerged 23 Apr and females with newborn cubs 15 May. Dens were all excavated, "at higher elevations on home range peripheries", and successive dens were 6.1 km apart. Compared with sympatric black bears, brown bears "denned at higher elevations, spent less time in dens, and entered dens earlier" (wording as given in the accessible summary; it is internally odd, so confirm against the full text before using the entry-date comparison). — [Miller 1990](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/denning-ecology-of-brown-bears-in-southcentral-alaska-and-comparisons-with-a-sympatric-black-bear-population/)
- [S, search snippet] Lake Clark NP, Alaska (61 bears, 2014–17): females entered dens **20 Oct**, males **28 Oct**. — [USGS, sex-specific variation in denning by brown bears](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70203008). Eastern Brooks Range (1973): 71% of new dens were dug 5–12 Oct. — [Reynolds, ADF&G 1976](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/static/home/library/pdfs/wildlife/federal_aid/76_grb_nsl_reynolds.pdf) (search-result text)
- [S] Interior Alaska black bears (Tanana Flats, the northernmost collared population): mean entry **1 Oct** (females 30 Sep, males 4 Oct), emergence 21 Apr, 205 days in the den. 83% of dens were excavated, reuse was 18%, and 29% flooded. Bears "significantly favored **willow/alder and black spruce** habitat types for den sites, avoiding marshland and heath meadow". — [Smith, Hechtel & Follmann 1994, Ursus 9](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/black-bear-denning-ecology-in-interior-alaska/). Black bears in the harsher Susitna basin entered almost two weeks earlier than on the Kenai. — [Denning ecology of three black bear populations in Alaska](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/denning-ecology-of-three-black-bear-populations-in-alaska/) (search-result text)
- [G] Elsewhere (west-central Alberta): grizzly arrival at the den area ranged **14 Oct–11 Dec** and den entry **20 Oct–19 Dec**. Dens ran from 795 to 2,284 m on slopes of 10–60°. "Cold temperatures, heavy snow or a poor berry crop may cause early den entry." "Many bears reduce their activity and movements in late fall, even before they are near their den. Many will spend days around the den prior to entry"; despite this lethargy they "can react quickly if disturbed". — [fRI Research den brochure](https://friresearch.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Corrected_HWP_Grizzly_Bear_Den_Brochure_v1-1.pdf)
- [S] Elsewhere (Grand Teton): a late elk hunt (Nov–Dec) drew only ~15 resident bears, because it "coincides with the onset of hibernation". — [van Manen et al. 2019, Ursus 30e1](https://bioone.org/journals/ursus/volume-2019/issue-30e1/URSUS-D-18-00018R2/Primarily-resident-grizzly-bears-respond-to-late-season-elk-harvest/10.2192/URSUS-D-18-00018R2.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Proposed denning terms (inference):**
  - **Black bears:** feeding winds down from ~15–20 Sept (after frost); den entry ~25 Sept–10 Oct; weight moves to lowland black spruce, willow/alder and valley-bottom slopes.
  - **Grizzlies:** active feeding continues into early October (roots, ground squirrels, persistent berries, carcasses). From ~10–15 Oct, weight moves to den terrain: steep (≥25–30°), well-drained, permafrost-free slopes near or just above treeline, south-tending in permafrost country (Highland Lake), with horizontal cover (krummholz or shrub). Entry is ~15 Oct–early Nov, with pregnant and mountain females first.
  - The Ontario rule "after mid-October → thick conifer" should not apply to grizzlies in these areas.
- **Suggested den bands:** Highland Lake ~1100–1500 m; Blanchard ~1300–1800 m. These are centred on the stated treelines and are inference, not measured den elevations.
- **Hunt-season safety note.** The late-Sept/Oct moose hunt overlaps pre-den grizzlies near treeline. Females (with cubs) use high alpine ground, so encounters on alpine benches in October are more likely to involve females.

### Gaps
- No Yukon-specific den elevation, slope or aspect numbers: the Libal 2012 paper and thesis are paywalled or blocked, and I could not reach the Yukon plan or Pearson 1975.
- No den-entry dates measured in the Yukon. The October range comes from the Yukon fact page and from BC and Alaska analogues.
- I found no northern data on when grizzlies stop eating (a hypophagia transition) beyond the Alberta "reduced activity" note.

## 5. Gut piles, moose kills and hunter safety around grizzlies

### Takeaway
Hunter offal and wounding loss are predictable, high-calorie fall foods. Grizzlies learn them, some individuals follow hunters, and self-defence kills during ungulate hunts are the single biggest known human-caused grizzly mortality where it has been measured (Yellowstone). In the north the same risk peaks with the moose season (Aug–Oct), and fatal and serious attacks at moose kill sites are documented next door (Wrangell–St. Elias 2020) and at the BC–Yukon border (2017). The app can carry a short, agency-backed kill-site protocol.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Elsewhere (Greater Yellowstone): a successful elk kill leaves **30–40 kg of edible offal** (14% of body weight excluding rumen), and wounding losses may be **10–30%** of harvested elk. After the September elk season opened, grizzlies were **2.4–2.7×** and **2.3–4.4×** more likely to be outside Yellowstone's northern and southern boundaries (Haroldson et al. 2004). Bears killed in self-defence by ungulate hunters (n = 27) were **36.5% of all human-caused grizzly mortality 1992–2000**, and 27.5% in 2001–2015. Risk "may be more a function of … hunter movements and bear responses to olfactory cues". — [van Manen et al. 2019](https://bioone.org/journals/ursus/volume-2019/issue-30e1/URSUS-D-18-00018R2/Primarily-resident-grizzly-bears-respond-to-late-season-elk-harvest/10.2192/URSUS-D-18-00018R2.pdf)
- [S] Elsewhere (Montana, IGBST): one GPS-collared grizzly shadowed elk hunters "behind and to the side … probably downwind", sometimes within 100 yards, and picked up their cold trail after they bedded. Researchers did not know how common this is. — [Anchorage Daily News, 2014](https://www.adn.com/wildlife/article/alaska-hunters-beware-bears-could-be-following-you/2014/11/27/)
- [H] Gunshots in early fall are "a dinner bell" to grizzlies (a Wyoming game official, as reported); hunting encounters are called "the single largest source of known human-caused grizzly deaths". — [gohunt.com summary of the Grand Teton study](https://www.gohunt.com/browse/tips-and-tricks/tracking-grizzly-bear-and-elk-hunter-interactions) (search-result text)
- [H] Alaska, next to Kluane: a moose hunter was killed by a grizzly at his moose kill on **20 Sept 2020** in the Chisana drainage, Wrangell–St. Elias NPP, after returning the next day to pack meat. It was the park's first fatal attack since 1980. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1240782) (search-result text)
- [H] A "predatory attack by [an] 'emaciated' grizzly near [the] B.C.–Yukon border" (2017). — [CBC North](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/north/man-expected-to-make-full-recovery-after-predatory-attack-by-emaciated-grizzly-near-b-c-yukon-border-1.4314753) (headline only; the article body was blocked, so the location and activity are unconfirmed)
- [S] Elsewhere (Kenai, Alaska): 50 brown bears were killed in defence of life or property in 1990–99, against 8 in the 1960s; "most brown bears were killed at residences or by hunters". DLP risk rose with salmon-stream, trail and road density. — [Suring & Del Frate 2002](https://www.bearbiology.org/download/spatial-analysis-of-locations-of-brown-bears-killed-in-defense-of-life-or-property-on-the-kenai-peninsula-alaska-usa/)
- [S] SW Yukon: calf carcasses were **buried at 68% of bear kill sites** (0% of wolf sites), and ≥90% of the hide was left at 46% of bear sites, so a buried carcass means a bear. — [Gauthier & Larsen, predator identification at moose calf mortality sites](https://emrlibrary.gov.yk.ca/emrlibrary/environment/predator-identification-at-moose-calf-mortality-sites-southwest-yukon.pdf) (search-result text)
- [G] Alberta guidance for hunters: move the carcass **≥200 m from the gut pile** before leaving. Hang meat **≥3.5 m high and 1.5 m from trees**. Approach the kill **from upwind**, glass it with binoculars and make noise. "If you see a bear at your kill site or if the carcass has been moved or buried, do not approach. Leave the site immediately." Do not drag a carcass to camp (it lays a scent trail). With bear spray, "only 2% of people are hurt". — [Alberta, Bears and hunters](https://www.alberta.ca/index%2Ephp/bears-and-hunters)
- [G] Idaho guidance (search summary): separate the carcass and gut pile by **≥100 yards**. Leave the carcass where it can be seen from **200 yards** on the approach. "Do not attempt to frighten away or haze a grizzly … that does not leave the carcass." — [IDFG, Hunting in bear country](https://idfg.idaho.gov/press/hunting-bear-country)
- [G] Yukon regulations: "You cannot kill a bear that has been attracted to a kill site of a harvested animal unless it is necessary for self-defence." DLP kills must be reported and the head and pelt surrendered. Baiting bears is illegal. All meat must be taken from the kill site. In Tombstone Park, "please don't leave gut piles on or near trails." Garbage and attractants must not be left. — [Yukon Hunting Regulations Summary 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)
- [G] BC: carcasses are a Wildlife Act "attractant". Leaving one where people are likely is an offence (fine up to $50,000 on first conviction), except when lawfully hunting. In parks, hunters are "encouraged to remove gut piles from near hiking trails … or advise park staff of kill locations". Baiting bears is prohibited. — [BC Hunting & Trapping Synopsis 2026–2028, Region 6](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)
- [G] Moose seasons that create offal at the two sites: BC MU 6-17 to 6-29, bulls **Aug 23–Oct 31**; Haines Highway area (MU 6-28/6-29) any bull Aug 23–31, then antler-restricted (spike-fork, tri-palm, 10-point) Sept 1–Oct 31, plus a bow-only season Sept 1–Oct 31. — [BC Synopsis 2026–2028, Region 6](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf). Yukon Zone 4 (incl. 4-09) male moose **Aug 1–Oct 31**. — [Yukon Regulations Summary 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Safety card for the app** (agency-backed; distances from Alberta and Idaho, which match general practice):
  1. Move meat ≥200 m from the gut pile, ideally upwind and in sight from ~200 m.
  2. Hang it ≥3.5 m up and 1.5 m out.
  3. Return upwind, glass first, and make noise.
  4. If the carcass is moved or buried, or a bear is on it, leave; don't haze.
  5. Never drag meat to camp.
  6. Carry spray in hand while quartering.
  7. In the Yukon you may not kill a bear for being at your kill unless in self-defence.
- **Scent model tie-in.** Because grizzlies approach from downwind and follow scent (Yellowstone and Montana evidence), Groundwind's scent cone could show a "kill-site plume" warning: the downwind plume from a gut pile is where an approaching bear will come from, and the safe re-approach is from upwind. This is an inference, not a tested rule.
- **Moose offal.** No mass figure was found for moose. Scaling the elk figure (14% of body weight excluding rumen) to a 400–600 kg bull moose suggests roughly 55–85 kg of edible offal; this is pure arithmetic, not a measurement.

### Gaps
- No Yukon or BC statistics on bear–hunter conflicts or DLP kills during the moose season, and no study of northern grizzly response to moose offal.
- No source for the time a gut pile takes to draw a bear (hours to days) or for how long bears stay on a moose carcass.
- No primary Yukon government (yukon.ca) hunter bear-safety page could be fetched (403).

## 6. Burn age and berry production in the northern boreal

### Takeaway
Fire boosts the bear berries, but on longer and more site-dependent timelines than the Ontario "3–20 yr" window. Soapberry fruits from ~5 years after fire, is still productive at 23–25 years where canopy stays open, and may need ≥25 years for full benefit. Bog blueberry recovers fast after light burns but reaches peak cover 50–120 years after fire. Lingonberry comes back within ~5–6 years but fruits poorly after severe burns. Canopy closure, not age alone, is the main limiter.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Elsewhere (Banff, soapberry): **fruit production began 5 years after fire**. In burns 23 and 25 years old, fruit was comparable to older burns, but already low where regenerating lodgepole pine canopy reached 72%. Many old burns stay open on xeric, windy or avalanche sites and remain productive. Canopy cover explained 70% of the variation in fruit. — [Hamer 1996, J. Range Manage.](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/644214) (abstract via search and catalogue)
- [S] Soapberry: "fire generally increases … density and vigor, although full benefits may not be realized for at least 25 years". After a Montana fire, "4 to 5 years were required for 25 percent of the eventual crown size". Seed production starts at 4–6 years of age. Low-to-moderate fires may raise berry production for several years. In Alaska it grows in aspen forests on old burns. — [FEIS Shepherdia canadensis](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/shecan/all.html)
- [S] Bog blueberry: "reaches its highest postfire cover and frequency **50 to 120 years** after burning" and "grows rapidly for the first 50 to 60 years after fire". Fruit resumed 3 years after moderate fires in Russia, where burn berries were larger. After the Wickersham Dome fire near Fairbanks, "recovery in lightly burned stands was much greater than in heavily burned stands". — [FEIS Vaccinium uliginosum](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vaculi/all.html)
- [S] Lingonberry appears within the first 6 years after fire in black spruce, jack pine and white spruce–birch stands. In a severely burned interior Alaska black spruce stand it "became abundant within 5 years after fire but set little fruit". Cover after light burns rose from 3.45% to 6.10%; after heavy burns it reached only 0.35%. — [FEIS Vaccinium vitis-idaea](https://www.fs.usda.gov/database/feis/plants/shrub/vacvit/all.html)
- [S] Interior Alaska: the best blueberry patches were at the **edge of a 20-year-old burn**, and open spruce/muskeg was the best blueberry habitat. — [Hatler 1966](https://www.arlis.org/docs/vol1/ADFG/BlackBear/2319911-1965-66.pdf)
- [S, search snippet] Interior Alaska: low shrubs dominate for 10–20 years after fire, after which tall shrubs and deciduous trees close the canopy and limit light. Fruit set in V. uliginosum and V. vitis-idaea follows resource availability (canopy, soil) and pollen limitation. — [Vaccinium pollen/resource limitation study, interior Alaska (bioRxiv)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/796169.full.pdf)
- [S, weak: student poster] Murphy Dome near Fairbanks, a blueberry chronosequence across burns of several ages: the most fruit was at **very old (unburned control) sites and the most recent (2019) burns**. Fruit tracked soil temperature (partial R² 0.37), moss depth (0.26), char cover (0.21) and shrub cover (0.14); insect damage did not matter. — [Moen, Heinrichs & Spellman 2026, UAF poster](https://www.uaf.edu/ursa/research-day/2026-rca-day/2026-rca-posters/Moen-C-RCA-2026.pdf)
- [G/S] BC: shrub stages 3a/3b "can be very high, particularly in recovering burned or clearcut sites where Vaccinium berries are abundant". Dense stages 4–6 have little forage, though "forests that are not as densely forested may continue to support berry patches (soopolallie or Vaccinium)". — [BC Accounts and Measures 2004](https://www.env.gov.bc.ca/wld/documents/identified/iwAMAJB01020.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Proposed burn curve for northern areas (inference):**

  | Years since fire | Weight |
  |---|---|
  | 0–3 | 0.3 (little fruit; Hedysarum or fireweed only) |
  | 4–7 | ramps 0.5 → 0.9 |
  | 8–30 | 1.0 |
  | 30–60 | 0.7–0.8 if canopy cover < ~50%; 0.4 if canopy has closed |
  | > 60 | treat as the underlying stand type |

  Wherever canopy cover is known, cap berry weight with a canopy term, e.g. weight × (1 − canopy cover)^1, following Hamer's 70%-of-variance result.
- **Highland Lake burns in 2026:**
  - 2019 burn (7 yr): rising, ~0.8–0.9
  - 2004 (22 yr) and 1999 (27 yr): likely near peak where open, so they should not drop out as they would under the current 3–20 yr rule
  - 1984 (42 yr): 0.6–0.8, depending on canopy
  - Severe burns on permafrost black spruce may fruit poorly for lingonberry in the early years.
- **Logging analogue.** The Ontario "cuts 1.0" has little analogue at either mountain site. Old burns, avalanche paths and subalpine openings play that role.

### Gaps
- No northern Cordillera (Yukon or NW BC) study measured soapberry or blueberry fruit against time since fire. All the numbers are from Banff, interior Alaska, Russia or FEIS syntheses.
- I found no source on how burns at treeline (subalpine fir/SWB) recover berries compared with lowland boreal burns.

## 7. Regulation context (2026)

### Takeaway
BC: there is still no open grizzly season anywhere in BC (2026–2028 synopsis). Black bear in MU 6-28/6-29 is open Aug 15–Nov 30 and Apr 1–Jun 30, bag 2, with no baiting, no cubs or bears with them, and no white or blue ("glacier") phase. Yukon (2023–24 summary, the latest I could read): GMS 4-09 is open for black bear (Apr 15–Jun 21, Aug 1–Nov 15, two per licence year) and grizzly (same dates, one per three licence years). Females with cubs and cubs are protected, baiting is banned, and since 2025–26 no grizzly may be killed within 100 m of the Haines Road near Klukshu.

### Cited Findings
- [G] BC 2026–2028 synopsis (effective 1 July 2026 – 30 June 2028): "there is no open season for Grizzly Bears in B.C." **Black bear: MUs 6-1 to 6-11 and 6-14 to 6-30 (includes 6-28, 6-29): Aug 15–Nov 30 and Apr 1–June 30, bag 2**; parts of MUs 5-9, 6-3 and 6-11 are closed. Prohibited: hunting "a black bear less than 2 years old or any bear in its company", hunting "the white or blue (Glacier) colour phases of the black bear", hunting bears with bait, and feeding dangerous wildlife. — [BC Hunting & Trapping Synopsis 2026–2028, Region 6 Skeena](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/sports-recreation-arts-and-culture/outdoor-recreation/fishing-and-hunting/hunting/regulations/hunting-trapping-synopsis-region-6-skeena.pdf)
- [G/H] History: BC ended trophy grizzly hunting after the 2017 fall season and then closed the meat hunt too (effective 18 Dec 2017), after 78% of ~4,200 consultation responses asked to close the loophole. First Nations harvest for food, social or ceremonial purposes and under treaty rights continues. — [CBC, Dec 2017](https://www.cbc.ca/1.4247060); [RCI, Aug 2017](https://www.rcinet.ca/en/2017/08/15/grizzly-bears-no-more-trophy-hunting-after-nov-30/)
- [G] Yukon 2023–24 summary: Zone 4 subzones 4-01, 4-02, 4-04 to 4-50 and 4-52 (includes **4-09**; 4-03 Ddhaw Ghro and 4-51 are closed to all hunting) are open for **black bear Apr 15–June 21 and Aug 1–Nov 15, "two (total) each licence year"**, and **grizzly Apr 15–June 21 and Aug 1–Nov 15, "one (total) every three licence years"**. The Zone 4 table extracted poorly from the PDF; these dates match its column order and the Zone 2 table. "All cubs and female grizzly bears with cubs are protected", and a grizzly cub is any grizzly under three years old. All cubs and female black bears with cubs are protected, and a black bear cub is under two. "It's against the law to use bait for bear hunting." Grizzly hunting is prohibited within 100 m of several Southern Lakes roads. A black bear hunter may abandon either the meat or the pelt but not both; a grizzly hide may not be wasted. Grizzly harvests must be reported within 15 days of the end of the month, with skull and sex evidence. — [Yukon Hunting Regulations Summary 2023–24](https://archives-ftp.gov.yk.ca/library/normal/PER_0115_2023_2024.pdf)
- [G/H] For 2025–26 the Yukon banned killing grizzlies "within 100 metres of the Haines Road corridor from Gribbles Gulch to Unnamed Creek No. 1" on both sides of the road, near the Klukshu fishing village, after a case involving a grizzly shot near Klukshu (announced 31 Mar 2025). — [Turtle Island News, 8 Apr 2025](https://theturtleislandnews.com/?p=91150)

### Inferences
- **Blanchard:** the hunter may meet grizzlies (at kill sites, on salmon rivers, in the subalpine) but cannot hunt them in BC, so grizzly scoring there is purely a safety and avoidance layer. Black bear is huntable in Sept–Oct. Glacier-phase (blue) black bears may be seen in the St. Elias region and are protected, so the app could note "don't shoot a blue/grey black bear".
- **Highland Lake:** both species are huntable in the fall season. The app's bear score then serves both hunting and avoidance, and could flag "bears together in autumn = female and young: protected".
