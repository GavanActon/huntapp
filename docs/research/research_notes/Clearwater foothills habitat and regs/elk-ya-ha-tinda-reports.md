## Extracted facts
- **Size.** Ground counts (highest late-winter count):
  - 2001/02: 700; 2004/05: 948; 2007/08: 300; 2011/12: 279; 2017/18: 390; 2020/21: 328.
  - Winter aerial survey 2017/18: 416 (357 in one female–juvenile group, 57 males).
  - Summer aerial survey July 2017: 279 (47 in Banff NP, 58 "in the eastern portion of the study area", 174 on or near the ranch).
  - The Berg summary gives a decline of "almost 70%" from "a maximum count of ~2,200 elk in the early 1990s to a recent low of <400". The 2019 report says the herd "has fluctuated between 400–600 elk now for almost a decade."
- **Migration, then and now.**
  - Historically ~90% migrated "~32 (25–80) km westward" to summer above 2,000 m in Banff NP.
  - Morgantini & Hudson 1988: summer to winter range 26–68 km; a yearly cycle of 52–138 km of travel and ≥2,000 m of elevation change.
  - Hebblewhite et al. 2006: the migrant:resident ratio fell from 12.4 (1977–80) to 3.0 (2001–04); ">49% of radiocollared elk" lived near the winter range year-round by 2001–04. Most consistent explanations: "winter range enhancements, access to hay fed to wintering horses, recolonization by gray wolves (Canis lupus), and management relocations of elk".
  - A new eastern tactic: migrants go "east from YHT to low-elevation (~1,400 m) industrial and recreational forest"; ratio migrants : residents : eastern migrants "~1:10:5" (Berg summary).
  - The 2019 report places the eastern tactic in "Provincial multiple use lands in and adjacent to the 2001 Dogrib fire". Eastern migrants "leave the winter range 3-4 weeks earlier than western migrants."
  - ~70% of the herd is now resident (2019 report). 39% of the collared sample migrated in 2017 and 30% in 2018.
  - IPM (final report): ~600 western migrants in 2002 fell to ~100; residents stable at ~250 since 2010; eastern migrants grew until ~2013, then levelled off.
  - "Western migratory elk included elk that migrated west anywhere into Banff National Park (BNP) or northwest into the Clearwater River". That is the upper Clearwater, not the Clearwater box.
- **Mortality, adult females 2002–2021** (n = 223): wolf 42 (18%), hunting 36 (15%), grizzly 36 (15%), cougar 13 (5%), poaching 4, road/rail 4, other 19, unknown 69. "Hunting" is "legal licensed hunting (which was legal during 2002, 2003), but is otherwise treaty First Nation hunting."
- **Calves 2013–16.** Mortality to 90 days was 0.61 overall: 0.69 for residents' calves, 0.37 for eastern migrants'. Of 57 deaths: bears 29 (51.7%), cougars 7, wolves 4, unknown predators 8, other 9. Eastern migrants faced low bear and wolf risk "because they avoided risk by using areas of high human activity."
- **Bulls 2018–20** (80 collared):
  - Tactics: 64% western migrants, 17% eastern, 19% resident.
  - Deaths: ">90% of all male elk were harvested by humans"; 20 recreational, 11 First Nation, 2 wounding losses, 2 wolf kills.
  - Survival: 0.86 at 2–3 yr, 0.72 at 4 yr, 0.42 at 5+ yr. By tactic: residents 0.85, eastern 0.44, western 0.66 (p = 0.10).
  - Under the 6-point rule, <16% of 1–3-year-olds, 64% of 4-year-olds and >83% of 5+ bulls were legal.
  - 2019 report: "Five of the eastern migrants were within wildlife management units (WMUs 316 and 318) with unlimited hunting on the general Alberta elk tag and a 3-point regulation on bull harvest. Of these 5 bulls, 2 were harvested." Two Banff bulls "migrated over 65 km in September before being harvested in WMU 418."
- **Forage, fire, cuts.** Burning raised herbaceous biomass by 65% in grassland, 18% in shrubland and 170% in forest, peaking 4–6 years after the burn. East of the YHT, herbaceous biomass "remained high due to forest clearcutting."
- **Wolves.** Survival 0.733; mortality 6.7× higher outside Banff NP; the "Panther-Dormer Corners" is the main mortality hotspot.
- **Parasites.** Giant liver fluke prevalence was 66% in eastern migrants, 31% in residents and 42% in Banff migrants; wetland exposure raised egg output.

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] The YHT herd does not reach the box on present evidence. Its eastern migrants summer near the Dogrib burn and in WMUs 316/318; 318's north edge is ~13 km south of the box, and the winter range is ~64 km away. Collared YHT bulls in 3-point general units were shot at high rates (2 of 5 in one season).
- [I] The herd fell from ~2,200 (early 1990s) to ~330–400 in winter counts. Fewer migrants and fewer animals mean less spill-over into the foothills to the north.
- [I] Eastern migrants thrive in "industrial and recreational forest" in summer through lower bear and wolf risk near people. Cutblock country can hold elk when hunting is not the main risk. In fall hunting season the human risk takes over (bulls >90% human-killed).
- [I] As a test or demo area the YHT is the best-studied elk herd in Canada (GPS since 2001, causes of death, predators, forage maps), but it is a 6-point draw (elk-ab-harvest-draws-2010-2025.md).

## Not confirmed
- The exact extent of the eastern-migrant summer range (maps are figures only; Killeen et al. 2015 not read).
- The location of the Dogrib fire relative to WMUs 316/318 (not checked).
- Where the "management relocations" of YHT elk were taken.
- Why counts differ between sources: ~1,400 in 2002 (MacAulay et al. 2022) vs a ground count of 700 (2001/02).
