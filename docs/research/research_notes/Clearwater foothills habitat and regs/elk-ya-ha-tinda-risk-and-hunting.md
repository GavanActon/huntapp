## Extracted facts
- Hebblewhite & Merrill 2009 (summer):
  - "Migration reduced exposure to wolf predation risk by 70% relative to residents at the landscape scale"; migrants used areas "6% higher in forage digestibility".
  - Residents "reduced predation risk at fine scales to only 15% higher than migrants by using areas close to human activity, which wolves avoided."
  - "Human activity effectively decoupled the positive correlation between high forage quality and wolf predation."
- Hebblewhite & Merrill 2008 (wolves): as human activity rose, wolves selected areas closer to it but showed "spatio-temporal avoidance of human activity during daylight."
- Hebblewhite et al. 2008 (forage maturation, 119 females, 2002–04):
  - The growing season "started 2.2 days earlier every 1 km east of the continental divide, was delayed by 50 days for every 1000-m increase in elevation, and occurred 8 days earlier on south aspects."
  - Migrants selected intermediate biomass; "residents selected maximum forage biomass across all spatial scales"; migrant summer digestibility was 6.5% higher.
- Hebblewhite & Merrill 2011 (132 females' survival, 150 pregnancies, 79 calves):
  - "Resident elk traded high quality food to reduce predation risk by selecting areas close to human activity, and by living in group sizes 20% larger than migrants."
  - "Cause‐specific mortality of migrants was dominated by wolf and grizzly bear mortality, whereas resident mortality was dominated by human hunting."
- Berg et al. 2021: the population declined "by almost 70% from 2002–2016". Parturition ran 8 May–11 July. Calving areas exposed western migrants to bears and residents to wolves; eastern migrants faced "low risk of bear and wolf predation".
- Berg et al. 2022:
  - Calf survivorship: residents 0.24 vs migrants 0.69. Bear mortality rate 0.37 vs 0.19.
  - "as percentage of cut forest increased, risk of calf mortality marginally decreased, which benefited migrant elk that were exposed to more clear‐cuts compared to residents."
- MacAulay et al. 2022:
  - 1,322 km of scat-dog transects in 57 5 × 5 km cells, 1 July–30 Sept 2013–2016. Scat-based risk correlated with kill-site risk (r = 0.98).
  - "Elk migrating east of the Ya Ha Tinda winter range were exposed to the highest predation risk" from cougars.
  - Early seral stands (<20 yr) are "logged areas … and post-fire forest regeneration". Wolves recolonized "in the mid‐1980s".
  - Grizzly densities on protected federal land were 2.4× those on provincial land. Black bears averaged 49 per 1,000 km² across the five WMUs of the study area (1993 estimate).
  - Herd "~1400 elk counted in 2002 to ~450 elk in 2016".
- Morgantini & Hudson 1985 (west-central Alberta wapiti, special winter hunts):
  - Rough fescue in the diet "decreased from 86.9% to 34.3%, while browsing increased"; wild rye and wheatgrass, "mostly growing in forested areas, increased 15-18%".
  - "After the hunting seasons, animals returned to the same diet."

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] Foothills elk trade forage for safety, and the main threat changes by season: wolves, bears and cougars in summer, hunters in fall. Outside the hunt, elk can sit near people and roads (a human shield from wolves). In season, people are the predator. So the elk road and pressure term should apply in the hunting season (and by day), not as a year-round avoidance.
- [I] Hunted elk move from open grass into forest (fescue in the diet 86.9% → 34.3%). This supports the plan's day/night switch and argues for a stronger in-season daytime penalty on open cuts and meadows.
- [I] Cougar risk is highest exactly where cutblock-summering elk live (east of the YHT), so foothills elk in young cuts pay a predation cost too. This is one reason densities stay low in cut, roaded foothills.
- [I] Phenology: green-up runs earlier at low elevation and on south aspects, which favours a low foothills box like Clearwater (1,014–1,372 m) in early September. That matters little by November.
