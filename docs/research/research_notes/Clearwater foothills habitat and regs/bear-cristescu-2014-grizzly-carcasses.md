# Grizzly bears at ungulate carcasses, west-central Alberta (2014)
- **Source:** Cristescu, B., G.B. Stenhouse & M.S. Boyce. 2014. Grizzly bear ungulate consumption and the relevance of prey size to caching and meat sharing. Animal Behaviour 92:133–142. doi:10.1016/j.anbehav.2014.03.020. Read from the PDF copy at https://sci-northernalberta.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Cristescu-et-al.-2014_ANIM-BEHAV.pdf
- **Second source:** fRI Research. 2020. Grizzly Bear Predation Part 2: predation behaviour and its influence on grizzly bear health. QuickNote, March 2020 (T. Larsen, lead). https://friresearch.ca/data/GBP_2019_10_predation%20QN%202_v3_0.pdf
- **Read:** 2026-10-17 (full text of both)
- **Evidence:** [S]
- **Where and when:**
  - Cristescu: a 3,200 km² study area in the mountains and foothills of west-central Alberta, around coal mines, reclaimed mines and Crown land (density 4.79 bears/1,000 km²). Of 12 adults collared in 2008–2010, 9 were analysed (6 females, 3 males). 128 animal-matter sites were found May–November 2009–2010; 124 were ungulate consumption sites.
  - QuickNote: 29 bears; 1,128 GPS location clusters visited over 2 years.

## Extracted facts
- **Cristescu, where bears ate:** ungulates were consumed "in forested areas, close to edges, and where horizontal cover was high, whereby vegetation impeded visibility of the ungulate carcass". Most sites were in moderate and dense conifer. "Distance to roads had no effect on the distribution of prey consumption sites, but carcasses were further from trails than expected."
- **Caching:** bears cached prey at 75.9% of consumption sites. Medium-sized ungulates were the most likely to be cached. Caching was not linked to the calving pulse or to spoilage risk.
- **Time at carcass:**
  - Males: 40.3 ± 8.7 h (range 3–249 h). Females: 24.0 ± 2.7 h (range 3–125 h).
  - Time increased with carcass size.
  - Males used larger ungulates (212.9 ± 31.6 kg) than females (106.8 ± 15.0 kg).
- **Sharing:** 12.9% of carcasses were used by more than one bear. The second bear arrived 210.8 ± 35.2 h after the first (range 3–400 h). Scat of other carnivores (wolf, cougar, coyote) was found at 50% of sites, more often at large carcasses.
- **QuickNote, clusters:** about two-thirds were bedding sites and about one-third foraging. Evidence of predation or scavenging was found at "just over 13%".
- **QuickNote, prey remains:** 53 moose (39 yearlings), 20 elk (16 yearlings), 12 deer and 13 other.
- **QuickNote, who was there:** adult males were at 40% of prey-remains clusters. "For the really big moose and elk, 51% of the time it was an adult male grizzly bear that was there."
- **QuickNote, where:** "Moose remains were most common in the uncut forest just outside the harvest blocks and other forest edges". Bears "take advantage of this at key times of the year (spring and fall)".

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **How long bears stay.** A grizzly that finds a gut pile or hung meat here may stay a day or two (up to about 10 days) and bury the carcass. A second bear can arrive days later. This backs the Alberta line "leave a kill that has been moved or buried".
- [I] **Where the bear will be.** Bears work carcasses in dense cover at edges, often the same cut-edge timber where moose are shot. Expect the bear bedded in thick cover beside the kill, not out in the open.
- [I] **Who.** Big carcasses (moose, elk) are mostly visited by adult males.
- [I] **Suggested card line:** "Grizzlies here bury most carcasses and stay a day or more. A buried or dragged kill means a bear may be bedded close by: back out and call 310-0000."

## Not confirmed
- Neither source splits carcass use by season (fall vs spring).
- Hunter-killed carcasses and gut piles were not studied as such.
- The QuickNote reports unpublished data.
- Both study areas are mountain-edge foothills about 150–200 km north-west of Clearwater.
