# Grizzly bears and forestry I and II, west-central Alberta (2004)
- **Source:** Nielsen, S.E., M.S. Boyce & G.B. Stenhouse. 2004. Grizzly bears and forestry I: selection of clearcuts by grizzly bears in west-central Alberta, Canada. Forest Ecology and Management 199:51–65. Also Nielsen, S.E., R.H.M. Munro, E.L. Bainbridge, G.B. Stenhouse & M.S. Boyce. 2004. Grizzly bears and forestry II: distribution of grizzly bear foods in clearcuts of west-central Alberta, Canada. Forest Ecology and Management 199:67–82. Both read as reprinted chapters 2–3 (with chapters 4–5) of Nielsen, S.E. 2005, PhD thesis, University of Alberta: https://sites.ualberta.ca/~scottn/documents/NielsenPhDa.pdf
- **Read:** 2026-10-17 (full text of the thesis reprints)
- **Evidence:** [S]
- **Where and when:**
  - Paper I: 2,677 km² of Upper Foothills (53°15′ N, 117°30′ W; 953–1,975 m); 19.6% clearcut since 1956; closed conifer (lodgepole pine, spruce) 41%. 21 GPS-collared bears, 1999–2002, 10,127 locations.
  - Paper II: 355 clearcut plots and 183 upland reference plots, June–August 2001–02. Clearcut age 0–46 yr. Reference stands were 105 ± 37 yr, with ≥20% lodgepole pine. Fruit was counted only on plots visited between 15 July and 1 September.

## Extracted facts
- **Seasons:** hypophagia (den emergence to 14 June), early hyperphagia (15 June to 7 August), and late hyperphagia (8 August to denning). Late hyperphagia is when bears "sought out berries from Shepherdia canadensis and Vaccinium spp. followed by late season digging for Hedysarum spp."
- **Clearcut selection vs the rest of the landscape (odds ratio, 95% CI):** hypophagia 1.14 (0.88–1.46); early hyperphagia 1.56 (1.31–1.85); late hyperphagia 0.85 (0.59–1.23). So in fall, cuts were used about as available.
- **Within cuts in late hyperphagia,** bears "used clearcuts that were irregular in shape and either young or more preferably old (up to 46 yrs)". Use fell with distance from the cut edge. Overall, cuts were used more at dawn, dusk and night (χ² = 5.69, p = 0.017). The authors read this as Shepherdia in old cuts and raspberry plus herbs in young cuts.
- **Table 3-4, food occurrence, % of clearcut vs reference-forest plots (odds ratio, p):**
  - Hedysarum 10.7 vs 2.7 (4.27, 0.001)
  - Shepherdia 17.8 vs 14.2 (1.30, 0.29, not significant)
  - Vaccinium myrtilloides 14.7 vs 8.7 (1.79, 0.045)
  - V. membranaceum (huckleberry) 6.2 vs 22.4 (0.23, <0.001)
  - V. caespitosum 37.8 vs 49.7 (0.61, 0.008)
  - V. vitis-idaea (lingonberry) 51.0 vs 81.4 (0.24, <0.001)
  - bearberry 21.7 vs 19.1 (not significant)
  - ungulate pellets 36.1 vs 39.9 (not significant)
- **Scarification** had "strong negative effects on the occurrence of Arctostaphylos uva-ursi, Hedysarum spp., and Shepherdia canadensis".
- **Fruit vs canopy (Table 3-7, predicted optimum canopy for fruit):** Shepherdia 0%; bearberry 34%; V. caespitosum–membranaceum 34%; V. vitis-idaea 64%; V. myrtilloides 100% (linear). For Shepherdia, fruit was "generally stable until canopy cover reached more than 50%, causing precipitous declines". The authors note this agrees with Hamer 1996.
- **Hedysarum** was most frequent "in clearcuts with approximately 25% canopy cover". Canopy was correlated with cut age (r = 0.66).
- **Shepherdia occurrence model in cuts (Table 3-6):** age +0.228, age² −0.00348 (printed ×100). [I] My calculation puts peak shrub occurrence at about 33 yr after cutting. This is shrub presence, not fruit.
- **Fruit production, fresh weight, all plots (Table 3-8):** clearcuts 22.9 kg/ha vs upland forest 32.3 kg/ha (difference not significant).
  - Shepherdia 6.65 vs 3.49 kg/ha. On plots where the shrub was present: 200,400 vs 98,000 berries/ha.
  - V. vitis-idaea 5.56 vs 19.18 kg/ha (significant).
  - V. myrtilloides 3.84 vs 0.35 kg/ha (significant).
  - V. caespitosum–membranaceum 5.73 vs 8.08 kg/ha.
- **Thesis chapter 5, late hyperphagia (16 August to 15 October):**
  - Adult males were found "most often in lower elevations, including riparian zones and lower foothill bogs".
  - Adult female top habitat averaged 1,805 m (±316); adult males averaged 1,301 m (±364).
  - Males favoured treed bog, open conifer and deciduous forest. Closed conifer and regenerating forest were least favoured by males in this season.
  - Bears selected edges strongly, read as fruit-bearing edges.
- **Thesis chapter 4 (mortality):** the thesis cites Benn & Herrero 2002 that "a high proportion of mortalities occurred in the berry season when bears were most likely to forage at low elevation sites for … buffaloberry". Mortality sites were nearer access, water and edges.
- **Related, abstract only:** Roever, Boyce & Stenhouse 2008 (Forest Ecology and Management 256:1253–1261), via https://npn.rngr.net/publications/fnn/2009-winter/new-nursery-literature/grizzley-bears-and-forestry-i-road-vegetation-and-placement-as-an-attractant-to-grizzly-bears. Roadsides had more ants, horsetail, dandelion, clover, graminoids and sedges. Interior forest had more soopolallie, blueberry species and ungulate pellets.

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **Buffaloberry depends on canopy, not cut age as such.** Fruit holds up to about 50% canopy. In lodgepole pine regeneration that is probably about 5 to 20–25 yr after cutting, plus edges and gaps of older stands.
- [I] **Cut-age window.** The app's 3–20 yr window at 1.0 is roughly right for fruit. It should start at 5 yr and taper over 21–30 yr. The 21–30 yr class is the box's largest cut class (8.1%).
- [I] **Grizzlies in fall** do not favour cuts overall (OR 0.85). They use edges of young and old cuts, mostly at dusk and at night. A flat daytime 1.0 for cuts overstates fall grizzly use.
- [I] **Lingonberry.** This persistent late fruit is about 3.5 times more productive (by fresh weight) in upland conifer than in cuts here. That gives closed upland pine and spruce some real late-fall food value, mainly for black bears.
- [I] **Which grizzlies.** Clearwater is low foothills (≤1,372 m). Fall grizzlies there are more likely adult males, whose fall habitat averaged about 1,300 m; females averaged about 1,800 m.
- [I] **Scarification** removes buffaloberry, bearberry and sweetvetch. If site preparation is known, downweight scarified cuts.

## Not confirmed
- The study area is about 150 km north-west of Clearwater and mainly Upper Foothills.
- These papers contain no black bear data.
- The thesis reprints may differ slightly from the published papers.
- The roughly 33 yr shrub peak is my own calculation from a model with large standard errors.
- The fruit counts come from two years only, and fruit was sampled only up to 1 September.
