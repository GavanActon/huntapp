## Extracted facts (spruce grouse)
- "In southwestern Alberta, Spruce Grouse begin to use lodgepole pine forests once such stands have reached ten years of age, with population density increasing to a maximum of 24 birds per 100 ha in 30-year-old pine stands (Boag and Schroeder 1987). The suitability of the stand begins to decline at 40 years of age (Boag 1991) and the bird population again decreases (Schroeder and Boag 1991) as the stand ages."
- Use and density are "associated with shorter canopies, lower densities of Populus spp., and higher densities of spruce (Boag and Schroeder 1987)". Schroeder and Boag (1991) "indicated that habitat suitability increases when even a few shade-tolerant conifers are interspersed with the pine."
- "Schroeder and Boag (1991) revealed that in Alberta, Spruce Grouse may actually be attracted to young lodgepole pine stands, suggesting that its habitat may improve following burning or timber harvesting."
- "In optimal habitat in Alberta, an average density of 12.2 territorial males per km² is common (Boag 1991; Schroeder and Boag 1991)." Density range is 4–50 individuals/km². Winter range expands to 100–150 ha (Ellison 1973). The smallest known occupied patch is 8 ha, and patches under 4 ha are too small (Whitcomb et al. 1996).
- In late summer, early fall and winter the species is an "upper-canopy forager" on buds and needles of spruce, lodgepole pine and fir. It forms winter "activity trees".
- Cover suitability vs stand age (Figure 7, read from graph): 0 at 0–10 yr, linear to 1.0 at 30 yr, 1.0 to 40 yr, linear to 0.5 at 100 yr, 0.5 beyond.
- Food suitability rises linearly to a maximum at 75% desirable conifer (spruce/pine weight 1, fir 0.8) and at 75% canopy closure. Cover suitability peaks at 25% desirable species, with white spruce weighted 1.1 (preferred roost tree), black spruce 1.0, fir 0.9 and pine 0.9.
- Nest success fell within 10 m of a trail or seismic line (Keppie & Herzog 1978), and the model docks 0.1 where a road, seismic line or utility line is in the pixel. "Since the bird does not flee when disturbed by a human, it can easily be killed."
- The model treats Alberta birds as the Franklin's subspecies (*D. c. franklinii*).
- Original citations as listed: Boag, D.A. 1991. Spring population density of Spruce Grouse and pine forest maturation. *Ornis Scandinavica* 22:181–185. Boag, D.A. and M.A. Schroeder. 1987. *Can. J. Zool.* 65:2430–2435. Schroeder, M.A. and D.A. Boag. 1991. Spruce Grouse populations in successional lodgepole pine. *Ornis Scandinavica* 22:186–191.

## Extracted facts (ruffed grouse)
- Very young (<10 yr) aspen is "most suitable as chick summering habitat". "Preferred food material is readily available in immature, mature, and old aspen forests (i.e., those older than 25 years of age)." "In Alberta, the preferred species after aspen is willow (Doerr et al. 1974)."
- Winter thermal and hiding cover is in deciduous-dominated stands and "Young stands of 10 to 25 years of age". Breeding stands are aspen-dominated and 10–25 yr old, with canopy >50%, trees >4.6 m and shrubs >0.9 m (best 2.5–3.4 m).
- Gullion (1990a): young (10–25 yr) forest optimally "within 90 m of mature aspen forest". In conifer-dominated country, relatively pure aspen sites of at least 0.4 ha support grouse if there is one per 4 ha of conifer, or if 10% of the area is aspen.
- Hunting and roads: "Studies by Fischer and Keith (1974) in Alberta suggested that the territorial male, in particular, is susceptible to hunting mortality since its drumming behaviour notifies hunters of its location. This research revealed that loss of these males to hunting decreased significantly with distance from roads. Of the birds banded, 48, 13, 5, and 1% were shot within < 101, 101 to 200, 201 to 301, and > 302 m, respectively of roads."
- "Grouse frequent roads to sun themselves on cool mornings and evenings and to eat the herbaceous vegetation along roadsides."
- Road suitability (Figure 8, read from graph; a survival penalty, not an encounter rate): 0 within 100 m of a road, linear to 0.9 at 300 m, 1.0 at ≥400 m.
- Aspen share: cover suitability rises from a minimum of 10% aspen to a maximum at 30% aspen. A reviewer pushed this to 70%; it was set back to 30% "since the birds tend to be found in more diverse habitat types than only pure aspen stands."
- Hannon noted that break-up of an overmature aspen canopy could give both the young and the mature characteristics that chicks and adults need.
- Major Alberta predators are great horned owl, lynx and goshawk (Rusch & Keith 1971; Doerr et al. 1974).
- Original citation: Fischer, C.A. and L.B. Keith. 1974. Population responses of central Alberta Ruffed Grouse to hunting. *J. Wildl. Manage.* 38(4):585–600.

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] Spruce grouse curve for lodgepole-led (and spruce-led) conifer: 0–10 yr 0.1; 10→30 yr ramp to 0.8; 30–40 yr 0.8; 40→100 yr fall to 0.45; >100 yr 0.4; +0.1 where spruce or fir is mixed into the pine. This replaces "conifer ≥70% and age 10–40 yr → 0.7, else 0.3" and needs lodgepole pine recognized as the pine lead.
- [I] Ruffed grouse: stands at 30% aspen/poplar already reach full value in this model, so the app's 40% gate could drop to 30%.
- [I] Roads cut both ways. Birds do come to road edges at dawn and dusk, which supports the roadside bonus. But hunters remove near-road birds: 48% of banded males within 100 m of a road were shot, against 5% at 200–300 m. By mid-October in a block roaded at ~1 km/km², the 0–100 m roadside strip has likely been hunted down. Keep the roadside bonus for dawn, dusk and frosty mornings, and add about +0.1 for cover 150–400 m from any drivable road once the season is ~3 weeks old.
