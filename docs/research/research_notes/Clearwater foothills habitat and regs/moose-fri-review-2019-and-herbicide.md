# fRI review of Alberta boreal and foothills moose, with herbicide evidence (2019)
- **Source:** Lamy, K., Finnegan, L. 2019. *Moose Habitat and Populations in Alberta: Boreal and Foothill Regions.* Draft final report prepared for West Fraser Mills Ltd. by the fRI Research Caribou Program, Hinton, 1 Nov 2019, vii + 68 pp. Posted 2021. https://friresearch.ca/data/CP_2021_07_Moose_Lit_Review.pdf
- **Read:** 2026-10-17. Full text of the review; for the herbicide papers, only abstracts or search summaries (listed below).
- **Evidence:** [S] A draft science review, mostly secondary.
- **Where and when:** Alberta's Boreal and Foothills natural regions, literature to 2019. West Fraser commissioned it; West Fraser also holds the Sundre FMA that covers Clearwater.

## Extracted facts
- **Cut age** (secondary).
  - "Recent clearcuts are used least ... or not selected at all by moose, since young stands of < 10 years may not have enough shrub production."
  - Favourable browse occurs "between 5 and 40 years post-disturbance".
  - "peak suitability of clearcuts for moose can fall within the age range of 7-20 years post-disturbance (Dodds 1960; Lynch 1979). As young forests age over 20 years, much of the available browse grows out of reach for moose (Lynch 1979)." Lynch 1979 is the *Moose Management Plan for Alberta*, which I did not see.
- **Deciduous vs conifer regeneration.**
  - "During summer, moose prefer cutblocks with more deciduous trees, more deciduous shrubs, and fewer pine or spruce trees within browsing height (Pigeon, McKay, and Finnegan 2019)". This is west-central Alberta work.
  - "In Alberta, preliminary analysis found that during summer, moose in the lower and upper Foothills preferred sites that were mechanically prepared during summer".
  - After mountain pine beetle in west-central Alberta, naturally regenerating sites had 70% broadleaf and 90% spruce regeneration, with slower pine (Steinke 2019a).
- **Distance from cover.**
  - Moose "will only venture a maximum of < 60m to 200m from the forest into a harvest block (OMNR 1990 in Chikoski 2003)".
  - "In west-central Alberta, moose preferred clearcuts (41-80 Ha in size) with a minimum distance of 101m between neighbouring harvest blocks (Tomm, Beck Jr., and Hudson 1981)."
- **Herbicide.**
  - Glyphosate is the herbicide most used. "In Alberta, over the last 20 years (2000-2017), glyphosate has been applied to 29,052 Ha of forest on average per year (NFD 2019)."
  - "in Alberta, glyphosate use has increased over time due to 'free-to-grow' standards". In contrast, BC is reducing glyphosate to promote moose winter browse.
  - Lost moose forage "generally persists for 1-5 years after treatment". In Maine, moose were >50% less abundant 1–2 yr after treatment, but by 7–11 yr forage and use exceeded untreated blocks.
  - "In Alberta, treated harvest blocks stopped producing increased browse at 26 years, several years before untreated cutblocks (Strong and Gates 2006)." Hexazinone effects lasted ≥17 yr.
  - "Herbicides have a stronger effect on reducing browse species important for moose during winter." Moose avoid treated sites (Pigeon et al. 2019).
- **Linear disturbance** (the review's summary of Peters 2010, GPS moose in west-central Alberta and east-central BC).
  - Moose "avoided increased human-caused disturbance and predation risk at the landscape scale, but selected human caused disturbance at the home range scale".
  - "During winter ... moose do not avoid roads and select harvest blocks for forage, exposing themselves to higher risk compared to during summer when they avoid these disturbances."
  - Foothills moose lived amid linear-feature densities of 1.67 km/km² in summer and 1.86 in winter, against 0.49 and 0.64 for mountain moose.
- **Heat stress.**
  - "Above 14°C in summer and -5°C in winter (Renecker and Hudson 1986; Schwartz and Franzmann 1991), moose become heat stressed."
  - Areas with hot periods above 27 °C and no shade are unsuitable.
  - "In windy areas heat stress can occur at temperatures 4°C to 10°C higher than in other areas (McCann, Moen, and Harris 2013)."
  - Ontario moose showed no signs of heat stress within the expected range. Nova Scotia moose sought thermal cover at about 24 °C.
- **Winter ticks and disease.**
  - Winter tick has been in Alberta moose since 1930. Widespread outbreaks occurred in 1977–79 and 1999. No outbreak is recorded for the 2015–16 El Niño. Brain worm has not been recorded in Alberta.
  - "in the Blue Ridge Lumber FMA, WMU 347 in the Alberta Foothills, between 1998 and 2005 the moose population decreased by 45.6%, with ~7.2% of the moose observed having light hair loss and 1.1% with moderate hair loss. The decrease is suspected to be caused by tick infestation through higher exposure to white-tail deer from surrounding agricultural areas (BRL 2005)".
- **Population and harvest.**
  - "in the Boreal and Foothills, moose populations have been declining since the 1980s (1978-2018 WMU reports)."
  - Alberta held 115,000 moose in 2014. Hunters took 8,227 in 2018, about 6–10% of the population.
  - "In 2001, undocumented kills were estimated as 30% of the legal harvest (Rippin 2001)."
- **Hunting after logging.** "within the 1-2 years after logging hunters can cause local moose population declines (Courtois and Beaumont 1999)." This is Québec work.
- **Supporting:** Strong, W.L., Gates, C.C. 2006. Herbicide-induced changes to ungulate forage habitat in western Alberta, Canada. *Forest Ecol. Manage.* 222:469–475. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378112705006456 (the page returned 403; content comes from search-engine abstract summaries only).
  - Hexazinone at 0, 2 and 4 kg/ha in a hardwood clearcut, sampled before treatment and in Years 5, 9 and 20 after cutting.
  - Winter ungulate forage was lower on treated plots for the whole study. A 20-yr projection gave up to ~20% less winter ungulate forage and ~6.5% less summer elk forage.
  - Summer moose forage was ~14% higher. Aspen and saskatoon were the most abundant and most affected species.
- **Supporting:** FRIAA project BRL-01-023, *Glyphosate Effect on Ungulate Browse* (Blue Ridge Lumber, Lower Foothills Natural Subregion). https://friaa.ab.ca/abstracts/glyphosate-effect-on-ungulate-browse
  - Browse abundance and use, pellet groups and vegetation were measured one year before and two years after aerial glyphosate.
  - Final report, Sept 1997, 42 pp. Its results are not online.
- **Supporting:** Higgelke & MacLeod 2000 (Millar Western moose HSI) cite Penner (1997): "the presence of unsprayed strips within the cutblock greatly increases ungulate use of the area."
- **Supporting:** fRI Moose Research Initiative update, Feb 2026, one page. https://friresearch.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/CP_2026_02_MRI-winter-2026-update-B.pdf
  - "The proportion of mixedwood, wildfire aggregation, harvest aggregation, winter severity, elevation, and proportion of fire are important to moose population sizes. Proportion of harvest was not significant."
  - "In west-central Alberta, moose are using different forest ages and types depending on season and spatial scale."

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **Cut age.** The review backs the Ontario curve's shape: a 7–20-yr peak, with browse out of reach after ~20 yr.
- [I] **Herbicide shortens the window.** Herbicide-tended conifer cuts are common in Alberta. Treated blocks stop producing browse at ~26 yr, earlier than untreated ones, and lose more winter browse than summer browse.
- [I] **Proposed regeneration-type modifier** (low confidence: McKay & Finnegan 2023 found no herbicide effect on moose occurrence). There are no block-level treatment records, so use 2020 land cover as a proxy:
  - If a 5–20-yr cut maps as needleleaf (conifer regeneration) rather than shrub, mixed or broadleaf, multiply its cut browse curve by ×0.8.
  - End its 1.0 peak at 18 yr rather than 20.
- [I] **Link to the wind model.** McCann (2013, via the review) puts heat-stress onset 4–10 °C higher in wind. The ground-wind model could relax the warm-afternoon shift into cover on windy ridges. See the heat note.
- [I] **Harvest share is a weak predictor.** Population-level fRI models found the proportion of harvest not significant. The overall harvest share is a poor predictor of moose numbers, so the score should not lean on it.

## Not confirmed
- Draft report. Many statements are secondary. I did not read Lynch 1979, Dodds 1960, Pigeon et al. 2019, Peters 2010 (403 from ScholarWorks) or BRL 2005.
- I saw Strong & Gates 2006 only through search-engine abstract summaries. The "26 years" figure is the review's wording.
- The Blue Ridge 1997 glyphosate results are not online.
- Whether West Fraser (Sundre FMA) uses herbicide on Clearwater blocks is unknown.
