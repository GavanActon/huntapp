- **Where and when:** "central Rocky Mountain foothills of Alberta" (FRIAA). Elk were released there in the winters of 2000–2004 from four source populations (thesis summary seen in search results) and compared with resident elk. GPS collars; 2-h fixes (2005 paper).

## Extracted facts
- **Frair et al. 2005, movement scales** (first-passage time on 2-h steps):
  - Three states: inactive (<50 m), foraging (mean 276.7 m, SD 56.6) and relocating (mean 1,628.3 m, SD 436.6).
  - The scale separating relocating from foraging "ranged 550–1650 m" across individuals and "varied quadratically with the mean patch size of cutover forest" in the home range (abstract, as summarised on the Enlighten page).
  - Inactive more likely where wolf use was low, "away from linear clearings", and in "cool microclimates (dense shrubs, north- to east-facing slopes)".
  - Foraging "most likely in areas with intermediate levels of herbaceous biomass and low movement costs". Relocating more likely in high wolf-use areas, near linear clearings, and when moving upslope.
- **FRIAA summary.**
  - "Elk readily used smaller cutblocks but stayed near the edges of larger ones. They spent little time near cutlines."
  - Wolf predation peaked in the first winter after release; "hunting mostly occurred in late summer and fall".
  - By the second year, translocated elk survival matched residents'.
  - Two GPS-bias correction methods were developed with the Foothills Model Forest grizzly team.
- **Frair et al. 2007, translocation.**
  - "Wolf predation, poaching, and legal Native hunting were equivalent in magnitude and accounted for the majority of elk mortalities."
  - Prior familiarity with wolves or hunters gave "first-year survival rates 1.9-2.2 times greater" than in naive animals.
  - The two main mortality sources "traded off temporally as well as spatially given the proximity of roads, which wolves avoided."
  - Abundant forage at release sites raised site fidelity but "coincided with higher mortality risk during the critical first year, potentially setting an ecological trap".
- **Frair et al. 2008, road networks** (individual-based model with empirical elk movement rules):
  - Road design caused "up to 30–55% difference in mortality risk and emigration rates", largest at intermediate densities "(1–1·5 km km−2) when road effects began to saturate the landscape".
  - "Maintaining roads in association with clearcuts caused a decline in habitat accessibility equivalent to replacing 50–75% of these foraging patches with conifer forest."
  - Varying elk tolerance gave a nine-fold difference in emigration. The thresholds "were reflected in the home range occupancy patterns of a large sample of elk in the region."
  - "road densities ≤ 0·5 km km−2 yielded the highest probability of elk occurrence where elk were hunted", but separating roads from foraging habitat, or managing access, "may maintain effective elk habitat at substantially higher road densities."

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] The box has ~1.04 km of road per km² (place-roads-alberta-access-layer.md) before seismic lines and pipelines. That is twice Frair's ≤0.5 km/km² optimum for hunted elk and inside the 1–1.5 km/km² band where road effects saturate. It is one structural reason elk are rare here.
- [I] Roads beside cutblocks cancel most of a block's forage value (50–75% equivalent loss). An elk habitat model should multiply cut forage by a road term, not add the two.
- [I] Bedding: away from linear clearings (cutlines, seismic lines), in dense shrub or young conifer on N–E aspects. That is a foothills-specific bed rule, wider than the plan's heat-driven north bonus.
- [I] Large cuts: value near edges, little in the middle. Elk moved further between foraging bouts where cut patches were larger (the 550–1,650 m scale).
- [I] Mortality traded between wolves (away from roads) and people (near roads). In hunting season, cells near roads are risky to elk; the plan's road ramp is right in sign.
