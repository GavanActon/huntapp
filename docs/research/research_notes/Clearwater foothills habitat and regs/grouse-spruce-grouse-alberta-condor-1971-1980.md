- **Where and when:**
  - (1) Gorge Creek (50°39'N, 114°39'W), 27 km W of Turner Valley, eastern foothills of the Rockies, SW Alberta. The 555 ha study area was "predominantly lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta) with scattered clumps of white spruce (Picea glauca) and poplar (Populus spp.)". Years 1970–1975; 165 classified birds plus 24 transients; 95% of birds marked; 4 radio-tagged migrant females in 1975.
  - (2) Swan Hills, central Alberta (lodgepole pine country; the paper gives no elevation or subregion), from monthly shot samples, plus captive feeding trials at Edmonton in January 1968 (6 birds, 51 bird-days).

## Extracted facts
- (1) "Migrants comprised 39% of this population; these individuals migrated each year regardless of weather conditions." Migration ran "from mid-February to late May and from early September to mid-December". In the text: "Autumn migration occurred between 17 August and 31 December."
- (1) Table 1: 22% of males (18/81) and 55% of females (46/84) were migrants. Straight-line distances between summer and winter sites ranged 0.5–9.5 km. Females averaged 5.0 ± 1.0 km (n = 9), males 1.7 ± 0.5 km (n = 4).
- (1) Timing: "The median date for females (n = 17) leaving the study area was 23 September, compared to 14 October (n = 53) for those returning for winter". The median for males arriving for winter was 24 October (n = 14).
- (1) Radio-tagged migrant females moved 1.4 ± 0.2 km/day in autumn, against 0.15 ± 0.02 km/day for resident females. "On three occasions when activity of these females was monitored before sunrise, movement began at daybreak." Birds "directly crossed ravines and ridgetops."
- (1) Winter: "home ranges and daily movements were minimal in winter and birds were concentrated in certain areas. Total density of grouse in winter was at least as great as that in summer." Birds showed strong fidelity to the same small winter area each year.
- (1) Annual survival was 75% for migrants and 83% for residents (not significantly different).
- (2) "In central Alberta this food [conifer needles] constitutes 79 per cent of the annual diet on a dry weight basis. During that part of the year when the ground is snow-covered this percentage rises to 99 (Pendergast and Boag 1970)."
- (2) Lodgepole pine needles are the main winter food, and "large amounts of Vaccinium spp. are taken in summer and autumn". The foods analysed include *Vaccinium membranaceum* and *V. vitis-idaea* fruit.
- (2) "The fact that lodgepole pine is a preferred species in Alberta (Pendergast and Boag 1970) suggests that Spruce Grouse may be closely associated with such a fire-induced disclimax. It remains to be established whether densities decline as succession continues."
- (2) "those parts of a pine forest inhabited by Spruce Grouse may be selected for their physiognomic features rather than the nutritional characteristics of their foliage."
- (2) The authors raise the possibility of "heavy autumn mortality, particularly in young of the year" when a sudden prolonged snow forces the switch to a needle diet (untested). There are no gizzard grit data; captive birds were given soil "for possible grit and mineral needs".

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] Lodgepole pine has to count as the spruce grouse "pine lead" here, because it is both the main food and the cover in Alberta.
- [I] Mid-October is inside the fall migration window. Up to ~40% of spruce grouse, mostly females, are moving 0.5–9.5 km to winter sites, arriving 14–24 October (medians). Expect birds to concentrate in their winter conifer stands, with short flights at first light. Birds seen on roads and edges at daybreak fit this.
- [I] Verdict-line support:
  - In the first hour of light, spruce grouse are on the move.
  - Once snow covers the ground, spruce grouse feed in conifer crowns (99% needles), so look up into pine and spruce.
  - Before snow, open pine with blueberry and lingonberry is the feeding cover.

## Not confirmed
- Pendergast & Boag 1970 (*J. Wildl. Manage.* 34:605–611), which has the monthly diet values, was not read.
- Keppie 1977, "Snow cover and the use of trees by spruce grouse in autumn" (*Condor* 79:382–384), studied the same Turner Valley lodgepole site in 1970–72. Only metadata was seen; the full text was blocked.
- Whether Clearwater spruce grouse are Franklin's (*franklinii*) or intergrades was not established.
