## Extracted facts
- **McKenzie et al. 2012.**
  - The area "supports prey populations of moose …, mule and white-tailed deer …, and elk … as well as their main predator, wolves. Wolf densities ranged from 9.7 to 22.3 wolves per 1000 km²" (citing Webb 2009).
  - "Seismic line density varied from 0.18 km km−2 near the Western border to 4.4 km km−2 near Rocky Mountain House, with a mean of 1.8 km km−2." Collared wolves' territories had 1.73–3.60 km/km².
  - Wolf movement: mean-squared displacement was 0.017 km² per 5 min off lines vs 0.043 on lines (4.90 vs 12.4 km² per day for wolf 233).
  - "Encounter rates were significantly higher in landscapes with high seismic line density and were most pronounced at low prey densities." The functional response was type III, with a seismic-line × prey interaction.
  - "in landscapes with high densities of seismic lines, species such as caribou … and elk … may be at higher predation risk owing to higher encounter rates".
- **Webb et al. 2011.**
  - Annual wolf harvest rate 0.34: traplines 0.22 ± 0.03, hunters 0.12 ± 0.04. 71% of harvested wolves were pre-reproductive.
  - Pack breeding probability 0.83; litters averaged 5.6; natural mortality 0.04; dispersal 0.25. "Reproductive rates balanced total wolf mortality, indicating harvest was likely sustainable."
- **Knopff et al. 2010.**
  - 1,509 predation events; 9,543 days of continuous monitoring of 42 GPS cougars.
  - Ungulates killed per year: subadult female 24, subadult male 31, adult male 35, adult female 42, adult female with kittens 47 and 67 (two kitten-age classes).
  - Prey mass per year: 1,441 to 4,708 kg.
  - "adult females subsisted primarily on deer", "adult males killed more large ungulates (e.g., moose)".
  - Cougars killed ungulates "1.5 times more frequently in summer when juveniles dominated the diet"; the proportion of males "increased dramatically in autumn during the rut".
  - The press summary lists elk, bighorn sheep and feral horses among the prey; no elk share was given.
- **Rocky Mountain Outlook 2008** [H]:
  - A University of Alberta experiment to sterilize wolves "to keep wolf packs small so elk herds could grow" in "the Clearwater area" was scrapped after a science panel review.
  - Researchers' wolf densities were given as 19 per 1,000 km² in the foothills vs 5 in the mountains.
  - Six-point bull tags "from the Bighorn River to Burnt Timber Creek" fell from 240 (2006) to 153 (2007) and 70 (2008).
  - Hunters and trappers take 60–80 wolf pelts a year.

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] The box sits in the high-wolf, high-line part of the east slopes. Wolves run 9.7–22.3 per 1,000 km² (foothills ~4× the mountains). Seismic lines peak "near Rocky Mountain House" (up to 4.4 km/km²) on top of ~1 km/km² of roads. Wolves travel ~2.5× faster on lines, and encounter rates rise with line density, most at low prey density: a trap that keeps a sparse elk population sparse.
- [I] Cougars add summer calf predation and rut-time bull kills. With bears (calves) and hunters (bulls), this explains why a cut, roaded, low-foothills landscape full of deer and moose forage still holds few elk.
- [I] The managers' own response in the adjacent mountain units (six-point tags cut from 240 to 70 in 2006–08; the sterilization proposal) shows the central east-slopes elk decline was a recognised problem by 2008.
- [I] For the app: a predator layer is not needed for a fall verdict. Linear-feature density belongs in the "why elk are scarce" text and in the bed and security cost.
