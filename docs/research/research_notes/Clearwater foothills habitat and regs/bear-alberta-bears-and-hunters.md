# Alberta "Bears and hunters" guidance (current page) and BearSmart hunting handout (2013)

## Extracted facts
- "Being quiet, using animal attractants and calls and travelling alone mean that hunters place themselves at a heightened risk of a bear encounter."
- **On the hunt:**
  - Carry bear spray and a noisemaker on you.
  - "Distress or mating calls, decoys and cover scents can attract bears as well as game."
  - Be cautious when tracking a wounded animal, and have a partner keep watch.
  - "If you see a bear, leave the area."
  - Watch for ravens, magpies, crows or jays at a carcass or gut pile.
  - Bears feed in berry patches, digging sites, carcasses, grain fields, garbage pits and bee yards.
  - Take extra care at dusk, night and dawn, in wind, rain or running water, and where sightlines are short.
- **After the kill:**
  - Make noise while field-dressing. "Remove the carcass as quickly as possible." (current page only)
  - If you must come back: "Separate the carcass from the gut pile. If you can, move the carcass at least 200 m from the gut pile before you leave. Bears will often be attracted to the gut pile first."
  - "The carcass should be suspended at least 3.5 metres (11.5 feet) off the ground, and 1.5 metres (5 feet) from nearby trees in an area easily seen from a distance."
  - "Approach the site from upwind, use binoculars to see if the site is clear and make as much noise as possible."
  - "If you see a bear at your kill site or if the carcass has been moved or buried, do not approach. Leave the site immediately and advise Fish and Wildlife by calling 310-0000."
  - "Do not drag the carcass back to camp." Store harvested animals at least 100 m from camp.
- **Encounters:**
  - Spray is the first line of defence. The page says "only 2% of people are hurt" when defending themselves with bear spray. A bear can run 60 km/h.
  - "Shooting the bear out of fear in bluff situations may result in the needless death of a bear."
  - Spray: a warning blast at 9–15 m, then 1–2 s blasts at 6–9 m, then continuous blasts at 0–6 m.
- **2013 handout only:**
  - "Self-defence actions by hunters have been the number one cause of death for grizzly bears since 2006."
  - "several grizzly bears have been accidentally killed by hunters that mistook them for black bears."
  - "Shooting a grizzly bear in Alberta carries a maximum fine of $100,000 and two years in jail."
  - Bear-hunter conflicts have caused maulings in Alberta, "including two human fatalities".
  - **Grizzly ID:** shoulder hump; rounded, smaller ears; claws 7.5–10 cm, often pale; toe pads in a near-straight line with claw marks 5–7.5 cm ahead.
  - **Black bear ID:** no hump; claws about 2.5 cm; toe pads in an arc. "Colour is not a good indication."
- **[H] news:**
  - 30 Oct 2025: a hunter was mauled by a grizzly south of Cochrane (Springbank area; the southern end of BMA 4). His partner shot the bear, and a second bear fled. Bears were "still trying to fatten up for winter." Sources: https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/9.6960974 and https://globalnews.ca/news/11505435/jumping-pound-grizzly-bear-attack
  - 17 Sep 2026: elk hunters on foot near Cadomin were charged by a sow with two subadult cubs. Both fired; the bear was not found (Calgary Herald via Yahoo, 1 Oct 2026, https://ca.news.yahoo.com/hunters-shot-grizzly-charged-them-215838074.html). The same article reports a 2015 Cadomin mauling at a bighorn sheep kill, and quotes the province: "From 2020 to 2025, a reported total of 161 grizzlies were killed by human contact in Alberta, 14 of those due to self-defence".
