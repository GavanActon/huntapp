# Roads, settlement and hunting: northern Alberta census and Québec GPS (2000, 2008)
- **Source:** Schneider, R.R., Wasel, S. 2000. The effect of human settlement on the density of moose in northern Alberta. *Journal of Wildlife Management* 64:513–520. https://doi.org/10.2307/3803249 (abstract read via https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2307/3803249)
- **Read:** 2026-10-17. Abstracts only: Schneider & Wasel 2000, Laurian et al. 2008, Dussault et al. 2007.
- **Evidence:** [S]
- **Where and when:**
  - Schneider & Wasel: northern Alberta, using the 1993 moose census. It compares the White Zone (agricultural and settled land) with the Green Zone (public forest).
  - Laurian et al. 2008 and Dussault et al. 2007: Laurentides Wildlife Reserve, Québec, 47 GPS-collared moose.

## Extracted facts
- **Schneider & Wasel 2000.**
  - "We found a linear decline in the density of moose with increasing distance from the White Zone. The median density of moose in the White Zone was 0.40 moose/km² compared with 0.25 moose/km² in the Green Zone."
  - "at the regional scale the density of moose was positively associated with the density of roads. The regions with the greatest moose densities also had the greatest intensity of licensed hunting."
- **Laurian et al. 2008**, *J. Wildl. Manage.* 72:1550–1557 (https://doi.org/10.2193/2008-063).
  - The study area had highways and forest roads.
  - Of 196,710 movement segments, 328 crossed highways and 1,172 crossed forest roads, "16 and 10 times lower than expected by chance".
  - "Moose usually avoided road proximity up to ≥500 m on each side but 20% of collared moose made visits to areas within 50 m of highways". The authors link these visits to sodium-rich roadside vegetation and salt pools.
- **Dussault et al. 2007**, *J. Wildl. Manage.* 71:2338–2345 (https://doi.org/10.2193/2006-499).
  - Only 0.29% (spring) and 0.18% (summer) of 2-h steps crossed a highway.
  - "roads and their surroundings are perceived as low-quality habitat by moose."
- **Alberta cross-references** (details in the other notes):

  | Source | Road finding |

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **Two scales give opposite signals.**
  - Regionally, settled and roaded country can hold more moose: the farmland edge offers food and fewer predators. Clearwater's 8% White Area on its east edge may have moose along its forest margin.
  - Locally, moose avoid roads out to ~500 m (Québec), and fall habitat use tracks distance to road (BC).
- [I] **The current access factor does not separate quiet ground from roadside.** It treats roads as hunter access: 0.85 within 150 m, 1.0 out to 2.5 km. No point in Clearwater is more than 1.7 km from a road, so the factor is ~1.0 almost everywhere.
- [I] **Proposed moose-presence road factor**, replacing "0.85 on any road or trail":

  | Distance to open road | During open seasons | Outside open seasons |
  |---|---|---|
  | <100 m | ×0.7 | ×0.9 |
  | 100–300 m | ×0.85 | 1.0 |
  | 300–500 m | ×0.95 | 1.0 |
  | >500 m | 1.0 | 1.0 |

  Keep the paved-highway factor (×0.6 within 250 m of Hwy 752, rising to 1.0 at 1 km).
- [I] **White Area edge.** Make no cell-level change. The evidence is regional density, not habitat at the cell scale.

## Not confirmed

- I found no Alberta study of moose kill locations relative to road distance.
- Québec highways carry far more traffic than Clearwater's gravel roads. The abstract's 500 m figure covers highways and forest roads together.
