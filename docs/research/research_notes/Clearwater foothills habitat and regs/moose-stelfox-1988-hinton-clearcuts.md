# Moose use of foothills clear-cuts over 32 years, Hinton (1988)
- **Source:** Stelfox, J.G. 1988. *Forest succession and wildlife abundance following clear-cut logging in west-central Alberta.* Report dated 15 December 1988, 166 pp. Funded by the Alberta Recreation, Parks and Wildlife Foundation, Weldwood of Canada (Hinton Division), Wildlife Habitat Canada, the Alberta Fish and Game Association and Alberta Fish and Wildlife. https://www.archive.org/download/forestsuccession00stel/forestsuccession00stel.pdf
- **Read:** 2026-10-17. Full text from an OCR scan: abstract, study area, sections 4.3.1–4.3.4 and the summary. Figure and appendix numbers were not extracted.
- **Evidence:** [S] Agency-backed field study. It is unreplicated: one logged area per forest type.
- **Where and when:**
  - Foothills near Hinton, ~53° N, 117° W.
  - Three forest types: old (125–140 yr) white spruce and mature (80–100 yr) mixedwood at ~1,000 m; young-mature (60–70 yr) lodgepole pine at ~1,350 m.
  - Logged in 1956–57, some blocks scarified. Pellet-group, track and sign counts ran 1956–1988 (Years 1–32).
  - Snow cover early November to mid/late April.
  - Pre-logging densities were 0.6 moose, 0.8 deer and 0.4 elk per km².

## Extracted facts
- **Stages.**
  - Grass–forb, 1–10 yr.
  - Shrub, 11–20 yr: "cervid use was higher in clear-cuts, especially those unscarified, than in unlogged mature forests."
  - Pole-sapling, 15–25 yr for mixedwood and pine, 20–40 yr for spruce: "Use by elk and moose declined while deer use increased. The former two species are more sensitive to human harassment than are deer."
- **Peak and decline.** Across all three forest types, "big game abundance increased up to Year 17 but then declined sharply by Year 26."
- **Pine cuts held moose longest.**
  - "Pine clear-cuts received considerably more use by moose than did spruce and mixedwood clear-cuts." Suggested reasons: more security and thermal cover in 10–32-yr pine cuts, and less human harassment.
  - Year 32: "Moose were more common in upland pine than in lowland spruce and mixedwood clear-cuts. Cover and forage conditions were optimal for big game in both pine clear-cuts." Moose did not use the spruce and mixedwood cuts that year. In the pine cuts, moose use was ~28% of deer use.
- **Residual blocks.**
  - In spruce and mixedwood cuts, moose abundance "was virtually nil, except during Years 1-11 before the coniferous residual blocks were removed when their abundance was light."
  - The 100 m-wide residual blocks were removed at Years 12–13; "elk and moose use of clear-cuts declined sharply after the mature blocks were removed."
- **Winter cover.**
  - "Security (hiding) and thermal cover was a greater determinant of habitat use of clear-cuts by deer, elk and moose than forage availability."
  - "Mature coniferous blocks, at least 100 m wide, were essential for winter thermal and security cover during the first 15-20 years following logging of the pine forest and the first 25 years following logging of spruce and mixedwood forests."
  - Cuts themselves gave adequate winter cover at 15–20 yr in pine and 25–30 yr in spruce and mixedwood (>32 yr if scarified).
  - "Critical cover values of about 50% for each of security and coniferous canopy are needed before intensive yearlong use of clear-cuts by big game will occur."
  - Winter wildlife abundance correlated with wind chill at r = −0.77 and with animal visibility at r = −0.72.
- **Browse.**
  - At Year 17, browse carrying capacity exceeded actual stocking by about 40:1. That is 6.7 ha per moose in scarified cuts and 4.4 ha in unscarified cuts.
  - At Year 26, browsing took <5% of poplar, willow and rose.
  - Mountain ash and alpine fir grew only in the pine cuts and were browsed heavily: 75% of new mountain-ash growth was gone by late August of Year 32.
  - Alder also grew only in the pine cuts. Its 11.1% browse use was more than poplar's and less than willow's.
- **Harassment.**
  - The mixedwood cuts were "interlaced with accessible roads and trails". Big-game use there declined after Year 9.
  - Access "caused an almost complete evacuation of the area by elk, and to a lesser extent by deer, within a week of the opening of the fall big game season."
  - Years 26–27: "negligible big game sign within 335 m of gravel pit activities."
  - Summer of Year 32: the mixedwood cuts beside an active gravel pit held fewer than six whitetails and no elk or moose. Spruce cuts in the same valley, closed to vehicles, held 6–12 whitetails, 3–5 mule deer and 5–6 elk.
  - "Road access and continuous harassment from human activities, plus inadequate cover, appeared mainly responsible for elk and moose populations failing to increase."
  - "Hunting may have suppressed moose and elk populations (Regional Biologist K. G. Smith pers. comm.)."

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **Spruce and mixedwood cuts.** This is the only Alberta foothills dataset that follows the same cuts for 32 years. It supports the Ontario curve's peak: use climbs to ~17 yr and falls sharply by ~26 yr.
- [I] **Upland pine cuts.** They depart from the curve. Moose used them through Year 32 because they become cover by 15–20 yr while still carrying browse (alder, mountain ash, alpine fir).
  - Hold a pine-regenerated cut at about 0.5–0.6 browse to ~32 yr, rather than 0.3 from 31 yr.
  - Count it as hiding cover from 15 yr.
  - Count spruce and mixedwood cuts as cover only from 25 yr.
  - The pine site sat at 1,350 m, inside Clearwater's 1,014–1,372 m range.
- [I] **Harassment outweighed habitat.** Clearwater has 1.0 km/km² of roads and 73% of its area within 500 m of one. The road and traffic terms matter as much here as the cut-age curve; see the roads note.
- [I] **Winter cover.** Early-winter (November) browse should require ≥100 m blocks of mature conifer within reach. The existing cover-cell rule already does this.

## Not confirmed
- One unreplicated site per forest type.
- 1950s logging (natural regeneration, scarification and slash crushing, 100 m residual blocks) differs from today's planted and often herbicide-tended blocks.
- The pine cuts were also the hardest to reach and the highest. The pine vs spruce/mixedwood contrast is confounded with harassment and elevation.
- The values in Figures 13–15 and the appendices were not read (poor OCR).
