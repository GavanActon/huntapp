# Elk vigilance near roads, and the "one vehicle every 2 hours" threshold (2012)
- **Source:** Ciuti, S., Northrup, J.M., Muhly, T.B., Simi, S., Musiani, M., Pitt, J.A. & Boyce, M.S. "Effects of humans on behaviour of wildlife exceed those of natural predators in a landscape of fear." *PLoS ONE* 7(11): e50611 (2012).
  - Read at: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3509092/
- **Read:** 2026-10-17. Full text via a reading tool; the last ~20% of the page was not read.
- **Evidence:** [S]
- **Where and when:**
  - The eastern slopes of the Rockies in south-west Alberta: about 5,000 km² of private agricultural land, public (Crown) land and Waterton Lakes National Park.
  - June 2010–May 2011.
  - 424 herd observations (15,032 elk) and 870 focal-animal observations. Herd groups: 124 in summer, 92 in the hunting season, 208 in winter–spring.

## Extracted facts
- **Threshold.** Traffic was modelled from 52 road counters and 21 trail cameras. The threshold class is "at least 1 vehicle every 2 hours (at least 12 vehicles per day)", one of seven thresholds tested.
- **Distance gradient.** "Group vigilance increased when the distance from the nearest road decreased." The reference class was >1,000 m from a road.
  - Group vigilance: 54.8 ± 3.2% at >1,000 m vs 67.7 ± 2.7% at <250 m.
  - Scan rate: 0.48 scans/min at ≥1,000 m vs 0.72 at 0–250 m.
- **Feeding cost.** Elk more than 1 km from roads had ≥20% more feeding time and foraging bouts at least twice as long as elk within 250 m. Elk within 250 m spent ≥10% more time travelling.
- **Discussion.** Elk "occupying areas close (e.g., <500 m) to roads" switch to a more alert mode once traffic reaches 12 vehicles/day.
- **Hunting season.** "The highest level of group vigilance was recorded on public land during the hunting season": 88.3 ± 4.3%. The lowest was in the national park in summer: 47.9 ± 4.9%.
- **Road types not separated.** Counters sat on "paved roads, gravel roads, unimproved roads, truck trails and ATV trails", but results are not split by surface. Roads were classed by traffic only.

## Related Alberta road and traffic studies (abstract or summary level)
- **Ciuti, Muhly, Paton, McDevitt, Musiani & Boyce (2012)**, "Human selection of elk behavioural traits in a landscape of fear." *Proc. R. Soc. B* 279: 4407–4416, doi:10.1098/rspb.2012.1483. Full text read via a reading tool: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479801/. [S]
  - Same south-west Alberta landscape: 122 GPS elk, 25 harvested, 2007–2011.
  - "Hunters access hunting areas using forestry roads and trails, searching at dawn and dusk until they detect prey."
  - Trail cameras counted 20.7 ± 5.7 people/day on weekends vs 12.4 ± 3.3 on weekdays.
  - Within 1 km of the nearest gravel road, harvested males walked 58 m more per 2 h than survivors (333.5 vs 275.9 m). Females walked 55 m more (317.0 vs 262.4 m).
  - "Elk that were harvested increased movement during weekends."
  - The rifle season ran from early September to the end of November. 93% of hunting deaths fell between early September and early November.
- **Frair, Merrill, Beyer & Morales (2008)**, "Thresholds in landscape connectivity and mortality risks in response to growing road networks." *J. Appl. Ecol.* 45: 1504–1513, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2664.2008.01526.x. [S]
  - Read: abstract only, at https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/thresholds-in-landscape-connectivity-and-mortality-risks-in-respo/, plus the WAFWA 2009 workshop abstract at https://wafwa.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/2009_WAFWA_Deer-Elk-Workshop_Proceedings.pdf (p. 10).
  - "road densities ≤ 0.5 km km-2 yielded the highest probability of elk occurrence where elk were hunted."
  - Road-network design changed mortality risk and emigration by 30–55%, "with the largest differences occurring at intermediate road densities (1–1.5 km km2) when road effects began to saturate the landscape".
  - Roads beside clearcuts cut habitat accessibility as much as "replacing 50–75% of these foraging patches with conifer forest".
  - "The ecological footprint of roads may extend for several kilometers".
  - Study area: GPS-collared female elk in the Rocky Mountain foothills of Alberta, according to a search summary I could not check against the paper.
- **Prokopenko, Boyce & Avgar (2017)**, "Characterizing wildlife behavioural responses to roads using integrated step selection analysis." *J. Appl. Ecol.* 54: 470–479, doi:10.1111/1365-2664.12768. Abstract only: https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/wild_facpub/2761. [S]
  - 175 elk-years.
  - "Elk selected areas farther from roads at all times of day with avoidance being greatest during twilight."
  - "Road crossings were generally avoided, but this avoidance was weakest during daytime."
  - Elk "sought cover and moved more when in the vicinity of roads."
  - No distances in metres appear in the abstract.
- **Muhly, T.B. et al. (2011)**, "Human activity helps prey win the predator-prey space race." *PLoS ONE* 6(3): e17050. Co-authors were not checked. Full text via a reading tool: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0017050. [S]
  - South-west Alberta: 43 cameras on roads and trails, 17 Apr–21 Nov 2008.
  - "prey species were three times more abundant on roads and trails with >32 humans/day".
  - "predators were less abundant on roads and trails that exceeded 18 humans/day". The Results give "≥18.71 humans/day".
- **Northrup, Pitt, Muhly, Stenhouse, Musiani & Boyce (2012)**, "Vehicle traffic shapes grizzly bear behaviour on a multiple-use landscape." *J. Appl. Ecol.* 49: 1159–1167, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2664.2012.02180.x. Search-result summary of the abstract only; not opened. [S]
  - Bears selected areas near roads with <20 vehicles/day and crossed them more.
  - They avoided roads with 20–100 vehicles/day, and avoided roads with >100/day at all times.
  - Near roads they moved and crossed mostly at night.
- **Thurfjell, Ciuti & Boyce (2017)**, "Learning from the mistakes of others: How female elk (*Cervus elaphus*) adjust behaviour with age to avoid hunters." *PLoS ONE*, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0178082 (published 14 June 2017). Full text via a reading tool: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0178082. [S]
  - South-west Alberta and south-east BC, 46,000 km²; 49 collared females; 2007–2012; road density 0.55 km/km².
  - Roads were classed only as close (<500 m) or far.
  - Older females "increased their use of rugged terrain close to roads".

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] **The existing highway rule fits Hwy 752.** That rule is ×0.6 within 250 m of a paved highway, rising to 1.0 by 1 km.
  - It matches Ciuti's gradient: the largest cost is at <250 m and the reference is >1 km.
  - Hwy 752 carries 150–1,000+ vehicles/day (2025 AADT), which is 12–80× the 12/day elk threshold.
- [I] **Gravel roads are where the hunters are.** Hunters work forestry roads and trails at dawn and dusk, and weekend use is about 1.7× weekday use (20.7 vs 12.4 people/day).
  - Suggested gravel-road (types 4/5) rule: ×0.8 at ≤150 m, ×0.9 at 150–400 m, 1.0 beyond 500 m.
  - Optionally an extra ×0.9 on weekends and opening days of the rifle season.
- [I] **Road density as a second signal.** With 0.82 km/km² of all-weather roads, the box sits between Frair's ≤0.5 "best" and the 1–1.5 "saturating" band.
  - A 1 km-window all-weather road density could add ×0.9 at ≥1.0 km/km² and ×0.8 at ≥1.5 km/km².
  - This is an inference; the paper's own thresholds were not read in full.
- [I] **Bigger herds near busy roads are expected.** Prey concentrating where people are (Muhly, >32 humans/day) means elk or deer near the highway or farm edge may be numerous but on high alert. Do not read those sightings as "good undisturbed habitat".

## Not confirmed
- Effects split by road surface (paved vs gravel vs ATV trail). The studies classed roads by traffic, not surface.
- Frair 2008's study area and its avoidance distance in metres. Only the abstract was read; the full text returned 403.
- The Northrup 2012 thresholds were not read at source. Paton et al. 2017 (*Ecosphere*, hunting and elk migration through a road network) returned 403 and was not read.
- Traffic on Clearwater's gravel roads. No counts exist.
