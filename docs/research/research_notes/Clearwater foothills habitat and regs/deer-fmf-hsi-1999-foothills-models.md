# Foothills Model Forest deer habitat models, white-tailed and mule deer (1999)
- **Source:**
  - Gould, D., Smith, K., Beck, B., Beck, J., Bonar, R., Todd, M. & Quinlan, R. (1999). *White-tailed Deer Winter Habitat: Habitat Suitability Index Model, Version 5* (last modified 21 Oct 1999). Foothills Model Forest / Weldwood of Canada, Hinton. https://friresearch.ca/data/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_WhiteTailedDeerWinterHabitat.pdf
  - Wood, L., Smith, K., Beck, B., Beck, J., Todd, M., Quinlan, R. & Bonar, R. (1999). *Mule Deer Winter Habitat: HSI Model, Version 5*. https://friresearch.ca/data/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_MuleDeerWinterHabitat.pdf
- **Read:** 2026-10-17 (full text of both, 7–8 pp each)
- **Evidence:** [G] planning models built from the literature with input from the Alberta Fish & Wildlife area biologist (Edson). Untested: verification level 1 (white-tailed deer) and 2 (mule deer).
- **Where and when:** Foothills Model Forest (Weldwood FMA, Hinton). Lower and Upper Foothills, Montane and Subalpine subregions. **Winter** habitat only.

## Extracted facts
- **White-tailed deer:**
  - "White-tailed deer are an 'edge' species."
  - "Most white-tailed deer habitat use is within 180 metres of cover/open area edges (MacCallum and Ebel 1985)."
  - **Cover components:**
    - S1 tree canopy closure: 0 at ≤30%, rising linearly to 1 at ≥70%.
    - S2 canopy height: 0 below 4 m, 1 at ≥10 m.
    - S3 conifer share: 0.5 at 0%, rising to 1 at ≥50%. "a pure deciduous forest would be rated as one-half as suitable".
    - S4 distance from cover to food: 1 up to 140 m, falling to 0 at 220 m.
  - **Food components:**
    - S5 deciduous sapling (≤2 m) plus shrub cover: 0 → 1 over 0–50%.
    - S6 distance from cover: "Deer need to be within 140 m of cover to have a foraging HSI of 1"; 220 m "is the farthest deer will travel from cover".
    - Feeding areas are defined as ≥25% shrub cover and ≤30% tree canopy.
  - Food:cover interspersion of 3:2, borrowed from the mule deer model.
  - Model assumption 4: "White-tailed deer do not modify their feeding and cover use behaviour in the presence of human activity." There is no road term.
  - **Cited Alberta field result (Stelfox, Roy & Nolan 1995, aspen mixedwood):**
    - In winter, white-tailed deer were more often in old (120+ yr) stands than in mature (50–65 yr) or young (20–30 yr) stands.
    - "Winter deer abundance increased with distance from roads and cutblocks and decreased with distance from small conifer patches."
    - In summer, deer were in all stand ages, with no relationship to roads or cutblocks.
  - Breeding "mid-October to late December" (Banfield 1974). Does "typically come into heat in November". Fawns born in June.
  - Optimal-habitat density about 0.04 deer/ha, i.e. 4/km² (K. Smith, personal communication).
- **Mule deer:**
  - "In forested habitat, deer are usually found within 180 m of the cover/forage edge."
  - Thermal cover is >1.5 m tall with >70% canopy closure.
  - Optimum forage:cover ratio is 60:40.
  - **Roads:** "The estimated zone of influence extends for 100 m from the road into adjacent habitat." S4 and S8 score 0 at any human vehicle access (roads, trails, seismic lines, gas pipeline corridors) and rise linearly to 1 at ≥100 m.
  - Food within 140 m of cover scores 1, falling to 0 beyond 220 m.
  - Rut "between late October and early December"; gestation 210 days; fawns typically early June.
  - Optimal winter density about 4 mule deer/km².

## What it means for Clearwater
- [I] These are the only foothills-specific deer distance rules found. The deer edge term should replace the moose curve (≤80 m 1.0 / ≤200 m 0.6 / ≤400 m 0.3) and the flat "+0.15 if cover within 500 m". Proposed deer edge curve:
  - food cell to cover: ≤140 m 1.0; 140–180 m 0.6; 180–220 m 0.3; >220 m 0.
  - cover cell to food: the same curve.
- [I] Thermal cover for November and snow: conifer share ≥50%, closure ≥70%, height ≥10 m.
- [I] The mule deer model's 100 m access zone (roads, trails, seismic lines, pipelines) is a defensible starting distance for an in-season pressure penalty. The white-tailed deer model's "no response" assumption is contradicted by Stelfox 1988 and Stelfox et al. 1995 (see the Stelfox note).
- [I] Pure deciduous stands rate half as good as cover for winter. For fall deer, aspen is food and mixedwood is the best food–cover mix. Keep hardwood and mixed browse high, and let the edge term reward conifer next to them.

## Not confirmed
- Neither model was tested against deer data.
- Both are for winter, not the fall season.
- MacCallum & Ebel (1985) and Stelfox, Roy & Nolan (1995) were not read in the original; cited here as given in the model.
