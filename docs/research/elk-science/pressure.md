# Elk (Cervus canadensis) and pressure: roads, hunters, recreation, refuges, and a pressure layer from open data

Scope: hunting-season (Sept to Dec) responses of elk to open and closed roads, trails, hunters and recreationists; where elk go when pushed (refuges); and how to build a "pressure" and "refuge" layer for Groundwind from open data. Research current to October 2026 (newest items: Proffitt et al. 2025, Bealer et al. 2026). Web search budget ran out partway, so later items came from direct fetches of primary PDFs (USFS Treesearch, Montana FWP) and abstracts pulled from the OpenAlex index. Older papers often call elk *Cervus elaphus*.

## 1. Distance-to-road effects: how far elk stay from open roads, closed roads and trails; day vs night; before vs during hunting season

### Takeaway
Elk use of the land rises steadily with distance from roads open to motor vehicles. Outside hunting season the effect fades at about 1.8 km (Starkey). In hunting season the distances that hold most elk grow to about 1.5 to 3.5 km from motorized routes (Montana). Closed roads with no traffic are not avoided, but hunters on foot use them. Traffic matters more than whether a road exists: about 1 vehicle per 12 h (Starkey) or 1 per 2 h (Alberta vigilance) is enough to trigger a response. Elk move toward roads at night unless hunting pressure is heavy.

### Cited Findings
**Starkey (NE Oregon, Blue Mountains) – distance bands vs road density**
- Rowland et al. 2000 (JWM 64:672–684) used >100,000 locations of 89 radio-collared female elk, spring and summer 1993–95, in 0.1 km distance bands from open roads. Selection ratios rose with distance from open roads and varied by season but not by year or individual. Habitat effectiveness (HE) from road density matched observed use only weakly, because the spatial pattern of roads matters and density does not capture it. The authors call for a spatially explicit (distance-based) road term. — [Rowland et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802737)
- Rowland et al. 2004 restate the 2000 result: "no relation between numbers of elk locations and HE scores based on open road density in 15 elk analysis units", but "a strong, linear increase in selection ratios of elk as distance to roads increased." — [Rowland et al. 2004, Trans. 69th N. Am. Wildl. Nat. Res. Conf.](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24797)
- **Ready-made distance-band weights (Rowland et al. 2004):** five 360 m bands plus a sixth band beyond 1,800 m. 1,800 m is "the distance at which elk response to open roads diminished markedly at Starkey". Band weights rise linearly: 0–360 m = 0.17, 360–720 = 0.33, 720–1,080 = 0.50, 1,080–1,440 = 0.67, 1,440–1,800 = 0.83, >1,800 m = 1.0. The authors suggest raising band weights where topographic barriers screen disturbance and adjusting by traffic or security cover. — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- In the Dark Meadow project worked example (Wallowa-Whitman NF), an open road density of 4.99 mi/mi² (3.09 km/km²) gave road HE of 0.20 by density and 0.17 by distance bands. Cutting roads by 17–23% raised density-based HE 40–55% but distance-band HE only 12–18%, which shows density overstates the gain when the remaining roads are spread out. — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- Elk at Starkey were "generally farther from open roads during daytime but moved closer to roads during nighttime" (Wisdom 1998; Ager et al. 2003); the same pattern was seen in South Dakota (Millspaugh 1999). Bulls avoid roads more strongly than cows (Marcum & Edge 1991). — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- Rough habitat-loss rule: road footprint about 5 acres per linear mile (1.4 ha/km). — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)

**Traffic thresholds (Starkey)**
- Wisdom et al. 2004b classed roads by day traffic: D1 = 0, D2 = >0–1, D3 = >1–4, D4 = >4–10, D5 = >10 vehicles per 12 h; night: N1 = 0, N2 = >0–1, N3 = >1 per 12 h. "Elk were generally farther from roads with traffic rates more than 1 vehicle per 12 hours, both day and night, while deer were closer." In summer 1994 elk were 338 yd (309 m) farther than available from D5 roads. — [Wisdom et al. 2004b](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24837)
- **Refuge beats road:** in the one-month either-sex bow season after summer 1994, elk moved closer to those same high-traffic D5 roads (use minus availability = −227 yd), because the roads sat inside a no-hunting zone extending 400 yd out from them. — [Wisdom et al. 2004b](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24837)
- Starkey work found "threshold rates of traffic above which a response by elk is elicited but below which open roads are functionally equivalent to closed roads". — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- During Starkey hunting seasons, open roads always carried more than the high-traffic minimum (>4 vehicles per 12 h). The hunting-pressure analysis therefore used plain "distance to open road". — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)

**Hunting season vs non-hunting (Starkey, rifle and archery hunts)**
- Distance of elk to open roads rose with rifle hunter density: distance (m) = 83 × (hunters/km²) + 751 (r² = 0.12). It also rose with daily traffic: distance = 0.8 × (vehicles/day) + 740 (r² = 0.11). During hunts elk "did not move closer to roads" at night as they did before hunts. — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- In a 6-year experiment (89 animal-years, 62 females) with three 5-day hunts (elk archery, deer rifle, elk rifle), elk avoided roads and trails more strongly during hunts. They avoided forage by day, and responses were stronger by day than at night and stronger in elk hunts than in deer hunts. Lactating females that avoided roads more strongly entered winter in poorer condition. — [Spitz et al. 2019, Ecosphere](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.2864)

**Montana (hunting season) – distances from motorized routes**
- 325 GPS-collared adult females in 9 SW Montana populations. **Archery: manage for ≥13% canopy cover and ≥2,760 m from motorized routes. Rifle: ≥9% canopy cover, ≥1,535 m from motorized routes, and blocks ≥20.23 km².** Elk avoided motorized routes more where hunter effort was higher, in both seasons. — [Ranglack et al. 2017, JWM 81:778–791](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21258)
- Elkhorn Mountains, SW Montana, 2015–2017 (male and female GPS; beetle-killed lodgepole). Across sexes and seasons, **75% of elk use lay in areas with mean canopy ≥31% (±6.65) and ≥2,072 m (±188) from a motorized route; 50% of use lay in areas with canopy ≥53% (±5.7) and ≥3,496 m (±157)**. Top models had positive effects of canopy, distance to motorized routes, terrain ruggedness and slope. — [Lowrey et al. 2019/2020, JWM 84:138–149](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21781)
- The Hillis paradigm (Hillis et al. 1991, western Montana, rifle season, continuous conifer forest) defines a security area as a nonlinear block of cover of 250 acres (≈101 ha) or more, more than 0.5 mile (0.8 km) from an open road. Such blocks should make up more than 30% of the analysis unit. — [Christensen, Lyon & Unsworth 1993, INT-GTR-303](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf); see also [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- Across 82 females in 2 Greater Yellowstone herds, "the density of roads open to motorized use was the strongest predictor of elk distribution" in the hunting season. Selection for areas closed to public hunting was stronger than selection for Hillis security habitat. — [Proffitt et al. 2013, JWM 77:514–524](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.491)

**Road density (older models, still used in forest plans)**
- Lyon 1983 (Journal of Forestry 81:592–595): habitat effectiveness drops by at least 25% at 1 mile of road per square mile, by at least 50% at 2 mi/mi², and to under 25% of potential at 5–6 mi/mi². — quoted in [Forest Encyclopedia Network, "Lyon, 1983"](https://forestencyclopedia.net/c/c8793/?showNav=n) (search snippet; curve also shown as Fig. 1 of [INT-GTR-303](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf))
- INT-GTR-303 guidance: HE ≥70% where elk summer range should keep high use, and ≥50% where elk are a primary consideration. Below 50% an area makes "only minor contributions". — [Christensen et al. 1993](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf)
- An individual-based movement model of Alberta elk found road density ≤0.5 km/km² gave the highest probability of elk occurrence where elk are hunted. Road effects saturate the landscape at 1–1.5 km/km². Road network design changed mortality risk and emigration by 30–55%. — [Frair et al. 2008, J. Appl. Ecol.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2664.2008.01526.x)
- Paton et al. note Castle-Carbondale (SW Alberta) road density of 0.55 km/km² is "at levels known to affect elk movements". — [Paton et al. 2017, Ecosphere 8:e01841](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1841)

**Other distance figures (various regions, mostly non-hunting)**
- Elk and mule deer in Colorado avoided areas within 200 m of roads, more so along heavily travelled roads (Rost & Bailey 1979). — [BHA literature review](https://www.backcountryhunters.org/media/literature-review-the-impact-of-roads-on-elk)
- Roosevelt elk (Oregon Coast Range, females 1991–94) used areas <150 m from roads less than expected regardless of vehicle access (Cole et al. 2004). — [BHA literature review](https://www.backcountryhunters.org/media/literature-review-the-impact-of-roads-on-elk)
- Roosevelt elk (6 females, central Oregon Coast Range) were found at half the expected frequency within a 500 m band around paved roads. Fewer locations also fell in a narrower band (about 125 m; the scanned figure is garbled) around spur roads open to vehicles, but **there was no difference for spur roads closed to vehicles**. — [Witmer & deCalesta 1985, Northwest Science](http://hdl.handle.net/2376/1842)
- A review of >30 studies over 5 decades found displacement of elk from open forest roads "that often exceeded 0.5–1.5 km". — [Wisdom et al. 2018, For. Ecol. Manage. 411:223–233](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220)
- Idaho (Clearwater NF; 121 collared elk, 4,527 locations, 1986–90): in roaded areas elk used habitats with more canopy cover, most strongly cows and bulls ≥2 years old (Unsworth et al. 1998). — [BHA literature review](https://www.backcountryhunters.org/media/literature-review-the-impact-of-roads-on-elk)
- South Dakota (human-dominated landscape; 21 females, 7 males): males made home ranges near roads with no vehicle traffic in winter, spring and autumn. In summer, at peak traffic, males used less habitat that was both visible from and close to primary roads. Females responded to primary roads in spring (calving) and autumn (mating) and **selected areas near roads closed to traffic** in spring and summer. Road covariates mattered most in the core of home ranges. — [Montgomery, Roloff & Millspaugh 2012/2013, JWM 77:313–325](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.462)

**Day vs night and season (Alberta)**
- SW Alberta (138 migration events, 2007–2010): "All elk selected greater forest cover, reduced movement rates, and avoided roads during fall-day than in any other season or time of day". Road avoidance was weakest on spring nights, with no hunting and the fewest people. The only time elk were near high-traffic roads was spring nights. In fall both sexes moved closer to roads at night. Males avoided roads far more than females in fall days and were less likely to cross. **Vigilance was >3× higher in the fall hunting season.** Selection was modelled for traffic from 1 vehicle per 15 min to 1 per 8 h; avoidance was greater along high-traffic roads than along secondary forest roads. — [Paton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1841)
- Alberta winter ranges (175 elk-years, integrated step selection): elk selected areas farther from roads at all times of day, most strongly at twilight. They sought cover and moved more near roads. Road crossings were avoided, least so in daytime. — [Prokopenko, Boyce & Avgar 2017, J. Appl. Ecol.](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2664.12768)
- SW Alberta: road traffic of at least 1 vehicle every 2 hours switched elk into a more vigilant mode with less feeding. Human factors (land use, traffic, distance from roads) plus herd size explained >80% of the variation in vigilance. Vigilance was highest on public lands with both hunting and motorized recreation. — [Ciuti et al. 2012, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0050611)

### Inferences
- **Scoring rule (distance to open motorized route):** use a rising ramp rather than a hard buffer. A sensible default is Rowland's linear 0.17 → 1.0 ramp over 0–1,800 m outside hunting season. In hunting season, stretch the ramp so full security is reached at about 2.0–2.8 km in archery and at least 1.5 km in rifle (Ranglack), with the Elkhorns showing half of elk use beyond about 3.5 km. Johnson's Starkey regression (+83 m per hunter/km²) suggests scaling the ramp with expected hunter density.
- **Closed roads with no traffic are close to neutral for elk** (Witmer; Montgomery; Starkey threshold). The pressure they carry comes from people on foot (see Q5).
- **Night relaxes road avoidance**, except under heavy rifle pressure (Johnson 2004). An app showing "where elk are now" should loosen road penalties at night outside hunting season. During rifle season keep most of the penalty at night, with a lower weight for dawn travel corridors.
- Bulls avoid roads more than cows (Marcum & Edge; Paton). A bull-hunting mode should weight road distance more heavily.

### Gaps
- Exact selection-ratio slopes from Rowland et al. 2000 (paywalled) were not read. The 1,800 m figure and band weights come from the 2004 restatement.
- Rowland 2000 and Wisdom 2004b data are spring/summer (non-hunting). Hunting-season distance curves come mainly from Montana (Ranglack, Lowrey, Proffitt) and Starkey hunts (Johnson, Spitz).
- Lyon 1983's percentages come from a secondary snippet, not the original article.
- The Spitz et al. 2019 full text (durations and effect sizes) could not be fetched (HTTP 403).

## 2. Response to hunting seasons: refuges, private land, opening day, movement rates, steep/dense terrain, night shift

### Takeaway
Elk respond to hunting season fast and strongly. At Starkey their movement rate rises about 1.4 m/min per rifle hunter/km² and 2.2 m/min per archery hunter/km². They go to refuges (private land closed to hunting, parks, no-hunting zones) and stay there. The odds of refuge use more than double, and an amenity refuge raised bull selection 545%. They also shift to forest, steeper and more rugged terrain, and more nocturnal activity. Archery season opening day sets the timing of moves to private land.

### Cited Findings
**Movement rate vs hunter density (Starkey, Oregon; 13 rifle and 2 archery elk hunts, 1991–2000; 25–60 collared elk per hunt)**
- Rifle hunts averaged 0.91 hunters/km² (range 0.03–1.66), archery 0.26 hunters/km², deer rifle hunts 0.04/km². **Deer hunts at 0.04 hunters/km² caused no measurable elk response.** — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- Elk daily mean velocity rose linearly with rifle hunter density: about 1.4 m/min per hunter/km² (R² = 0.46), from 2.7 m/min before the hunt to about 5 m/min at the highest densities. In archery the rise was **2.2 m/min per hunter/km²** (archery hunters disturb elk more per head). — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- At <0.3 hunters/km² (<0.8/mi²), crepuscular peaks widened by about 1 m/min and midday returned to pre-hunt levels. At >1.25 hunters/km² (>3.2/mi²), velocity stayed high all day, peaking about 6 m/min above pre-hunt at 08:00. Only 00:00–04:00 returned to near pre-hunt. — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- Hunter density predicted elk disturbance better than traffic counts (traffic vs velocity R² = 0.28), especially in archery. At average density (0.91/km²) elk velocity rose 1.3 m/min, a 4–10% increase in the daily energy budget. "Elk responded by fleeing disturbance; whereas, deer eluded hunters by hiding." — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- Missoula WUI, Montana (9 GPS females, 2007–2009): movement rates rose with hunting pressure and were lower in general than in focal (intensive) seasons, and lower with archery than with rifle. — [Cleveland et al. 2012, Wildl. Soc. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1002/wsb.182)
- Greater Yellowstone winter range: elk changed grouping and increased movement as predation risk rose. Responses to human hunters were stronger than responses to wolves. — [Proffitt et al. 2009, JWM 73:345–356](https://bioone.org/journals/Journal-of-Wildlife-Management/volume-73/issue-3/2008-210/Contrasting-Effects-of-Wolves-and-Human-Hunters-on-Elk-Behavioral/10.2193/2008-210.full)

**Individual behaviour and who gets shot (SW Alberta)**
- 45 two-year-old males (15 harvested) and 77 females (10 harvested). In the hunting season, harvested males moved 321.7 ± 9.9 m per 2 h vs 269.4 ± 4.4 m for survivors; females 304.2 ± 8.4 vs 242.0 ± 2.2 m. Within 1 km of roads, harvested animals moved about 55–58 m more per 2 h. Surviving males avoided open areas (selection ratio 0.65 vs 0.82 for harvested). Surviving females avoided open areas (0.87) while harvested females selected them (1.13). Hunters encountered elk most "close to roads, flatter terrain, during the weekend". **Humans counted per day: 20.7 on weekends vs 12.4 on weekdays.** 93% of hunting deaths fell between early September and early November. — [Ciuti et al. 2012, Proc. R. Soc. B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479801)
- 49 females, 2007–2012, 46,000 km² of SW Alberta and SE BC. With age, females moved less and used more rugged terrain, especially near roads at dawn and dusk. They used more forest when close to roads, and the age effect was stronger in bow season. Behaviour differed inside vs beyond **500 m from roads**. Females became "almost invulnerable" to hunters after 9–10 years of age. Effective ranges are ≤300 m for rifle and <40 m for bow. About 20% of females were killed by hunters vs about 5% by natural predators. — [Thurfjell, Ciuti & Boyce 2017, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5470680/)
- In the Castle-Carbondale fall migration (hunting season), **females used steeper terrain during fall days; males did not.** Males avoided roads more strongly. About 50% of males vs <20% of females were shot over the long-term monitoring. The male season is 110 days (archery and rifle, early September to December) and the female season 51 days. — [Paton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1841)

**Refuges and private land (Montana, Greater Yellowstone)**
- Madison Valley late-season hunt (GPS females): during the hunt elk shifted to privately owned refuges and "the estimated odds of elk occupying refuge areas more than doubled". — [Proffitt et al. 2010, JWM 74:210–218](https://doi.org/10.2193/2008-593); also [Proffitt & Garrott 2009, IJS 15(4)](https://arc.lib.montana.edu/ojs/index.php/IJS/article/view/2060)
- 82 females in 2 GYE herds: selection for areas restricting public hunting was stronger than selection for Hillis security habitat in both areas. The jump came in the **rifle** period, with no consistent trigger from archery. — [Proffitt et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.491)
- 325 females, 9 SW Montana populations: in both archery and rifle seasons the **strongest** covariate was "areas that restricted access to public hunters". In archery that was followed by NDVI, canopy, distance from motorized routes and lower hunter effort; in rifle by distance from motorized routes, canopy and higher hunter effort. — [Ranglack et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21258)
- Missouri River Breaks vs Larb Hills (46 females, 2 years). MRB was 97% accessible to hunters, Larb Hills 79%. Elk selected no-access areas at population and individual levels, more strongly in archery than rifle. The share of individuals selecting no-access was 76% (Larb) vs 43% (MRB) in archery and 60% vs 18% in rifle (IJS version). The JWM version gives 46% and 24% for MRB. Yet 68% (archery) and 91% (rifle) of MRB locations were on accessible land: "even small refuge areas can significantly affect overall elk distribution". — [Thompson et al. 2016, IJS 22(4)](https://arc.lib.montana.edu/ojs/index.php/IJS/article/view/743); [Proffitt et al. 2016, JWM 80:1167–1176](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21122)
- Sapphire Mountains, Montana: an amenity refuge expansion closed 31 km² to hunters. **The odds of bull selection of the refuge were 545% higher (95% CI 397–738%) in years 3–4 after expansion.** Closing 41 km of motorcycle routes and 4 km of vehicle routes on USFS land did not hold elk on public ground: the odds of males selecting public hunting grounds fell a further 48% (37–57%). "Amenity refuges can rapidly alter elk distribution, while public land route closures may not." — [Proffitt et al. 2025, Wildlife Biology](https://doi.org/10.1002/wlb3.01509)
- Devil's Kitchen, central Montana (58 females; 2026 JWM). Elk moved to less accessible private land in the early shoulder and archery seasons. Restrictive regulations on open-access public land in the general season pulled elk back onto that public land. — [Bealer et al. 2026, JWM](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.70170)
- The same project's daily transition probabilities (2023 interim report, periods: early shoulder Aug 15–Sep 3, archery Sep 4–Oct 17, general firearm Oct 23–Nov 28, late shoulder Nov 29–Feb 15). In the general rifle period, elk on open-access land had an 81.51% chance of staying there. Moves were restricted→controlled 18.05%, controlled→restricted 9.61%, restricted→open 5.59%, controlled→open 9.80%. The chance of staying in restricted access was 76.37% (the lowest of any period). In the late shoulder season, open→restricted was 28.97% and restricted→restricted 92.52%. Moves between access classes were rare in archery and early shoulder. — [Montana FWP W-179-R interim report, Dec 2023](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/1---elk-habitat-management-in-mt-2023-annual-report.pdf)
- During archery (Aug–Oct), females whose home ranges were more accessible to hunters selected areas farther from motorized routes. Elk kept or increased selection for high forage quality whatever the risk. Low-risk home ranges had the best nutrition, often irrigated private fields closed to hunting. — [DeVoe et al. 2019, JWM 83:801–816](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21638)
- Elk "sometimes move much longer distances (e.g., > 25 km) from public to private lands during hunting seasons." — [Wisdom et al. 2018](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220)

**Opening day and timing (NW Colorado experiments; 80 radio-collared females captured July 1996)**
- Opening archery season 21 days later (13 Sep vs 23 Aug) delayed elk moves onto private land by 10 days overall (P = 0.013). The delay was 14 days in the north area and 6 days in the south. About twice as many collared elk moved to private land in the north (44%) as in the south (23%), a difference credited to topography and migration. — [Conner, White & Freddy 2001, JWM](https://doi.org/10.2307/3803041)
- **Cutting archery licences about 50% did not change elk movement to private land.** The mean date of movement (95% CI) overlapped archery opening day in 7 of 8 cases. Delaying opening day did more than reducing hunter numbers. — [Vieira et al. 2003, JWM](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802678)

**Night shift and refuge commuting (Alberta agricultural matrix)**
- Wainwright Dunes Ecological Reserve (a hunting refuge) in east-central Alberta, studied with cameras. With the onset of hunting, elk became more nocturnal, leaving the refuge after sunset and returning earlier before sunrise. They were more nocturnal under brighter moonlight, and cow–calf pairs were the most nocturnal. — [Visscher et al. 2017, Wildl. Soc. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1002/wsb.741)
- SW Manitoba (67 collared elk; 796 hunter kills, 2002–2012): **distance to protected area was the most important covariate for both elk selection and kill sites (AICw = 1.00).** Collared adult males rarely used sites outside parks during the hunting season. Kill sites were associated with lower road density. — [Dugal et al. 2013, Ecology and Evolution](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.788)

**Mortality and vulnerability vs roads (bulls)**
- North-central Idaho (121 radio-monitored elk, 1986–91): 86% of deaths occurred in September and October and were associated with hunting. Annual survival was 0.886 for cows and 0.600 for bulls. — [Unsworth et al. 1993, JWM](https://doi.org/10.2307/3809273)
- Bull survival "more than doubled" with open road density <0.5 vs >4 mi per section (Unsworth & Kuck 1991). Bull mortality was 62%, 45% and 31% at 4.5, 2.6 and 1.0 mi of open road per section (Leptich & Zager 1991). Assuming average topography and about 10 hunter-days per section over a 26-day season, bull mortality probability is 60% higher with 1 mi/section of road than with none, and more than doubles at 2 mi/section. "At higher road densities bulls usually do not survive the hunting season." — [Christensen et al. 1993, INT-GTR-303](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf)
- South-central Washington Cascades (78 elk, 1992–99): survival 0.65 for males and 0.83 for females. Harvest risk was best explained by road density plus topographic complexity (aspect diversity), with the managed-forest share as a proxy for roading. — [McCorquodale et al. 2003, JWM](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802766)
- Coeur d'Alene Mountains, Idaho: male mortality rose with **total** (open + closed) road density and with seasons during the rut. "Road closures alone may not always reduce the risk of mortality." — [Hayes, Leptich & Zager 2002, JWM](https://doi.org/10.2307/3803182)

### Inferences
- **Refuge is the single strongest hunting-season attractor** where it exists (Proffitt 2013, 2016, 2025; Ranglack 2017; Dugal 2013). A pressure layer without a "no-hunting / no-access" class will misplace elk in mixed-ownership country.
- **Two clocks run in the season.** Archery opening (late August or September) triggers moves to refuges (Conner, Vieira). The general rifle opener then brings the largest redistribution and movement spikes (Proffitt 2013; Johnson 2004; Montana transitions). Per hunter, archers are more disruptive (2.2 vs 1.4 m/min per hunter/km²).
- **Day vs night:** pressure effects are mainly daytime (Paton, Spitz, Visscher). In heavy rifle pressure (>1.25 hunters/km²) movement stays elevated except 00:00–04:00 (Johnson). A "now" model should move elk toward feeding areas at night and back to cover or refuge before sunrise, and weight weekends about 1.7× weekdays (20.7 vs 12.4 people/day; Ciuti).
- **Low hunter densities barely register.** About 0.04 hunters/km² caused no response at Starkey. Pressure is roughly proportional to hunter density, so a "remote" cell with few hunters may need no penalty at all.

### Gaps
- Proffitt et al. 2009 (wolves vs hunters) numbers are paywalled; only the abstract was read.
- Millspaugh et al. 2000 (elk and hunter space-use sharing, South Dakota) had no accessible abstract.
- Quantified "opening-day dispersal distance" in km (as opposed to timing and proportion) was not found, apart from Wisdom's ">25 km public→private" remark.
- Spitz et al. 2019 durations (how many days speeds stayed elevated) were not readable.

## 3. Disturbance flight responses (Starkey experiments: ATV, mountain bike, hiker, horseback)

### Takeaway
At Starkey, elk kept 558–879 m from recreationists in real time and about 240–310 m from the trails themselves. ATVs were the most disruptive, then mountain bikes, then hikers and horses. Flight probability was still clearly above baseline at 1 km from ATVs and bikes, and most elk stayed beyond the roughly 300 m from which people could see them. Elk returned toward trails within the 9-day quiet periods.

### Cited Findings
- **Design:** 1,453 ha enclosure (Northeast Study Area, Starkey) with 32 km of trails. One activity was run twice daily for 5 days, then 9 control days with no people, repeated 3 times per year per activity, spring to fall 2003–2004 (pilot 2002). 35 of 123 elk carried telemetry. — [Wisdom et al. 2018](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220); [PNW Science Findings 219 (2019)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/58484)
- **Minimum separation from people:** mean minimum separation from recreationists was 558–879 m across the four activities. It was highest for ATV riding, intermediate for mountain biking, and lowest and similar for hiking and horseback. That is 2–4× the distance elk kept from the trails and 3–5× the mean distances at which elk could be seen from trails (n = 19 LORAN-C elk). — [Wisdom et al. 2018](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220)
- **Distance from trails** during recreation: 239–310 m mean. — [Wisdom et al. 2018](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220)
- **Viewing distance:** mean near and far viewing distances from trails were 172 and 222 m, maximum 300 m. 85% of the study area was within 300 m of a trail, yet only 56% of elk locations were. **During recreation, 44% of elk locations were in the 15% of the area beyond 300 m (52% during ATV riding, 50% during mountain biking).** Over 75% of minimum separation distances exceeded 300 m. Elk shifted back toward trails in control periods. — [Wisdom et al. 2018](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/56220)
- Separation distances of 1,830–2,880 ft (558–878 m) from people vs 780–1,020 ft (238–311 m) from trails. Flight responses were seen at >1,000 m for ATVs, "close to that" for mountain bikes, and 500–750 m for horseback riding and hiking. Thinning that opened sight lines increased the distances elk kept. — [PNW Science Findings 219](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/58484)
- **Flight probability vs distance** (Wisdom et al. 2004a, 2002 data; flight means hourly speed above the 95th percentile of control-period speeds, so the null probability is 0.05):

  | Distance from people | ATV | Mountain bike | Horse |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 100 m | 0.62 (0.52–0.73) | 0.58 (0.46–0.68) | 0.50 (0.40–0.59) |
  | 500 m | 0.43 (0.36–0.49) | 0.31 (0.26–0.35) | 0.22 (0.19–0.26) |
  | 1,000 m | 0.25 (0.20–0.30) | 0.13 (0.10–0.16) | 0.07 (0.05–0.08) |
  | all distances | 0.19 | 0.14 | 0.11 |

  Hiking peaked at about 0.65 close-in but had "little effect when hikers were beyond 550 yards (500 m)". Raised flight probability continued beyond 750 m for horses and 1,500 m for bikes and ATVs. Mule deer barely responded (≤0.11 at 100 m). — [Wisdom et al. 2004a](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24836)
- **Movement rates:** peak morning elk speed was 19 m/min under ATVs, 16 under bikes and about 14 under horses and hikers. In the afternoon it was 12 (ATV), 10 (horse) and about 9 (hike and bike). Speeds were raised mostly around sunrise and sunset. — [Wisdom et al. 2004a](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24836)
- **Activity budget:** 13 females with motion-sensor collars (April–October 2003–2004). Travel time rose under all four activities, most under ATVs, then bikes, hikers and horses. Feeding fell under ATVs, and resting fell under biking and hiking (2003). — [Naylor, Wisdom & Anthony 2009, JWM 73:328–338](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70035402)
- A Starkey hunter survey (n = 416, 6 years) found that whether hunters used an ATV was unrelated to their success. Successful hunters spent 87.8% of effort on foot (vs 84.6% for unsuccessful hunters), and scouting raised the odds of success 418% for rifle elk hunters. — [Rowland et al. 2022, Wildl. Soc. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1002/wsb.1414)

### Inferences
- **Per-source distance-decay kernels for the pressure layer** (fit a logistic to the Wisdom 2004a table): ATV ≈ 0.43 at 500 m and 0.25 at 1 km; bike ≈ 0.31 and 0.13; horse ≈ 0.22 and 0.07; hiker ≈ near baseline beyond 500 m. Elk-on-foot pressure therefore reaches about 0.5 km, and motorized-trail pressure 1.5 km or more.
- Because elk keep out of sight (beyond about 300 m in open dry forest), a **viewshed term** from roads and trails, weighted by canopy and terrain screening, adds information that distance alone misses (also Montgomery 2012 on "visibility from roads").
- Recovery: elk "shifted distribution back toward trails" during the 9-day controls. Disturbance is reversible within days, so a pressure layer can be time-varying (weekday vs weekend, pre-season vs opener).

### Gaps
- The hiking column of the 2004a flight table did not parse from the scanned PDF; only a qualitative "little effect beyond 500 m" is sourced.
- Exact hours or days for elk to return after a disturbance pass were not found in the text read (only "control periods"). The Starkey trials ran in spring to fall, with hunted elk but not during hunts.

## 4. Terrain and cover that predict refuge; slope, canopy, ruggedness, distance from access; walk-in-time models

### Takeaway
Refuge is predicted, in order of strength, by: (1) land closed to public hunting; (2) distance from motorized routes (about 1.5–3.5 km in hunting season); (3) canopy cover (thresholds from about 9–13% to about 31–53% depending on the forest); (4) terrain ruggedness and slope (always positive, but no published numeric slope threshold was found); (5) being out of sight. Hunters cluster near roads and on gentler slopes: about 267 m from roads on average, a mean maximum of 0.84 km from open roads (deer hunters, PA), and half going beyond 2.5 km from their start. A walk-time (travel-cost) surface is therefore a sound pressure proxy, though no published elk model built exactly that way was found.

### Cited Findings
**Cover and distance thresholds**
- Archery: ≥13% canopy and ≥2,760 m from motorized routes, plus high-nutrition patches inside. Rifle: ≥9% canopy, ≥1,535 m and ≥20.23 km² (SW Montana; 325 females). — [Ranglack et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21258)
- Elkhorns: 75% of use at ≥31% canopy and ≥2,072 m; 50% of use at ≥53% canopy and ≥3,496 m. Slope and ruggedness were positive. Beetle kill cut canopy only 8.5% in infested lodgepole and did not reduce security. — [Lowrey et al. 2019/2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21781)
- Hillis: ≥250 acres (≈101 ha), nonlinear, >0.5 mi (0.8 km) from open roads, ≥30% of the unit. "Recognition that summer cover blocks are also fall hunting season security areas is an important coordinating consideration." Northern Idaho: "Cover is so ubiquitous that security can be controlled with road management alone." Unsworth et al. (1993) model bull mortality from open road density, the circular standard deviation of aspect (topographic roughness) and hunter density. — [Christensen et al. 1993](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf)
- Montana FWP now treats security as varying between archery and rifle seasons and has built a statewide road layer for it. Recent work found elk "in addition to traditional security habitat (far from roads and timbered)" also use land that restricts hunter access or private land not in access programs. — [Montana FWP 2023 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/1---elk-habitat-management-in-mt-2023-annual-report.pdf)
- Topography reduces road effects (Edge & Marcum 1991). Avoidance varies with cover next to roads (Perry & Overly 1977; Lyon 1979). Rowland suggests raising band weights where "topographic barriers to human disturbance" exist. — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- Lyon 1979 (8-year pellet counts, Montana): the area avoided next to open roads "increases where the density of tree cover is low". — [BHA literature review](https://www.backcountryhunters.org/media/literature-review-the-impact-of-roads-on-elk)
- Topographic complexity (aspect diversity) raised survival apart from road effects (Washington Cascades). — [McCorquodale et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802766)
- Older females used more rugged terrain and forest when near roads. Harvested elk used open and flatter areas. — [Thurfjell et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5470680/); [Ciuti et al. 2012 Proc B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479801)
- Starkey archery hunts: elk use of canopy cover *decreased* as hunter density rose (canopy % = −7.3 × hunters/km² + 38.5, r² = 0.18). Cover is not always the refuge under bow pressure (archers hunt the timber). — [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)

**Hunter behaviour: where pressure actually is (the basis for a walk-in model)**
- Montana elk hunters in a road-closure area (Blackfoot BMA, about 1,000 km²; 99 GPS-tracked hunts, 1993–95, 15 s fixes): hunts averaged 4.7 h at 40 m/min over 10.7 km. **Half of hunters went more than 2.5 km from their start and only 12.5% more than 4.5 km.** The mean maximum distance from start was 2.6 km. **Average distance from the nearest road while hunting was only 267 m, and hunters on foot spent 26% of hunting time on roads.** 60% of hunting was on slopes gentler than the area's average of 22% (one-third of the area was >30% slope). "Closed roads have a significant impact on hunter behavior … closed roads facilitate access to areas most distant from open roads and trailheads." Hunters spent 9% of their time sitting or standing. — [Lyon & Burcham 1998, RMRS-RP-3](https://doi.org/10.2737/rmrs-rp-3)
- Pennsylvania public land (45,749 ha, white-tailed deer season, GPS-tracked hunters): hunter density was **negatively related to distance from roads and to slope**. The mean *maximum* distance from a road open to public vehicles was 0.84 km. Hunters walked 5.48 km per day. Hunters *self-reported* 2.23 km, 2.5× the true figure. Density was 7 hunters/1,000 ha in the morning, and 72% of hunters were stationary at 06:00–08:00. — [Stedman et al. 2004, JWM 68:762–773](https://doi.org/10.2193/0022-541x(2004)068[0762:iwahrm]2.0.co;2)
- Oklahoma deer hunters (83 hunters, 487 GPS hunts): 3.7 h afield, 2,085 m travelled, and they hunted >50 m from roads in 25–50% forest cover and rougher terrain. — [Meier et al. 2025, Wildlife Research](https://doi.org/10.1071/wr24114)
- Ranglack et al. used "hunter effort" (FWP harvest-survey hunter-days) as a covariate. The Montana FWP overabundance analysis expresses effort as hunter-days per km² of elk habitat per hunting district (2004–2020 average). — [Ranglack et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21258); [Montana FWP 2023 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/1---elk-habitat-management-in-mt-2023-annual-report.pdf)
- DeVoe et al. used the share of a home range accessible to hunters as the hunting-risk metric. — [DeVoe et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21638)

**Tools for a travel-cost surface**
- Tobler's hiking function: walking speed W = 6·e^(−3.5·|dh/dx + 0.05|) km/h. That gives 5 km/h on the flat and a peak of 6 km/h at about −2.86° (slight downhill). Off-path travel is multiplied by 3/5 (about 3 km/h on the flat). — [Tobler's hiking function (Tobler 1993)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobler%27s_hiking_function)
- "Roadless volume", a national metric derived from distance to the nearest road (USGS), is a prior example of a distance-from-access surface. — [Watts et al. 2007, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1138141)

### Inferences
- **Walk-in pressure model (proposed, unvalidated):** compute walking time from every access point (open-road cells, trailheads, parking, gates or closed-road entries, campgrounds) over a 10 m DEM with Tobler speeds: on-trail and closed road ×1.0, off-path ×0.6, plus a penalty for dense regen, deadfall and water. Map hunter density as a decaying function of walk time, calibrated so that about 50% of hunters reach about 2.5 km from start and 12.5% reach >4.5 km (Lyon & Burcham). At about 4–5 km/h on closed roads and trails, that puts half of pressure inside about 30–40 min. Off-trail at 3 km/h it is about 50 min. Add slope aversion: 60% of hunting is on below-average slopes, and density falls with slope (Stedman).
- **Elk security score** (hunting season), roughly multiplicative: refuge/no-access (strongest) × f(distance to motorized route; ramp to about 1.5 km rifle, 2.8 km archery, 3.5 km very high pressure) × g(canopy; 0 at <9–13%, saturating about 50%) × h(ruggedness and slope; positive) × (1 − visibility from roads and trails within 300 m) × (1 − foot-hunter density from the walk-time model).
- Because archers hunt timber and elk then *reduce* canopy use (Johnson 2004), the canopy term should be weaker in archery than in rifle season, with nutrition (green forage) counting more in archery (Ranglack).
- **Steep-terrain refuge:** slope and ruggedness are consistently positive, but no numeric slope cut-off was found. Lyon & Burcham's hunters avoided slopes above the 22% average, which suggests "above about 25–30%" as a provisional starting point to field-check.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed elk study was found that builds hunter pressure from a travel-time or least-cost surface. Web search budget ran out before the moose, deer and European literature (e.g., Brøseth & Pedersen 2000; Lebel et al. 2012) could be checked.
- No published numeric slope threshold for elk hunting-season refuge was found.
- The Ranglack 2017 full text (how hunter effort was spatialised; whether slope was tested) could not be downloaded (server reset). Only the abstract was used.

## 5. How to build the pressure layer from open data (US and Canada); handling gated/closed roads when status is missing

### Takeaway
In the US, the USFS Motor Vehicle Use Map (MVUM) is the legal "open to motor vehicles" layer: absent from MVUM means closed to motor vehicles. Each vehicle class carries seasonal open dates. RoadCore maintenance level 1 = closed. PAD-US gives land ownership and access, but access is assigned categorically and needs care. OSM `highway=track` plus `tracktype`, `access` and `motor_vehicle` tags fill gaps on private and provincial land. In Canada, Alberta's ABMI Human Footprint Inventory covers seismic lines, roads and trails annually. When status is missing, treat roads as open during hunting season (closures often fail) and treat every closed road as a foot-hunter corridor.

### Cited Findings
**USFS layers**
- The MVUM roads dataset holds National Forest System roads "designated as open to motorized vehicles" under the Travel Management Rule (169,353 segments). "Routes not designated for motor vehicle use (such as non-motorized trails, single-purpose roads and trails, unauthorized roads and trails, and temporary roads and trails) are not included." Fields per vehicle class: PASSENGERVEHICLE, HIGHCLEARANCEVEHICLE, TRUCK, BUS, MOTORHOME, FOURWD_GT50INCHES, TWOWD_GT50INCHES, ATV (≤50"), MOTORCYCLE, TRACKED_OHV, OTHERWHEELED_OHV, E_BIKE_CLASS1–3. SEASONAL is yearlong or seasonal, and *_DATESOPEN gives MM/DD–MM/DD ranges. Updated "as needed" per unit. — [USFS EDW metadata, S_USA.Road_MVUM](https://data.fs.usda.gov/geodata/edw/edw_resources/meta/S_USA.Road_MVUM.xml)
- RoadCore OPER_MAINT_LEVEL: 1 = "Basic Custodial Care (Closed)", meaning roads "placed in storage (greater than one year) between intermittent uses". 2 = High Clearance Vehicles. 3 = Suitable for Passenger Cars. 4 = Moderate and 5 = High degree of user comfort. OPENFORUSETO: "All" = open for public motorized travel for at least part of the year; "Admin" = administrative only, "not open for public motorized use". — [USFS EDW metadata, S_USA.RoadCore_FS](https://data.fs.usda.gov/geodata/edw/edw_resources/meta/S_USA.RoadCore_FS.xml)
- The Forest Service Travel Management Rule (2005) requires designated roads, trails and areas for motor vehicles and prohibits use outside them. — [PNW Science Findings 219](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/58484)

**Land ownership and access**
- PAD-US (current version 4.1, 2024, doi 10.5066/P96WBCHS) is the national inventory of public and protected lands. Public access is "fully attributed but primarily assigned categorically instead of through individual unit review". Authoritative agency data and field checks should verify access. — [USGS PAD-US overview](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/gap-analysis-project/science/pad-us-data-overview)
- Montana FWP classes hunter access by parcel into open (public land, or private Block Management not needing reservations), controlled (landowner allows on request, or reservation BMA) and restricted. Landlocked public parcels take the class of the surrounding private land. This was built from manager and landowner contacts, not from a public dataset. — [Montana FWP 2023 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/1---elk-habitat-management-in-mt-2023-annual-report.pdf)

**OSM tagging**
- `tracktype` grade1 is solid (usually paved), grade2 mostly gravel, grade3 an even hard/soft mix, grade4 mostly soft (soil/sand/grass), grade5 soft and unimproved. About 96% of use is on `highway=track`. — [OSM wiki Key:tracktype](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Key:tracktype)
- `access` values: yes, no, private, permissive, destination, designated, forestry, agricultural and unknown ("explicitly indicating uncertainty"). Mode-specific keys (`motor_vehicle`, `motorcar`, `motorcycle`, `atv`, `foot`, `horse`, `bicycle`) override `access`. Defaults come from the element type when untagged. — [OSM wiki Key:access](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Key:access)

**Canada**
- The ABMI Human Footprint Inventory (Alberta) dates back to 2000 and is "now updated annually". It has "117 feature types and 20 individual sublayers including seismic lines, harvest areas, roads, and pipelines". It covers land reset by industry (cutblocks, seismic lines) but notes that "grazing, hunting, and trapping, are not yet accounted for". — [ABMI Human Footprint Mapping](https://abmi.ca/abmi-home/what-we-do/land-cover-and-land-use-mapping/human-footprint-mapping.html)
- In SW Alberta, 95% of motorized traffic on public land (camera data) was recreational: 80% cars, trucks and RVs, 15% ATVs and motorbikes, and 5% industrial. Traffic for the whole network was modelled from 52 counters and 21 cameras (Northrup et al. 2012; Ciuti et al. 2012). Paton et al. suggest gating roads within 500–1,000 m of important stopovers. — [Paton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1841)

**Closed or gated roads and missing status: evidence**
- More than half of 802 road closures inventoried on national forests in Idaho, Montana, Washington and Wyoming were ineffective, even after allowing for administrative use (Havlick 2002). In Idaho, elk mortality correlated with the density of both closed and open roads (Hayes et al. 2002). Roads "designated as closed but not promulgated … are often traveled by off-highway vehicles". The Dark Meadow analysis counted "closed roads that have no promulgation" as **open**. "Careful assessment of how roads are being used, rather than their official status, is important." — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf)
- Total (open plus closed) road density predicted male mortality, so closures alone may not cut risk. — [Hayes et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.2307/3803182)
- Hunters on foot use closed roads to reach the deepest country (26% of time on roads; the farthest-ranging hunters used closed roads most). — [Lyon & Burcham 1998](https://doi.org/10.2737/rmrs-rp-3)
- Elk did not avoid spur roads closed to vehicles (Oregon Coast Range), and females selected areas near closed roads (South Dakota). — [Witmer & deCalesta 1985](http://hdl.handle.net/2376/1842); [Montgomery et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.462)
- Closing 41 km of motorcycle routes and 4 km of vehicle routes did not keep elk on public land next to an amenity refuge. — [Proffitt et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/wlb3.01509)
- "Area closures with open routes designated will most likely provide better security than individual closures." — [Christensen et al. 1993](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf)

### Inferences
**Recipe (proposed; numbers are starting points to field-calibrate)**
1. **Access network, classed by mode.**
   - US: open motor routes come from MVUM, with a class (passenger, high-clearance or ATV/OHV only) and the season dates checked against the hunt date. RoadCore ML1 or "Admin" roads become *closed* (foot corridor). Fill non-NFS land from OSM: `highway` primary to unclassified = open public; `track` grade1–3 with no `access`/`motor_vehicle=no|private` and no `barrier=gate` = open; grade4–5 or untagged tracks = ATV-capable (open to ATV unless tagged otherwise); `access=private|no` or a gate on the way = closed (foot corridor only if it starts from public access).
   - Canada: provincial resource roads (Ontario LIO, BC, Quebec AQréseau, which Groundwind already uses) plus OSM. ABMI seismic lines and trails in Alberta are ATV and foot corridors.
2. **Unknown status rule:** during hunting season treat an untagged drivable road as **open**. Closures fail more than half the time (Havlick), closed-road density still predicts mortality (Hayes), and Rowland counted unpromulgated closures as open. Give "unknown" roads about 0.5–0.7 of the full vehicle weight if a softer rule is wanted. Always add the foot-hunter weight.
3. **Access points:** open-road ends, gates (start of closed roads), trailheads and parking (OSM `highway=trailhead`, `amenity=parking`, `tourism=camp_site`; USFS recreation sites), boat launches and campgrounds. These seed the walk-time model.
4. **Vehicle-disturbance term:** a distance ramp from each open route, using the Rowland bands (0.17→1.0 over 1.8 km) outside the season and stretched to about 1.5 km (rifle) or 2.8 km (archery) in season. Scale by traffic class: paved or high-traffic (>10 vehicles per 12 h) > gravel > high-clearance or ATV. The threshold of about 1 vehicle per 12 h (Starkey) or 1 per 2 h (Alberta vigilance) means any hunting-season road counts. Use the Wisdom flight kernels for ATV trails.
5. **Foot-hunter term:** density decays with Tobler walk time from access points, about 50% within roughly 30–50 min and about 12.5% beyond 4.5 km. Add slope aversion. Use hunter-days per km² from state or provincial harvest reports as the overall multiplier (Starkey: no response at 0.04 hunters/km², strong response above 1.25).
6. **Refuge term:** PAD-US land without public hunting (parks, refuges, closed military land) and private parcels as "restricted/unknown", unless enrolled in an access programme (e.g., Montana BMA). In Canada: provincial and national parks, no-hunting and firearm-discharge zones, and First Nations reserves. In mixed land, refuges act as the strongest attractor (odds ×2 or more; ×6.45 for a new amenity refuge).
7. **Visibility term:** a viewshed from roads and trails out to about 300 m, reduced by canopy (LiDAR canopy height or FRI), so that open ground within 300 m counts as exposed.
8. **Time modifiers:** opening days (archery and rifle) at the peak, then decaying through the season. Weekend ×1.7. Daylight full; night about 0.3 (except heavy rifle pressure, about 0.7, per Johnson 2004).

### Gaps
- PAD-US Pub_Access code definitions (open, restricted, closed, unknown) could not be fetched (USGS 403). Only the "assigned categorically" caveat is sourced.
- BC (Digital Road Atlas or FTEN resource roads), Ontario LIO road and gate attributes, and Quebec closure status were not verified in this pass.
- No public, wide-area US dataset of gate locations or actual closure status was found. Montana FWP's access classes were built by contacting managers and landowners.
- Trailhead and parking completeness in OSM vs USFS recreation-site data was not assessed.

## 6. Regional differences (Rocky Mountain West, Pacific NW, Alberta/BC, Prairie, eastern restored herds)

### Takeaway
The direction is the same everywhere (avoid roads, people and hunters; seek cover, rugged ground and refuges), but the driver changes. In Montana and Wyoming ranch country, private-land refuges dominate. In the Blue Mountains and Cascades, road traffic and density plus topographic complexity dominate. Coast Range Roosevelt elk show shorter avoidance distances (150–500 m). In Alberta, traffic, migration corridors and agricultural refuges matter, with strong day/night switching. Eastern restored herds (Kentucky, Ontario, South Dakota Black Hills) live closer to roads, and their hunting-season data are thin.

### Cited Findings
- **Montana (Rocky Mountain West, mixed ownership):** restricted hunter access is the strongest selection covariate in archery and rifle (325 females, 9 populations). Even on 97%-accessible prairie breaks, elk selected the small no-access fraction. — [Ranglack et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21258); [Proffitt et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.21122)
- **Montana (continuous conifer, public land):** security is defined by cover and distance from open roads (Hillis). In northern Idaho cover is so common that "security can be controlled with road management alone". — [Christensen et al. 1993](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_int/int_gtr303.pdf)
- **Missoula WUI (exurban):** elk select areas about 1,200 m from houses and trails, and movement is lower about 750 m from them. More intensive hunting reverses habituation. — [Cleveland et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/wsb.182)
- **Pacific NW, interior (Blue Mountains, Starkey):** a linear road-distance effect to about 1.8 km (non-hunting), a traffic threshold of about 1 vehicle per 12 h, and hunter-density-driven velocity. — [Rowland et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/24797.pdf); [Wisdom et al. 2004b](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24837); [Johnson et al. 2004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/24784)
- **Pacific NW, Coast Range (Roosevelt elk):** reduced use within 150 m of roads regardless of access, or at half the expected frequency within a 500 m band around paved roads. No avoidance of closed spur roads. — [BHA review (Cole et al. 2004)](https://www.backcountryhunters.org/media/literature-review-the-impact-of-roads-on-elk); [Witmer & deCalesta 1985](http://hdl.handle.net/2376/1842)
- **Washington Cascades:** harvest risk is driven by open road density plus topographic complexity, and 100% of subadult male deaths came from hunting. — [McCorquodale et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802766)
- **Idaho:** 86% of deaths in September and October, from hunting. Mortality rose with open road density, hunter density and low topographic roughness, and with closed roads too. — [Unsworth et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.2307/3809273); [Hayes et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.2307/3803182)
- **Alberta/BC (eastern slopes, foothills, agricultural edge):** fall-day road avoidance and vigilance >3× in hunting season; weekends with about 1.7× the people; a 500 m road threshold for female behaviour; selection for steep, rugged and forested ground with age; road density ≤0.5 km/km² best; nocturnal refuge commuting in farmland. — [Paton et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1841); [Ciuti et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479801); [Thurfjell et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5470680/); [Frair et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2664.2008.01526.x); [Visscher et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/wsb.741)
- **Manitoba (southwest; elk in and around parks):** parks are the refuge. Distance to protected area was the top covariate (AICw = 1.00), and adult bulls rarely left parks in the hunting season. — [Dugal et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.788)
- **Colorado (NW, mixed land):** archery opening sets the timing of moves to private land. Halving archery hunters had no effect, and 44% vs 23% of elk moved depending on topography and migration routes. — [Conner et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.2307/3803041); [Vieira et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3802678)
- **South Dakota (Black Hills, human-dominated):** responses depend on season, sex and road type. Elk use closed roads, and visibility from primary roads matters. — [Montgomery et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/jwmg.462)
- **Kentucky (herd reintroduced about a decade before the study; 237 radio-collared elk, 2011–2015):** harvest caused 85.2% of deaths. Annual survival was 0.67 for females and 0.57 for males. Limited-hunter-access zones created in 2013 did **not** change male survival; cutting permits was judged more effective. — [Slabach et al. 2018, Wildlife Biology](https://doi.org/10.2981/wlb.00459)
- **Ontario (Burwash, south of Sudbury; 41 collars, Dec 2011–Aug 2013):** elk selected open habitats at night and forest by day in all seasons. They avoided areas near roads in spring but selected them at night in winter. Survival was 0.71–0.83 across subpopulations, and human-caused mortality dominated only in Burwash. — [McGeachy 2014, MSc thesis, Laurentian](https://zone.biblio.laurentian.ca/dspace/handle/10219/2181)
- Burwash elk (25 females, 3 years) selected intolerant hardwoods (aspen, birch, balsam poplar) in all seasons and used white and red pine less than expected. The authors warn that results differ from western studies and call for population-specific habitat work. — [Popp, McGeachy & Hamr 2013, Int. J. For. Res.](https://doi.org/10.1155/2013/415913)
- Ontario elk were closer to a railway in winter, and train strikes clustered at bends and rose with snow depth. — [Popp et al. 2018, Can. J. Zool.](https://doi.org/10.1139/cjz-2017-0255)

### Inferences
- **Regional defaults for Groundwind:**
  - Western mixed-ownership areas: refuge and access class first, road distance second.
  - Western big-public-forest areas: road distance, cover and ruggedness first (Hillis or Ranglack style).
  - Coast or wet forest: shorter road buffers (about 150–500 m) but still strong.
  - Eastern restored herds near farms and towns: expect habituation and night use of roadside fields. Daytime retreat to forest and hunting-season nocturnality are likely (by analogy with Alberta farmland, Visscher; and McGeachy's day/night pattern). Road ramps should be shorter (about 0.5 km, as in Thurfjell's 500 m threshold) and day-only.
- Hunting seasons in eastern herds (Kentucky, Ontario, Pennsylvania, Michigan) are short, quota-limited draws with low hunter density. Starkey's "0.04 hunters/km², no response" suggests the pressure layer should switch on only where and when hunter density is meaningful. Recreation and roads may dominate there instead.

### Gaps
- No hunting-season movement studies were retrieved for Pennsylvania, Michigan, Arkansas, Wisconsin, Virginia, Tennessee or Ontario elk (search budget ran out). Ontario's elk-hunt structure (WMUs, dates, tag numbers) was not verified (ontario.ca page 404).
- Pacific NW Roosevelt elk *hunting-season* responses (as opposed to year-round road effects) were not found beyond McCorquodale 2003 (Cascades, Rocky Mountain elk).
- BC-specific elk pressure studies (Kootenay, Peace) were not retrieved beyond the Alberta–BC transboundary Thurfjell and Ciuti work.
