# Mountain slope and valley winds (thermals): timing, strength, scent, and how wind models handle them

Scope: slope (anabatic/katabatic) and along-valley thermal winds in mountain terrain, what overrides them, what they do to a hunter's scent, and how WindNinja and similar fast models handle them. The aim is to check and extend Groundwind's thermal model for elk terrain. Researched 2026-10-05.

Source notes: most numbers come from primary papers read in full text: Zardi & Whiteman's review chapter, Butler et al. 2015, Wagenbrenner et al. 2016 (the ACPD manuscript, version 4), Forthofer et al. 2009, Farina et al. 2023, Pypker et al. 2007, and WindNinja's source code. Where only an abstract was read, the finding says so. Hunting sources are labelled **[hunter belief]**. ## 1. Timing of the morning reversal (downslope to upslope) and the evening reversal, by aspect, steepness and shading

### Takeaway
The flip is set by when the sun reaches each patch of ground, not by sunrise or sunset. A sunlit east-facing slope can turn upslope within 30 minutes of sunrise. Valley floors, west faces, deep canyons and forested drainages stay downslope or calm for 2 to 5 hours after sunrise. In the evening, shaded slopes and forested drainages start draining in late afternoon, often 2 to 4 hours before sunset. Open, isolated slopes turn 0.5 to 1.5 hours after sunset.

### Cited Findings
- **Gentle east-facing slope (Granite Mountain, Utah, MATERHORN, 3-6° slopes, fall 2012 and spring 2013):** the morning transition starts when net radiation turns positive, "which usually occurs less than 30 min after sunrise". The tower on the valley floor at the foot of the slope "experiences the transition almost two hours later than all the other towers along the slope". — [Farina et al. 2023, JAMC 62](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Steep west-facing slope (Val Ferret, Swiss Alps, summer 2010, nine clear days):** "it took on average about 5 h after astronomical sunrise for downslope flows to be replaced by upslope/upvalley winds". A calm spell with 2 m wind speeds below 1 m/s began right after *local* sunrise and lasted 1 to 40 minutes. Subsidence warming was the main driver of inversion breakup in the middle of the valley (abstract only). — [Nadeau et al. 2020, Environ. Fluid Mech. 20:1199-1220](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/field-observations-of-the-morning-transition-over-a-steep-slope-i/)
- Farina et al. say the same Val Ferret study saw the transition *start* "approximately 40-50 min after sunrise". Read with Nadeau's abstract, this suggests it started within about an hour but took about 5 hours to complete on a west face. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Isolated butte (Big Southern Butte, Idaho; grass and sagebrush; about 3 m sensors; June-Sept 2010):** sunrise was 06:00-07:00. Upslope flow was "fully established by 10:00", peaked around 11:00, and gave way to an afternoon convectively mixed regime at 12:00-13:00. Sunset was 20:30-21:30. Flow turned downslope at 21:00-22:00, was "fully established by 23:00" and lasted until about 08:00, peaking around 00:00. — [Butler et al. 2015, ACP 15:3785](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- **Steep river canyon (Salmon River Canyon, Idaho; 550-1600 m; July-Sept 2011):** upslope started around 09:00. The switch from down-valley to up-valley began near the surface and reached 100 m above ground by 09:30. Flow turned downslope at 20:00-21:00 (about sunset or a little before), was fully established by 22:00, and lasted until about 07:00. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- **Steep forested watershed (Oregon Cascades, 20-33° slopes, 25-28 m Douglas-fir):** "If cold air drainage was not already occurring, it usually began below the canopy between 16:00 and 18:00 h". It began once "the watershed was no longer receiving direct solar radiation", which is hours before sunset in summer. In the morning the air stayed well mixed for about 2 hours after sunrise. Drainage "ceased or was confined below the canopy" only after the base of the watershed got direct sun, at 07:00-08:00. — [Pypker et al. 2007, Agric. For. Meteorol. 145:149](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Evening, general:** the surface energy budget reverses "initially in late afternoon in areas of the valley that are shadowed by terrain". Downslope flows start once the heat flux reverses on the sidewalls or in shadowed parts of the valley. "In clear undisturbed conditions the nighttime phase starts sometime during the period from several hours after sunset until about midnight." Patterns where upper slopes go into shadow first while lower slopes are still sunlit "appear to be atypical". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013, ch. 2 of *Mountain Weather Research and Forecasting*](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Slope heating depends "especially near sunrise and sunset, on whether shadows are cast on the slope from surrounding topography". Mesoscale models have only recently added topographic slope shadowing. The chapter's Meteor Crater figure (15 October) shows shadows sweeping across sidewalls of different aspect at different hours. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Slope flows are "often strongest in the early evening when the ambient stability is weak". "The bulk of the total nighttime cooling occurs in the first few hours following sunset" (De Wekker & Whiteman 2006). — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Downslope flow is "strongest at or just following sunset". A deepening cold pool later sets up an adverse pressure gradient and limits buoyancy. — [Steenburgh, Univ. of Utah lecture, Thermally Driven Flows](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~steenburgh/classes/6250.fall17/lectures/ThermallyDrivenFlows.pdf)
- Upslope flows peak before local solar noon on small mountains and after it on large ranges (Banta & Cotton 1982; Geerts et al. 2008; McNider & Pielke 1981, as summarised). — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)

### Inferences
- A useful rule: the morning switch at a point happens soon after the sun hits that ground, from under 30 minutes (open, gentle, east-facing) to about an hour. The *time* the sun hits varies by hours with aspect, horizon shading and position in the valley. East faces and upper slopes go first. Valley floors go about 2 hours later than slopes on the same day. West-facing sidewalls of narrow valleys can take about 5 hours. Under tall dense canopy the switch may never happen below the canopy.
- Groundwind check: drainage strength in `model.ts` comes from a single *global* sun-elevation ramp (`cool` is 1 below 0°, falls linearly to 0 at 10°). It does not use each cell's own illumination. Upslope already uses each cell's incidence angle (`cosInc`) but has no horizon shadowing, and the `sunElev > 3` gate is global. In steep terrain this will (a) end drainage too early on shaded west and north faces and valley floors in the morning, and (b) start it too late (only below 10° sun) on slopes shaded in late afternoon. The fix is to compute local illumination per cell (cast shadows from the DEM, as WindNinja does) and drive both onset and decay from it, with a 0-60 minute lag after local sunrise and local sunset.
- The valley floor or cold-pool term should outlast the slopes by about 1.5 to 3 hours after local sunrise (see section 2).

### Gaps
- No study found that measured morning-transition timing by aspect (N/E/S/W) on steep forested slopes in September-October at elk-range latitudes (40-50°N). Most data are from summer and from bare or sagebrush slopes.
- A search summary claimed a steep alpine valley study found the morning transition "usually lasted 2 to 3 hours after astronomical sunrise" (AMS 21st Boundary Layer symposium, paper 247551). The page was not opened, so it is not used here.
- The evening transition on steep slopes (Nadeau et al. 2013, QJRMS, "Flow during the evening transition over steep Alpine slopes") could not be retrieved, so there are no exact minutes-before-sunset numbers for steep shaded slopes beyond Pypker's 16:00-18:00 in summer.

## 2. Speeds and depths of slope flows; along-valley winds and their lag; cold-air pools and inversion breakup

### Takeaway
Night drainage on a mid-latitude slope is shallow: a temperature-deficit layer 1-20 m deep, wind maximum 1-4 m/s at 1-15 m above ground. On steep slopes the jet sits at 0.6-2 m, which is nose height. Daytime upslope flow is deeper (20-200 m) and a bit stronger (1-5 m/s maximum at 10-50 m; about 2.4-3 m/s measured at 3 m). Along-valley winds lag the slope winds by hours and reach 3-10 m/s in big valleys. Valley inversions and cold pools take about 1.5-5 hours after sunrise to break.

### Cited Findings
- **Downslope (mid-latitude, typical):** temperature deficit 3-7 °C, deficit layer 1-20 m deep, maximum wind 1-4 m/s at 1-15 m above ground, flow layer 3-100 m deep. **Upslope (typical):** temperature excess 2-7 °C, excess layer 10-100 m deep, maximum wind 1-5 m/s at 10-50 m above ground, flow layer 20-200 m deep. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013 (after Whiteman 2000)](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Downslope by slope angle (Table 2.1 in the chapter):** 21° slope: inversion 4-8 m deep, 5 K, maximum 1-2 m/s at **0.6-2 m** (Horst & Doran 1986). 9° slope: 16 m, 4 K, 1-2 m/s at 6 m (Papadopoulos et al. 1997). 6.5° slope: 40-50 m, 10 K, 1.5 m/s at 25 m. 4° slope: 50 m, 3 K, 3.5-4 m/s at 40 m (Monti et al. 2002). 1.6° slope: 25 m, 7 K, 4-6 m/s at 15-20 m (Whiteman & Zhong 2008; Haiden & Whiteman 2005). "Downslope flows on low-angle slopes are deeper and stronger than those on steeper slopes." RANS and LES models agree, predicting a smaller maximum jet speed and smaller mass flux on steeper slopes. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- The inversion over a slope is "about 5% of the vertical drop from the top of the slope" (Horst & Doran 1986). The wind maximum sits at 30-60% of the inversion depth. The downslope flow itself extends to "several times the inversion depth" through entrainment. Speed and depth grow with distance down the slope. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Katabatic winds "reach maximum speeds around 1-3 m above ground" (Doran & Horst 1981, 1983; Horst & Doran 1986, 1988; Mahrt & Larsen 1990; Whiteman 2000). Anabatic winds usually peak faster and in deeper layers than katabatic ones. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- "Skin flows": shallow (under 1-2 m), weak (perhaps under 1 m/s), non-turbulent katabatic flows reported by several investigators. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Measured near the surface (about 3 m):** Big Southern Butte means were upslope 3.0 m/s (range 1.8-4.5), afternoon 4.1 m/s (2.3-8.1) and downslope 3.4 m/s (1.3-7.5), excluding decoupled ridgetops. Salmon River Canyon means were upslope 2.4 m/s (0.75-4.0), afternoon up-valley 2.5 m/s (0.92-4.2) and downslope 1.2 m/s (0.33-4.1). On the butte, upslope speeds rose with distance up the slope. In the canyon they generally fell with distance up the slope. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- Val Ferret (steep west face): downslope about 1 m/s, upslope/up-valley about 2 m/s (abstract). — [Nadeau et al. 2020](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/field-observations-of-the-morning-transition-over-a-steep-slope-i/)
- **Along-valley winds:** the valley wind reversal "lags that of the slope flows because the large mass of the valley atmosphere must be cooled" (or warmed). The morning up-valley reversal "occurs several hours after upslope flows are initiated". In the evening, up-valley winds "continue to blow for several hours after the energy budget reversal". Peak speeds over the valley centre are "frequently in the range from 3 to 10 m/s", with a jet-like profile peaking 10s to 100s of metres above the floor. Valley exit jets reach 13 m/s at 200 m above ground (Inn Valley). Mountain-plain flows are "generally weak, usually below 2 m/s". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- In Salmon River Canyon the slopes went upslope at 09:00, but up-canyon (along-valley) flow did not take over until about 15:00 (fully established 16:00, lasting through 19:00). That is about 6 hours of lag. Down-valley flow on the plain off Big Southern Butte reached 100 m above ground by 00:00. Pressure-driven down-canyon events gave the canyon's highest speeds (up to 9.1 m/s) in mid to late morning. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- **Inversion and cold-pool breakup:** "The Colorado valley inversions typically broke up within 3-5 h after sunrise." Breakup requires warming the whole valley to the potential temperature of the inversion top. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf). At the Dugway valley foot of Granite Mountain, inversions 50-165 m deep eroded in 1 h 30 min to 2 h 50 min, with erosion starting 06:04-07:55 local time. Every case matched one of Whiteman's (1982) three patterns. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- In the Oregon forested watershed a cold-air pool formed between 20:00 and 24:00 PST. Drainage continued but slowed as the night went on. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf). "Slope flows typically weaken as basin cold pool deepens." — [Steenburgh lecture](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~steenburgh/classes/6250.fall17/lectures/ThermallyDrivenFlows.pdf)

### Inferences
- Groundwind check, drainage speed: the bake uses `kat = 3.0·sqrt(sin slope)·fetch_f` (fetch_f 0.6-1.0), scaled in the browser by up to 1.5 and by 0.7 under trees. That gives about 1.2-2.9 m/s on a 25° open slope and 0.3-0.8 m/s on a 2° slope. At *head height* on steep slopes this is about right: the jet core is at 0.6-2 m and reaches 1-2 m/s. But the sqrt(sin) scaling runs the *opposite way* to the observed trend across slopes, where gentle, long slopes carry stronger, deeper drainage (4-6 m/s at 15-20 m on 1.6°). The model's flow depth should be about 5% of the vertical drop above the point. On long, gentle valley floors the head-height speed may be well below the jet speed. Speed and depth should grow down the slope; `fetch_f` already does this in part.
- Groundwind check, upslope speed: `ana = 1.4·sqrt(sin slope)·sAna` m/s (sAna ≤ 1.5) gives at most about 1.5 m/s on a 30° slope in full sun in the open, and about 0.75 m/s in trees. Measured 3 m means in open terrain are 2.4-3.0 m/s, so the open-ground upslope speed looks low by roughly 1.5-2×. Under dense canopy see section 3: the sub-canopy sign may even be wrong.
- Mountain areas need a separate along-valley term that lags the slope flows by hours. It should keep blowing up-valley for hours after slopes start draining in the evening, and keep blowing down-valley for 2-5 hours after sunrise on the valley floor. The Northern Ontario model has no such term because there are almost no valleys deep enough to have one.

### Gaps
- No quantitative dataset found for along-valley wind speed at 1-2 m in small forested elk drainages (catchments of 1-10 km²). Most along-valley data are from large Alpine valleys or canyons at 10-100 m above ground.
- No sourced number for how long a small-basin cold pool persists after local sunrise under a forest canopy, beyond Pypker's "about 2 h well mixed" and "ceased after the base received direct sun".

## 3. When the synoptic (gradient) wind overrides thermals; forest canopy effects on slope flows

### Takeaway
Thermals dominate only when the wind aloft is light. A background flow of only 2-3 m/s already reshapes night drainage. Field studies flag "synoptically forced" days when a low, open sensor sees more than 5-6 m/s, or when 700 hPa winds exceed about 5 m/s. Ridge tops stick up out of the night stable layer and keep the gradient wind. Under tall dense conifer canopy the air below the crowns can stay stably layered and drain downhill even on sunny days while the air above the canopy blows upslope or up-valley. Canopy is the least-studied factor.

### Cited Findings
- Diurnal wind systems are "particularly prevalent in anticyclonic synoptic weather conditions where background winds are weak and skies are clear". The reversal "is also seen under partly cloudy or wind-disturbed conditions, though it is sometimes weakened... or modified by winds aloft". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- "Background flows with strengths of only 2-3 m/s can have far reaching effects on the structure of the downslope flows and even their existence." As cross-slope flow increases, the downslope jet "becomes weaker and deeper and is eventually overpowered by the cross-slope-generated turbulence". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Valley drainage erosion is favoured by an along-valley wind above the valley opposing the drainage, weak stability in the valley, and winds aloft above "a threshold around 5 m/s" that are accelerating faster than 0.0004 m/s² (Orgill et al.). When the valley is very stable, background flows are channelled along the valley axis (Whiteman & Doran 1993). Upslope flows "are often disturbed... by convective mixing that brings down the generally stronger background winds from higher layers". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Field partition thresholds:** synoptically forced hours were those when a representative flat or low sensor (about 3 m above ground) exceeded **6 m/s** (Big Southern Butte) or **5 m/s** (Salmon River Canyon). Under strong forcing "the diurnal slope flows on BSB were completely overtaken by the larger-scale flows". Speeds then varied by up to 15 m/s across the domain from speed-up and lee sheltering. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/); [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016 (ACPD manuscript)](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- MATERHORN "quiescent" (thermally driven) days were selected with a 700 hPa synoptic wind under 5 m/s. A second criterion in the same table used under 9 m/s. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- In WindNinja's idealised 600 m hill test, an ambient wind of 2.7 m/s (6 mph) still showed diurnal effects, but "the ambient wind is beginning to overpower the diurnal winds". — [Forthofer, Shannon & Butler 2009](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/156275.pdf)
- **Ridge tops decouple:** sensors about 800 m above the plain "did not exhibit a diurnal pattern". They "likely protruded out of the nocturnal boundary layer and were exposed to the decoupled gradient-level winds". Mountaintop winds were "often twice as high" as on the plain. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/); [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- Under clouds, cold-air pools are less stable and better mixed vertically. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Canopy, Oregon (25-28 m Douglas-fir, LAI 4-10, 20-33° slopes):** "On summer days, the wind above the canopy flowed upvalley whereas the wind direction below the canopy was often down-valley." In mid-afternoon, air in the foliage layer (20-30 m) was 2-6 °C warmer than air below it, a canopy inversion. At 3 m the flow was down-valley *all day* on 20 July 2005 but up-valley for part of the day on 17-18 August. On those August days the ridge wind was stronger (2.3 vs 1.8 m/s at 23.5 m) and stability weaker, which "may have allowed winds above the canopy to penetrate into the canopy, thereby forcing the wind upvalley". After the evening transition a second drainage jet formed above the canopy, from the canopy top to about 45 m. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Canopy, Niwot Ridge (Colorado subalpine forest, about 3000 m, 5-6° slope):** a "very stable layer near the height of maximum leaf area density" limits exchange. "Nighttime drainage flows are restricted to a relatively shallow layer of air beneath the canopy, with little vertical mixing across a relatively long horizontal fetch" (abstract only). — [Yi et al. 2005, JGR 110](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027660); slope angle from [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- "Heating in canopy layer can produce upslope flow above shallow downslope in the snow-cooled sub-canopy layer." — [Steenburgh lecture](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~steenburgh/classes/6250.fall17/lectures/ThermallyDrivenFlows.pdf)
- "The effects of forest canopies and other types of varying and patchy surface cover on diurnal wind systems have been largely neglected in much of the experimental and modeling work to date." — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Groundwind today (`model.ts` 616-617, local 10 m wind in km/h): night mixing `exp(-max(0, U-5)/8)` and day mixing `exp(-max(0, U-8)/10)`. Under trees drainage is ×0.7 and upslope ×0.5. — `C:\dev\huntapp\app\src\weather\micro\model.ts`

### Inferences
- Groundwind's night fade starts at 1.4 m/s local wind (5 km/h). At 4.2 m/s (15 km/h) it keeps 29% of the drainage, and it reaches near zero around 6-7 m/s. That fits the literature (2-3 m/s already matters; 5-6 m/s at a low sensor means forced). Two things matter in mountains. First, the controlling wind should be the wind *above the valley* (ridge level or 700 hPa), not a sheltered local 10 m wind. Second, ridge-top cells should be treated as *coupled*: gradient wind, no diurnal term, even on calm-looking nights.
- Canopy: a flat ×0.5 on upslope under trees is probably the wrong shape for dense, tall conifer. The evidence supports a "decoupled sub-canopy" state. Below the crowns, flow follows the drainage (downhill or down-valley) on many sunny days. It turns upslope mainly where sun reaches the floor (openings, sparse or leafless stands) or where wind above the canopy is stronger and stability weaker (above about 2 m/s at the canopy top in Pypker's case). In that state, scent released at 1.5 m stays under the canopy and runs downhill even at midday.
- Tall dense timber also starts draining as soon as the drainage is shaded in late afternoon, hours before sunset. This is consistent with hunters' complaints about early, unpredictable evening thermals in timber.

### Gaps
- No field study found that gives a threshold above-canopy wind speed for sub-canopy flow to switch from drainage to coupled, beyond Pypker's single comparison (1.8 vs 2.3 m/s).
- No quantitative study found of slope flows in *open* or *patchy* subalpine forest (lodgepole pine, aspen, burn mosaics) typical of elk range.

## 4. Ridge-top, saddle and lee effects: eddies, rotors, swirling, cross-valley circulation. What "swirling wind" in a drainage is

### Takeaway
"Swirling" is not one thing. Five physically different situations can cause it: (1) calm transition periods when the wind direction wanders; (2) two flows fighting, such as up-valley wind after sidewalls start draining, or a tributary drainage meeting the main valley; (3) mechanical recirculation in the lee and in side drainages when the ridge wind blows across them; (4) daytime convective eddies on sun-heated slopes mixing gusts down from aloft; (5) nighttime pulsing, internal waves and sloshing of cold pools. Wind models catch (3) only with a momentum solver and miss the rest.

### Cited Findings
- **Calm transitions:** after local sunrise, 2 m winds fell below 1 m/s for 1-40 minutes before upslope flow began (Val Ferret). — [Nadeau et al. 2020](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/field-observations-of-the-morning-transition-over-a-steep-slope-i/). At sunrise in the forested watershed the air was "well mixed" for about 2 hours. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Competing flows:** up-valley winds "continue to blow for several hours after the energy budget reversal" while sidewalls already drain. Valley flows that strengthen in the evening "produce a cross-slope shear that disturbs the slope flows". Cold air from a tributary "tends to accumulate in the tributary and then enter the main valley through surges", sometimes as "a regularly pulsating outflow". Up-valley flow can be undercut by cooler air spilling through a saddle (Ora del Garda / Adige example). — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Cross-valley circulation:** when the two sidewalls heat unequally (aspect, shadows), "cross-basin flows toward the warmer sidewall" develop (Hoch & Whiteman 2010; Lehner et al.). In a bent valley (Riviera Valley), inertia carried up-valley flow to the outside of the bend, tilting the inversion and causing "anomalous downslope flows on the sunlit western sidewall". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Mechanical lee and side-drainage recirculation:** in the afternoon regime at Big Southern Butte there was "some apparent recirculation on the northeastern side of the butte as well as in some of the side drainages". In Salmon River Canyon, one sensor (NM2) saw easterly or southeasterly flow most of the day, probably because it sat "in a local recirculation zone formed in the small side drainage". The lowest speeds were "on the leeward side of the butte and in sheltered side drainages". — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- **Daytime convection:** the daytime boundary layer over slopes is "a convective-advective boundary layer, with bent-over plumes and convective elements moving up the slope". Incipient upslope flows are "somewhat intermittent and inhomogeneous". Upslope flows are disturbed by convective mixing that brings down stronger winds from aloft. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf). The upslope wind at Granite Mountain was "particularly steady for the first hours" but later deviated from the along-slope direction (cloud changes, synoptic forcing). — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Night pulsing and sloshing:** downslope flows are modulated by "meandering motions in stratified flows", internal waves, and "overshooting and oscillations caused by the resulting buoyancy disequilibrium". In basin inversions, seiche-like motions are seen as smoke plumes "carried back and forth quasi-horizontally relative to its source". — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf). Nighttime drainage at Granite Mountain had "a periodic component due to the formation of gravity waves" (Lehner et al. 2015). — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Ridges and summits:** ridgetop sensors were decoupled from the diurnal cycle and exposed to gradient winds. Winds were highest on ridge tops and upper windward slopes and lowest in the lee. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)
- Mass-consistent WindNinja "has been shown to have difficulties simulating flows on the lee side of terrain features due to the fact that it does not account for conservation of momentum". — [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf). The momentum solver did better in the lee. — [Forthofer et al. 2014, IJWF 23:969](https://connectsci.au/wf/article-lookup/doi/10.1071/WF12089)

### Inferences
- For the app, "swirl" should be flagged by *cause*, because each has a different fix for the hunter:
  - (a) Transition windows tied to local sunrise and sunset per cell: the next 0-60 minutes are unreliable. Wait, or play the side the flow is heading toward.
  - (b) Drainage confluences and the valley bottom in the evening, where slope drainage meets residual up-valley wind.
  - (c) Lee or side-drainage cells when the ridge wind blows across the drainage. The momentum bake already covers this (Groundwind's WindNinja turbulence and colMax bake).
  - (d) Sunlit slopes from late morning on, when the ridge wind is above a few m/s (convective gusts from aloft).
  - (e) Calm, clear nights in basins, where pooled air sloshes and scent drifts back and forth around the source instead of streaming.
- Sheltered side drainages can hold a recirculation that runs *against* both the main-valley flow and the slope flow all day (sensor NM2). Some spots are persistently "wrong-way" and would be good targets for the on-phone wind-check learning.

### Gaps
- No quantitative source found (searches exhausted) for wind *direction variability* (sigma-theta) against wind speed in weak winds over sloping forest. Mahrt's work on weak-wind meandering would be the place to look.
- Saddle-specific thermal behaviour (spill-over of cold air through cols, daytime upslope convergence on saddles) has only the qualitative Ora del Garda example. No numbers.

## 5. How scent moves in these flows

### Takeaway
At night and in shade, scent released at nose height goes into the core of a thin, stable drainage layer (jet maximum at 0.6-3 m on steep slopes). It travels downslope at about 1-2 m/s with little vertical dilution, follows the drainage network, and collects in cold pools on valley floors and in basins. There it lingers and sloshes until the pool breaks 1.5-5 hours after sunrise. In daytime upslope flow, scent rises along the slope as a bent-over plume in a 20-200 m deep, convective layer, so it dilutes faster.

### Cited Findings
- On a 21° slope the drainage inversion was 4-8 m deep, with the wind maximum at 0.6-2 m (Horst & Doran 1986). — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf). Katabatic maxima are typically 1-3 m above ground. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Tracer evidence, forested steep watershed:** SF6 released about 600 m upstream "was also readily detected at the base tower at night after a delay corresponding to an advection speed of 1-2 m/s". This confirmed that "air flow from the limits of the watershed was being channeled through the narrow valley outlet". In the late afternoon, when drainage was confined below the canopy, estimated SF6 advection below the 37 m tower top was "within about 20% of the release rate", so nearly all of it stayed in the sub-canopy drainage. After sunset only 20-25% stayed below 37 m. The rest moved in an upper drainage jet from the canopy top to about 45 m once the sub-canopy inversion broke down. Concentrations took about 30 minutes to fall to zero after a release stopped. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Tracer evidence, subalpine forest:** SF6 showed "minimal vertical dispersion of nighttime, subcanopy drainage flows... little vertical mixing across a relatively long horizontal fetch" (abstract). — [Yi et al. 2005](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027660)
- Cold air drainage occurs even on slopes under 1% gradient. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Pooling:** cold pools fill valleys between 20:00 and midnight, and drainage into them slows through the night. — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf). Smoke in basin inversions oscillates back and forth around the source (seiches). Tributary cold air builds up and enters the main valley in surges. — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- **Daytime:** "Upslope winds flow up the slope as a bent-over plume rather than rising vertically, even in a neutral environment." The volume flux of upslope flow grows with distance up the slope as air is entrained. Studies of pollutants carried in upslope flows describe "layering, venting and trapping" (Reuten 2008 and others). — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)
- Groundwind already makes scent "hug the ground" and follow drainage into low ground and pools. — `C:\dev\huntapp\app\src\weather\micro\scent.ts` (comments at lines 23, 41, 220, 440)

### Inferences
- At 1-2 m/s, drainage moves scent 3.6-7 km per hour. In a narrow drainage the plume stays concentrated, so a hunter at the head of a drainage on a clear evening can be smelled by elk in the bottom 0.5-2 km below within minutes. This is the physical basis for "evening thermals pull scent into the bottoms".
- Scent spread through a cold pool overnight can stay in the valley until the inversion breaks (1.5-5 hours after sunrise), then be lifted and carried up-valley or upslope. Valley bottoms keep a "memory" of the night's scent into mid-morning. The app could treat pooled cells as accumulating rather than streaming, as it partly does now.
- Daytime cones on sunlit slopes should be shorter and wider (stronger vertical mixing, more gusts) than night drainage cones, which should be long and narrow and follow the terrain. If a cone length scales with a stability-dependent dilution, it should be longest in drainage and pooled regimes.
- Under dense canopy in late afternoon, nearly all tracer stayed in the sub-canopy drainage. In timber, a hunter's scent can head downhill hours before sunset even while the treetops sway upslope.

### Gaps
- No study found that measured actual animal or human scent (as opposed to SF6 or CO2) in mountain slope flows, or the concentration an elk can detect. Detection distances belong to the olfaction research (outside this scope).
- No quantitative data on how far scent rises out of a daytime upslope layer, for example at what downwind distance a 1.5 m release is mixed through 50 m.

## 6. WindNinja's diurnal slope-flow scheme, its validation, combining it with the momentum solver, and other fast models

### Takeaway
WindNinja's diurnal option is Mahrt's (1982) one-dimensional "shooting flow" slope wind, as used in CALMET. It is driven by an energy-budget sensible heat flux (sun on each slope, terrain shadows, cloud). It is added to the initial wind field and then made mass-consistent. It was untested when introduced and later produced *too weak* slope flows at Big Southern Butte. Direction errors in thermal regimes stayed around 65-80° RMSE. In current WindNinja, including the 4.0.0 install on this machine, diurnal flow *can* be combined with the momentum solver: a mass-solver pass adds it on top of the CFD result. Non-neutral stability cannot be used with the momentum solver.

### Cited Findings
- **How it works:** "The model uses sensible surface heat flux, distance to ridge top or valley bottom, slope steepness, and surface and entrainment drag parameters to compute a diurnal slope wind." The slope flow model is "the shooting flow model of Mahrt (1982). It is implemented the same as in CALMET (Scire and Robe 1997)... a one-dimensional model of buoyancy driven flow along a slope." The acceleration distance is the distance to the ridge top for downslope flow and to the valley bottom for upslope flow, found by walking along the cell's slope and aspect. "The direction of the slope flow is exactly upslope or downslope as defined at the cell. The depth of the slope flow is assumed to be 5% of the elevation difference to the ridge or valley." — [Forthofer, Shannon & Butler 2009](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/156275.pdf)
- **Heat flux:** this comes from a CALMET-style micrometeorological model using the Holtslag & van Ulden (1983) energy budget. Net radiation is reduced by cloud fraction. Direct sun on each slope comes from NREL SOLPOS, with "a custom shadow computation... to account for terrain shadows". A Bowen ratio sets latent heat and an empirical ground-flux fraction is used. At night the van Ulden & Holtslag (1985) scheme gives the Monin-Obukhov length and boundary layer height. — [Forthofer et al. 2009](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/156275.pdf)
- **Combination:** "WindNinja computes wind fields by first initializing the flow domain using [a log profile] and a user specified input wind speed and direction. Then the slope flow component is computed and added to the initialized values. Last, the WindNinja solver is run which solves for a divergence free flow (mass-conservation) while minimizing the change from the initial flow field (including slope flow parameterization)." — [Forthofer et al. 2009](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/156275.pdf)
- **Initial test:** a hypothetical 600 m hill, 5.6 km across, maximum slope 15°, grass, 18 September at Kalispell, Montana. Ambient winds were 0, 1.3 and 2.7 m/s and output was at 6.1 m. The scheme gave "qualitatively reasonable results... although it has not been validated against measured data". — [Forthofer et al. 2009](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/156275.pdf)
- **Defaults (WindNinja master `cli.cpp`):** downslope drag 0.0001, downslope entrainment 0.01, upslope drag 0.2, upslope entrainment 0.2. Diurnal runs require air temperature, cloud cover, date, time and time zone. `conflicting_options(vm, "momentum_flag", "non_neutral_stability")`. — [firelab/windninja `src/ninja/cli.cpp`](https://github.com/firelab/windninja/blob/master/src/ninja/cli.cpp)
- **Momentum plus diurnal:** in `ninjaArmy.cpp`, "if it's a ninjafoam run and diurnal is turned on, link the ninjafoam with a ninja run to add diurnal flow after the cfd solution is computed". A second mass-conserving `ninja` run is started with `foamDomainAverageInitializationFlag` (or the wx-model or gridded equivalent), using the CFD velocity and angle grids as its starting field. — [firelab/windninja `src/ninja/ninjaArmy.cpp`](https://github.com/firelab/windninja/blob/master/src/ninja/ninjaArmy.cpp). The local WindNinja 4.0.0 build (`C:/tmp/windninja/app/bin/WindNinja_cli.exe` and `WindNinjadll.dll`) contains the "Adding diurnal winds..." step and lists both `--diurnal_winds` and `--momentum_flag`. A combined run was not tried. Its `--help` does not list the drag and entrainment options, so those are newer than 4.0.0 (checked locally 2026-10-05).
- **Validation (Big Southern Butte, 5-day period, 3 m sensors, hourly):** the diurnal scheme "helped to orient winds downslope". But "WindNinja predicted overly weak slope flows compared to observations". In the representative downslope case it "did not predict strong enough downslope flow... to reverse the flow from the prevailing northwest direction" on the windward side. Suggested causes were "improper parameterization of surface or entrainment drag parameters, poor estimation of the depth of the slope flow, or deficiencies in the micrometeorological model". — [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016, ACP 16:5229 (manuscript v4)](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- **Error numbers (downscaled values in brackets, across four NWP drivers):** speed RMSE downslope 2.03-2.36 m/s (1.83-2.66); upslope 1.73-2.02 (1.62-1.86); externally driven 2.92-3.21 (2.39-2.59). Direction RMSE downslope 78-88° (72-81°); upslope 68-74° (65-72°); 5-day 73-80° (71-76°). The largest gain in direction accuracy was in the downslope regime. Speed gains were largest when observed speeds exceeded 10 m/s. NWP accuracy "sharply dropped off for observed speeds above 10 m/s". Results under upslope and downslope (non-neutral) conditions were "mixed". — [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- "WindNinja assumes neutral atmospheric stability... so this stratification is not handled." Ridgetops were decoupled and exposed to gradient winds above the nocturnal boundary layer. — [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- **Momentum vs mass solver:** both "performed best on the upwind side and top of terrain features and had reduced accuracy on the lee side". The momentum solver did better in the lee. Run times range from "a few seconds for the mass-conserving model to ~1 h for the momentum-conserving model". Both assume neutral stability (abstract). — [Forthofer, Butler & Wagenbrenner 2014, IJWF 23:969](https://connectsci.au/wf/article-lookup/doi/10.1071/WF12089)
- **Other fast or simple approaches named in the literature:** the Prandtl analytical model "agreed qualitatively with observations of both upslope and downslope flows, successfully producing the typical jet-like wind profiles". The German Weather Service's KLAM_21 drainage model has "proven useful in simulations on a range of scales from an individual vineyard to a domain of several tens of kilometers". Hydraulic and mixed-layer models exist for up-valley wind (Zardi 2000). — [Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf). An analytical model for daily-periodic slope winds was published as Marchio et al. 2024, QJRMS, Parts 1-2 (seen in search results only). — [Marchio et al. 2024 Part 2 (PDF)](https://iris.unitn.it/retrieve/0c3a2d1d-3704-4055-8904-b76ae6902d25/Marchio%20et%20al%20QJ%202024%20An%20analytical%20model%20for%20daily%e2%80%90periodic%20slope%20winds%20%20Part%202%20%20Solutions.pdf). Farina et al. tested an analytical model by Zardi & Serafin (2015) against MATERHORN data (EGU21 abstract). — [EGU21-12408](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-12408.html)

### Inferences
- Groundwind's architecture (momentum bake per direction, plus a separate thermal vector added at run time and blended by mixing) is the same superposition WindNinja uses. The difference is that WindNinja re-solves mass conservation after adding the slope flow. Groundwind adds the vectors cell by cell. For a phone, cell-wise addition is fine. What matters more is the *inputs* the thermal term gets:
  - per-cell illumination with terrain shadows (WindNinja has it; Groundwind uses global sun elevation for drainage);
  - acceleration distance to the ridge or valley (Groundwind's `fetch_f` uses contributing area, which is similar in spirit);
  - flow depth of 5% of the drop (absent in Groundwind).
- Since WindNinja's own slope flows came out too weak and its thermal-regime direction errors are about 70°, baking WindNinja's diurnal output would not by itself give an accurate thermal field. The Mahrt scheme is cheap enough to compute in the browser: it is one-dimensional, needs the distance to ridge or valley, the slope, and a heat flux. Calibrating it to the 3 m observations above (upslope about 2.4-3 m/s, downslope about 1.2-3.4 m/s) and to on-phone wind checks is likely better value than a WindNinja diurnal bake.
- If a bake is wanted, the 4.0.0 CLI on this machine can run momentum plus diurnal for set hours and cloud covers. It needs a run per time of day and per sky state, which multiplies the 16-direction bake, and it cannot use non-neutral stability with momentum.

### Gaps
- Wagenbrenner et al. 2019 (*Atmosphere* 10:672, WindNinja RANS solver evaluation) could not be fetched (HTTP 403). Its error statistics and its statements on diurnal flow with the momentum solver are therefore not included.
- No published validation of WindNinja's diurnal scheme in *forested* terrain was found. Granite Mountain (MATERHORN) WindNinja comparisons were not located before the search budget ran out.

## 7. Common elk-hunting thermal advice compared with the meteorology; how elk use thermals when bedding

### Takeaway
Hunters' core model matches the physics: air drains downhill at night and early morning, rises on sun-heated slopes by day, and drains again toward evening; mornings are steadier than evenings. But hunters put the switch at fixed clock times (around 9-11 am up, 5-6 pm down), when it really depends on when the sun reaches or leaves each slope. That varies by hours with aspect, canyon depth and canopy. The right positioning follows from the physics: stay below the elk while air drains, above them while it rises. There is no peer-reviewed evidence about elk bedding with thermals in this research.

### Cited Findings
- **[hunter belief]** Elk move uphill to bed in the morning "with the thermals hitting them in their face" (air still falling). Once the sun warms the slope the thermals reverse upward, so bedded elk smell threats from below. In late afternoon elk "head down in elevation with the afternoon thermals in their face". Advice: hunt "under" elk during morning (downslope) thermals and still-hunt "above" bedded elk in the afternoon. — [GoHunt, "Using your nose to find elk"](https://gohunt.com/browse/tips-and-tricks/using-your-nose-to-find-elk)
- **[hunter belief]** "Thermals go down from 6pm-11 am. Up between noon and 5." 11am-noon and 5-6pm are "all over the place". "Morning thermals last much longer than the evening thermals." One hunter got only "30-45 minutes of consistent thermals in evening". Swirling is worst "in a draw" in the evening, so set up "at the top of a draw". Creek bottoms pull scent downhill "like a vacuum". Elk move to bed "with their nose in the wind". — [Elk101 forum, "Morning vs. Evening Thermals?"](https://forums.elk101.com/threads/morning-vs-evening-thermals.7810/latest)
- **Meteorology against those beliefs:**
  - Morning upslope on open slopes was fully established around 10:00 at an isolated butte (sunrise 06:00-07:00) and started around 09:00 in a canyon ([Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/)).
  - It took about 5 hours after sunrise on a west-facing alpine slope ([Nadeau et al. 2020](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/field-observations-of-the-morning-transition-over-a-steep-slope-i/)) and under 30 minutes on a sunlit east-facing slope ([Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)).
  - Evening drainage starts in late afternoon in shaded terrain ([Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)) and at 16:00-18:00 under forest canopy ([Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)).
  - Up-valley flow persists for hours after the slopes reverse, causing cross-slope shear ([Zardi & Whiteman 2013](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~whiteman/homepage/articles/ZardiWhiteman_2012.pdf)). That fits the hunters' experience that evenings in draws are short-lived and swirly.

### Inferences
- "Down 6pm-11am, up noon-5" is a fair *average* for shaded, forested elk drainages in fall. It is wrong in both directions for particular slopes: an east-facing open face can be upslope by 8 am, and a north-facing timbered bottom may never go upslope below the canopy. An app that times the switch per cell from local illumination, canopy and valley position would do better than the folklore. The 20-60 minute transition calm around each switch should be shown as unreliable.
- Positioning rule from the physics: be *downhill* of the elk while air drains (night, early morning before the sun reaches the slope, evening after it leaves) and *uphill* while it rises (sunlit slope, late morning to mid-afternoon). The caller's example, "hunt from above in the morning / below in the evening", is backwards for the early-morning and late-evening drainage periods. It only holds if "morning" means after the slope's own switch to upslope (for example, getting above elk as they climb to bed) and "evening" means after the slope goes into shade (sitting below the meadows they drop into). GoHunt's version (under in morning drainage, above in afternoon upslope) matches the physics.
- "Wait for the switch" is sound, but the wait is site-specific: about 0.5-1 hour after the sun reaches the ground on open slopes, about 2 hours more for valley floors, and possibly all day under dense canopy.
- Bedding: the hunter belief (bed high, wind or thermals from below, view downhill) fits the physics that daytime upslope flow carries scent from below to a bedded elk on a sunlit slope. On a shaded north-facing timbered slope, sub-canopy flow may instead run *downhill* past the bed, bringing scent from above. Whether elk choose beds by this has not been shown here.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found (searches exhausted) on elk choosing bed sites by wind or thermal direction. Classic elk bed-site studies (for example on thermal cover and slope position) were not retrieved. Animal behaviour is better taken from the elk-behaviour research notes.
- A Rokslide thread ("When do thermals switch?") reportedly gives 8:30-9:30 am. The page returned HTTP 403, so that number is unverified.
- MeatEater, onX and Elk101 articles were not retrieved. Only the GoHunt article and the Elk101 forum thread represent hunter belief here.

## 8. Field measurements of near-surface (1-2 m) wind in forested mountain terrain in fall

### Takeaway
There is little such data. The closest are Pypker et al. 2007 (steep Douglas-fir watershed in Oregon, below-canopy sonic and tracer data, spring to fall), Yi et al. 2005 (Niwot Ridge subalpine forest in Colorado, below-canopy drainage with tracer), Butler et al. 2015 (about 3 m sensors, mostly non-forest, through mid-September), MATERHORN fall 2012 (desert slope) and Val Ferret (2 m, summer, alpine). No data set was found of 1-2 m wind across steep forested elk terrain in September-October.

### Cited Findings
- **Oregon Cascades (H.J. Andrews WS1), 96 ha, 20-33° slopes, Douglas-fir 25-28 m tall:** sonic anemometers on a 37 m tower at the outlet plus a ridge tower, 30 thermistors along the slopes, sodar up to 105 m, tethersonde, SF6 releases in 2005-2006. "During spring, summer and fall, the cold air drainage... followed a consistent diel pattern." — [Pypker et al. 2007](https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/lter/pubs/pdf/pub4223.pdf)
- **Niwot Ridge subalpine forest, Colorado:** four towers with wind profiles, SF6 diffusion and an analytical drainage model. Drainage is confined beneath the canopy with minimal vertical dispersion (abstract only). — [Yi et al. 2005](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70027660)
- **Big Southern Butte and Salmon River Canyon:** 53 surface sensors (15 June to 9 September 2010) and 27 sensors (14 July to 13 September 2011), 10-minute averages at the top of each hour, at about 3 m above ground. Vegetation was mostly grass and sagebrush under 1 m, with "isolated stands of 3-10 m tall conifers" on north slopes of the butte and "some timber in the higher elevations on the northerly aspects" in the canyon. The data are public. — [Butler et al. 2015](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/15/3785/2015/); 3.0 m sensor height per [Wagenbrenner et al. 2016](https://acp.copernicus.org/preprints/acp-2015-761/acp-2015-761-manuscript-version4.pdf)
- **MATERHORN (Granite Mountain, Utah):** fall 2012 and spring 2013 campaigns, five towers on the east slope, tethersondes at the valley site. — [Farina et al. 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/451009/1/apme-JAMC-D-22-0011.1.pdf)
- **Val Ferret (Swiss Alps):** two turbulence towers on a steep west-facing slope, summer 2010, 2 m winds reported. — [Nadeau et al. 2020](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/field-observations-of-the-morning-transition-over-a-steep-slope-i/)

### Inferences
- The Butler et al. 2015 data (public, about 3 m, hourly, regime-labelled) are the best ready-made test set for Groundwind's thermal term in open mountain terrain. The Pypker data are the best qualitative guide for dense timber.
- Groundwind's own on-phone wind checks in elk terrain could become one of the only fall, head-height, forested-mountain data sets. That is a reason to capture regime, canopy, aspect and local sun state with each check.

### Gaps
- No 1-2 m wind measurements in steep, forested elk range (Rocky Mountain subalpine or montane, lodgepole and aspen) in September-October were found.
- AmeriFlux sites in mountain forests (for example Niwot Ridge US-NR1) may hold below-canopy sonic data for fall. Whether they are available and suitable was not checked.
