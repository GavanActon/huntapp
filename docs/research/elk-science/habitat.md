# Elk (Cervus canadensis) fall habitat selection: findings for cell-by-cell spot scoring

Scope: where elk bed, feed and travel from September to December, and how time of day and weather change that. Roads and hunting pressure appear only in passing (another researcher covers them). Researched 2026-10-05. Many core papers (Wiley, ScienceDirect) blocked full-text fetches. Where a number comes only from an abstract or a search summary, the line says so. Unless a line says otherwise, the "selection" numbers come from resource-selection or step-selection models fitted to GPS or LORAN telemetry.

Season caveat: much of the strongest telemetry work (Starkey, Westside models, Black Hills beds) covers **summer**, not fall. Each such line is marked [summer]. Fall-specific studies are marked [fall].

---

## 1. Bedding sites: slope, aspect, elevation, canopy, terrain position, distance to forage

### Takeaway
Elk bed in forest with clearly more canopy and basal area than around them: about 54% canopy against 29% random in the Black Hills, on north aspects in warm weather, with a microsite about 5 °C cooler than the open. Beds sit on gentle to moderate slopes. I found no GPS study that measured bed-site terrain position (benches, heads of drainages, finger ridges) in fall. That hunter rule is unverified.

### Cited Findings
- [summer] Custer State Park, South Dakota, n = 131 diurnal summer bed sites (June–Aug) against 131 random plots. Overstory canopy closure averaged 54% at beds against 29% at random. Basal area was 12.4 against 4.9 m²/ha. Trees stood within the 0.01 ha plot at 128/131 (97.7%) of beds. Grass cover was 15.4% against 35.7%. North aspects were selected (χ² = 15.14, P < 0.05). Ambient air before homing was 30.3 °C against 24.8 °C at the bed. Slope %, elevation, distance to roads, trails and water did **not** differ from random. Canopy, microsite temperature and basal area together classified 86.2% of sites. The authors' "average summer diurnal bed site": basal area > 12.4 m²/ha, > 110 trees/ha, > 54% canopy closure, on N aspects — [Millspaugh et al. 1998, Am. Midl. Nat.](https://www.originalwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/bsk-pdf-manager/2019/04/Millspaugh-et-al_1998_Summer-bed-sites-of-elk-in-the-Black-Hills-South-Dakota.pdf)
- The same paper cites earlier work: "good" canopy cover > 70% and "adequate" 50–70% for elk in WA/OR (Thomas et al. 1979; Leckenby 1984). Elk use was greatest on slopes < 30% in Utah, Montana and Oregon (Julander & Jeffrey 1964; Zahn 1974; Witmer & deCalesta 1983) — [Millspaugh et al. 1998](https://www.originalwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/bsk-pdf-manager/2019/04/Millspaugh-et-al_1998_Summer-bed-sites-of-elk-in-the-Black-Hills-South-Dakota.pdf)
- [all seasons pooled] Black Hills, SD, 1998–2001: 412 directly observed elk sites (225 bedded, 187 feeding) against 509 random sites. 92% of bed sites had little or no western snowberry (the open-forest shrub), which shows that bedded elk select "medium to dense forest". Feeding sites sat in grassland and open forest. Classification-tree error was about 15%. Site (microhabitat) variables mattered more than stand variables (250 m buffer) — [Rumble & Gamo 2011, Prairie Naturalist](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_other/rmrs_2011_rumble_m002.pdf)
- [summer, Starkey OR] Elk use of canopy cover was "far higher during the day than at night". In the later season elk used fir stands more, more by day than by night. Elk "consistently avoided ... steep slopes" — [Spitz et al. 2018, Ecol. Appl.](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- [May–Oct, Starkey] Over 30 years of telemetry, elk selected untreated (closed-canopy) forest by day in every month May–October. Daytime selection for it grew stronger as summer heat built, which the authors read as thermal-cover seeking — [Ruprecht et al. 2023, Ecol. Appl.](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)
- [boreal-parkland, Manitoba] Duck Mountains: 22 GPS cow elk (2005–06), 79,284 locations. Locations clustered at < 10% and 81–90% crown closure. 51% fell on **middle slopes**, and aspects were variable (~60%) or southerly. Field observation (not tested): elk in cut-blocks "find a spot near the crest of the knoll ... to bed down" — [Chranowski 2009, MEnv thesis, U. Manitoba](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)
- [fall hunting seasons, SW Montana] 325 GPS adult females, 9 populations, 2005–2014. Mean slope at elk locations was 14.3° (SD 9.4) in archery season and 14.1° (SD 8.9) in rifle season. Elk were "more likely to use moderate slopes". Mean canopy at elk locations was 27.7% in archery season and 19.6% in rifle season (this is a fairly open sagebrush and conifer landscape) — [Ranglack et al. 2017, MFWP final report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/fall-elk-resource-selection---final-report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Bed-score rule candidates: canopy ≥ 50–55% (rising to ~70%+), basal area ≥ 12 m²/ha, a north aspect bonus that grows with air temperature (see §7), and a gentle to moderate slope (peak around 10–20°, penalty above ~30° or ~58%). These come mostly from summer data. In cool fall weather the north-aspect bonus should shrink or flip (see §7, the warm/cool aspect swap).
- Distance to forage does not show up as a bed-site discriminator (Millspaugh found water, roads and trails did not differ). In the models it works at the landscape level through the "distance to cover-forage edge" term (§2, §8).
- Beds being on "middle slopes" in Manitoba and on knoll crests inside cut-blocks fits a TPI rule favouring mid-slope and minor rises over valley bottoms. That is weak evidence.

### Gaps
- I found no GPS or field study measuring **fall** bed-site terrain position (benches, heads of drainages, finger ridges, saddles) for elk. These are hunter heuristics with no measured effect sizes in what I could reach.
- A northern Idaho claim surfaced in search summaries: summer beds in pole timber on ridges, shifting in autumn to pole timber on mesic slopes. I could not trace it to a primary source.
- "Bedding cover 75–100% closed, 30–60 acres" circulates in hunting media without a traceable study.
- GPS fix success falls under canopy (a search result cited fix-rate drops of 14.5% deciduous, 25.6% mixed and 47.4% coniferous for one collar study — [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319949707_Importance_of_evaluating_GPS_telemetry_collar_performance_in_monitoring_reintroduced_populations_GPS_Collar_Performance_in_Reintroductions)). Telemetry-based studies therefore tend to **under-count** dense-cover bedding. Chranowski 2009 makes the same point for mature deciduous forest.

## 2. Feeding sites: openings, burns by age, cuts by age, aspen, shrubfields, agriculture; distance from cover by day and night

### Takeaway
Elk feed in openings and young disturbance (burns, cuts, reclaimed mines, fields, grassland), mostly **at night and in twilight**, and retreat to closed forest by day. Burns peak quickly: about 5 yr after prescribed fire, back to baseline by about 15 yr. Wildfire is used 2–3 yr after the fire in early autumn. Logged units peak later: about 14 yr after harvest, still selected at 26–33 yr. Classic guidelines put most feeding within about 180–200 m of cover. The Westside model uses distance to the cover-forage edge as a continuous negative term.

### Cited Findings
**Day/night commuting**
- [May 1–Aug 19, Starkey OR] 26 fir stands thinned and prescribed-burned 2001–03 against 27 controls, followed 1996–2016 (LORAN-C n = 432, GPS n = 282 adult females; ~34 collars/yr). Elk "avoid[ed] burns during the day, but select[ed] them at night". Nocturnal selection peaked **5 yr post-burn** and declined to pre-treatment levels by **~15 yr**. By day, recent burns were neutral (~1), and burns **6–12 yr old lowered** the chance of a location being chosen. Selection flipped with "steep crepuscular transitions". The authors conclude "the most important foraging hours for elk in our system are nocturnal rather than crepuscular" — [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- [May–Oct, Starkey] Logged units were selected **at night only**, strongest in midsummer (July peak). Selection peaked at **14 yr post-harvest** and persisted **26–33 yr**. By day, logged areas were avoided for **up to 28 yr**, with untreated forest selected instead. Selection for logged areas was **73% stronger** at low elk density than at high. Use peaked in harvest units of **0.17–0.21 km² (17–21 ha)**, but units averaged only 8.1 ha, so this is weak. Grasslands were selected day and night in **May and October** and avoided in the summer months — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)
- [all seasons, Black Hills] 82% of elk feeding sites had > 2% western snowberry cover (an open-forest shrub). Unclassified feeding sites were in grassland and open forest, while random sites were mostly pole forest > 40% canopy and mature forest > 70% canopy — [Rumble & Gamo 2011](https://www.fs.usda.gov/rm/pubs_other/rmrs_2011_rumble_m002.pdf)

**Burns in fall**
- [fall, Blackfoot–Clearwater, western Montana] 2017 Rice Ridge fire (~160,000 acres). 29% of the herd's autumn range burned, 54% of it high severity. 59 adult female GPS elk, years 2–3 post-fire. Probability of selecting low-severity burned forest was 0.99 in the prehunt period (24 Aug–6 Sep), 0.99 in archery-only, 0.99 in backcountry rifle and **0.0010 in the general rifle period** (late Oct–Nov). High-severity burns did not differ much from low. Digestible energy was positively selected (β = 0.77 prehunt). Canopy cover was negatively selected (β = −0.11 prehunt, −2.54 rifle). By late autumn, snowpack and hunter pressure drove distribution and "wildfire had little influence" — [Snobl et al. 2024, JWM 88:e22507](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/16---snobl.etal.2023.pdf)
- Contrast [summer ranges, Idaho Bitterroot]: in IDFG's Maxent seasonal-range model, elk selection **decreased** with more area burned within 2 km in the previous 11 yr (2005–2015) — [IDFG, Elk Seasonal Ranges in Idaho v2, 2016](https://fishandgame.idaho.gov/ifwis/idnhp/cdc_pdf/Elk%20Season%20Range%20in%20Idaho%20Version%202.pdf)
- Search summaries of older work disagree on burn duration: one study found more use 1–2 yr post-burn, back to pre-burn by 3–10 yr; another found more use for up to 15 yr — [FRAMES catalog 18056](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/18056), [FRAMES 51365](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/51365). I could not open these to confirm.

**Cuts and forage succession**
- In Alberta, forage biomass peaked at ~9 yr after logging. In NW Washington, the most nutritious deer forage rose for ~14 yr post-harvest (studies cited in the introduction) — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)
- [boreal-parkland, Manitoba] Elk were **displaced** from forestry cut-blocks: 3.5 elk locations/ha against 5.25 random/ha at 100 m, with displacement at all distances 100–1,000 m. Cut-blocks were still used (3,526 locations inside), most in Dec, Jan, May and Aug. Forage crops were used most in Mar, Apr and Dec. Cropland use peaked in Apr and **Nov**. Grassland/rangeland use peaked in Dec and Feb. Mixedwood forest use peaked in May and **Sep** — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)
- [winter, Alberta foothills HSI] Feeding sites had less tree cover, fewer conifers, lower tree height, lower stem density and more grass than random sites, and were closer to seismic lines (Jones 1997). Food suitability: non-forest canopy < 6% optimal. Forest canopy 6–20% optimal, falling to 0 at 80%. Stands ≥ 50% deciduous optimal. Fall and winter diets shift to more forbs and shrubs, but grass is preferred when available — [Beck et al. / FRI Research, Elk Winter Foraging HSI, 1999](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf)
- [Ya Ha Tinda, Alberta] Elk were most likely to forage in areas with **intermediate** herbaceous biomass and low movement costs. They were most likely to be relocating in high wolf-use areas, near linear clearings and when moving upslope (Frair, Merrill et al.; abstract-level summary) — [Frair et al., indexed at U. Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/277906)

**Fields, mines and grassland (eastern and parkland herds)**
- [Appalachia, SW Virginia, all seasons including Sep–Nov "breeding"] 33 GPS adult females, 2019–2022. **Reclaimed surface mines** were the strongest selection in all seasons and scales (distance-to β = −0.411 to −0.779, i.e. closer is better). Conifer forest selected (β −0.231 to −0.301). Mixed hardwood (β +0.070 to +0.340) and oak (+0.143 to +0.170) avoided. Unmined pasture and hay were selected only in mid and late gestation (winter–spring) — [Multiscale resource selection for a reintroduced elk population (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13072265/)
- [Virginia, 59 GPS females, 104,174 fixes] Smaller home ranges were tied to more non-forested habitat. Each 5% more mixed hardwood added 93.9–284.2 ha to the home range, and each 5% more open land took away 48.5–204.7 ha. The Sep–Nov "harem" season home range was 694.1 ± 45.2 ha — [Drivers of habitat quality for a reintroduced elk herd (PMC9723100)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723100/)
- [Kentucky] Early research found elk "primarily used reclaimed surface mines for feeding, and used the surrounding intact timberlands for thermal and escape cover" (Olsson et al. 2007). Recently reclaimed sites (wheat, rye, clover) are favoured over older ones dominated by sericea lespedeza and tall fescue. Newer GPS data show some elk spending "a significant portion of time in forested habitat" — [KDFWR Elk Management Plan 2015–2030](https://fw.ky.gov/Hunt/Documents/20152030ElkManagementPlan.pdf)
- [Wisconsin, Fall Sep–Nov] 79 GPS elk (2017–2020), 72,965 fixes. At the landscape scale in fall, elk selected **less canopy cover (β = −0.39)**. Canopy was avoided at all scales and seasons (β −0.25 to −0.50). Elk selected areas closer to escape cover, defined as **≥ 40% canopy** (β −0.12 to −0.20). Early-successional grassland and crop rotations were selected at the home-range scale — [Merems et al. 2024, Ecol. Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11445449/)
- [fall archery, Montana] Elk "maintained or increased selection for areas with higher forage quality" during archery season whatever their hunting risk. Low-risk elk had the best nutrition, from irrigated agriculture on private land. Adequate forage quality for adult females = **2.75 kcal/g DE** (Cook et al. 2004). Mean available forage quality was 2.78–2.81 kcal/g — [DeVoe et al. 2019, JWM](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/wildlife-reports/elk/devoe.etal.2019.pdf)

**Distance from cover / edge**
- Foraging "often occurs within 200 m of cover" (Thomas et al. 1979; Smith 1985). Cover should be "no greater than 180 m from a foraging area" (Smith 1985) — [FRI Elk Winter Foraging HSI](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf)
- Westside regional model [summer]: elk preferred areas **close to cover-forage edges**. Cover = any 30 m cell with ≥ 40% canopy and > 2 m stand height, in patches of ≥ 3 × 3 cells. A search summary of the monograph says most use fell within **1.6 km** of a cover-forage edge (I could not open the full text to confirm the number) — [Rowland et al. 2018, Wildl. Monogr. 199](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wmon.1033); [Westside user guide](https://data.fs.usda.gov/research/pnw/tools/westside-elk-modeling/WestsideElkModelUserGuide.pdf)
- Ruprecht et al. note that Wisdom et al. (1986) and Thomas et al. (1988) "documented higher elk use in forage areas closer to cover", and that Hammond (1980) found large clearcuts' forage benefit "negated" by elk's unwillingness to venture far from cover — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use a **time-of-day switch** for opening cells (burns, cuts, meadows, fields, mines). At night and in twilight, score them high. In daylight, score them neutral to negative (strongly negative for burns 6–12 yr and cuts up to ~28 yr). This is the single most replicated fall-relevant pattern (Spitz 2018; Ruprecht 2023). Grassland was selected even by day in October at Starkey, so daytime opening use may rise again in fall as heat eases.
- Burn age curve (prescribed fire, Starkey): rises from year 1, peaks at about 5, back to baseline by about 15. Wildfire is used years 2–3 in early autumn; a reasonable assumption is a similar shape stretched by fire severity. Cut age curve: rises to a peak at about 9–14 yr (Alberta biomass ~9; Starkey selection 14), long tail to 26–33 yr. For boreal Ontario, Manitoba gives a counter-signal: at the landscape scale elk avoided cut-blocks. Treat cuts as night forage near cover, not as a daytime draw.
- Edge-distance rule for forage cells: full value within ~0–200 m of ≥ 40% canopy cover, decaying beyond. Steeper decay by day and during hunting season.
- Forage quality (DE ≥ 2.75 kcal/g) is the threshold the PNW nutrition models treat as "adequate". If a forage-quality proxy exists (deciduous/shrub share, herb cover, young disturbance), weight it above canopy for feeding cells.

### Gaps
- No fall-specific GPS study of distance-to-edge decay (metres) by day against night was found. The 180–200 m figures are 1979–1985 guidelines, not modern RSF effect sizes.
- A claim that "elk use of openings is concentrated within 80 m of a forest edge" and one that Kentucky elk "rarely use open habitats more than 300 m from a forest edge" both surfaced in search summaries. I could not trace either to a primary study. The 300 m figure in the Kentucky plan refers to *hunters* distance from roads (Lyon & Burcham 1998), not elk.
- No fall study on **aspen** specifically or on shrubfields by age was found, beyond Manitoba's selection for deciduous forest and the Alberta HSI's ≥ 50% deciduous optimum.

## 3. Travel: saddles, benches, ridgelines, drainages; daily movement distances and timing

### Takeaway
Activity is crepuscular: the morning peak comes around or up to ~2 h after civil dawn, and the evening peak at the end of civil dusk. Daily habitat cycles are strongest in **spring and autumn**. Saddles and ridges show up only in winter and summer range models (Idaho), and ridgetops in Appalachia. I found no fall travel-corridor study with effect sizes for benches or drainages.

### Cited Findings
- [Starkey, 6 yr, female elk and mule deer] "Both elk and deer exhibited strong daily and seasonal patterns of movements and habitat use, with daily cycles being most pronounced during spring and autumn". The cycles were "composed of crepuscular and intraday habitat transitions", and were more pronounced for elk than deer. Crepuscular transitions "were accompanied by sharp increases in velocity" while intraday habitat changes were not (abstract) — [Ager et al. 2003, J. Mammal. 84:1076](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/journal-of-mammalogy/volume-84/issue-3/BBa-020/DAILY-AND-SEASONAL-MOVEMENTS-AND-HABITAT-USE-BY-FEMALE-ROCKY/10.1644/BBa-020.full)
- [Alberta, 46 GPS elk (31 F, 15 M), ~35,000 km²] The morning activity peak fell "almost two hours after civil twilight" and the evening peak "at the end of civil twilight". Canadian elk were more active in summer, and the crepuscular profile was weakest in winter. Peaks held stable timing relative to twilight across the year, suggesting light cues drive them directly rather than a strong internal clock — [Ensing et al. 2014, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160215/)
- [Starkey] Selection for burns switched "near dusk and dawn", but the strongest selection was **nocturnal**, not at crepuscular peaks — [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf). Before harvest (May–Oct), elk avoided grasslands by day, selected them in the evening, were indifferent through the night and avoided them by morning — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)
- [winter, Idaho] Elk selected terrain positions on **slopes and saddles** over valley bottoms (quadratic; "extreme ridges are slightly less preferred"). Slope selection rose linearly from 0 to 20% and peaked at 45% (Bitterroot), or favoured 1–35° (Idaho Batholith). [summer] Elk selected "slopes, saddles, and ridges relative to valley bottoms or ravine topographies" — [IDFG 2016](https://fishandgame.idaho.gov/ifwis/idnhp/cdc_pdf/Elk%20Season%20Range%20in%20Idaho%20Version%202.pdf)
- [Appalachia] Female elk selected **ridgetops** (TPI) and lower terrain roughness in all seasons. Roughness was the strongest topographic effect (β −0.276 to −0.363). Slope was dropped because it was collinear with roughness (r = 0.976) — [PMC13072265](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13072265/)
- [Wisconsin] Moderate-to-steep slopes were weakly selected (β 0.04–0.08), with S/W aspects favoured — [Merems et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11445449/)
- [Ya Ha Tinda] Moving upslope is energetically costly, and elk were more likely to be relocating (travelling) there — [Frair et al.](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/277906)
- Movement and range: Duck Mountains cow movements "increased in the spring, declined in June with a gradual increase from July to October". The 100% MCP was 127.85 km², the 95% kernel 58.24 km² and the 50% kernel 7.29 km². Most elk moved from farmland into the forest during the rut and fall hunting seasons — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content). In the Glass Mountains of Texas (desert), GPS bulls moved an average of 1.8 mi (~2.9 km)/day — [Borderlands Research Institute](https://bri.sulross.edu/big-game/elk-range-size-and-movements)
- Rut timing: in the Duck Mountains (MB), the mean estimated breeding date was **27 September**. The thesis cites a peak breeding period of 23 Sep–7 Oct, a rut of ~41 days for mature bulls and ~71 days for yearling bulls (Raedeke et al. 2002) — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)

### Inferences
- Activity curve: peaks centred about 0–2 h after civil dawn and around the end of civil dusk, with high night activity in openings and a midday trough in cover. Allow the dawn peak to fall later in northern and Canadian herds (Ensing 2014).
- Travel-cell rule: favour low roughness and gentle-to-moderate grades (energy cost), and saddles and mid-slope or ridge positions over valley-bottom ravines. Favour the paths between day-cover cells and night-forage cells (the "commute"). This is inferred from the habitat switch, not measured as corridors.

### Gaps
- Ager et al. 2003 velocities (m/h by hour, autumn), bedding hours and daily distances could not be read: the full text was bot-blocked.
- No study found quantifying elk use of **benches**, **heads of drainages** or **finger ridges** as travel or bedding features in fall.
- No fall GPS daily-distance figures by sex during the rut in forested systems were found (only the Texas desert number).

## 4. Cover: hiding vs thermal, and canopy thresholds in habitat models

### Takeaway
The 1979 Blue Mountains definitions (hiding cover hides 90% of a standing elk at ≤ 61 m; thermal cover is conifer ≥ 12 m tall with > 70% crown closure; 40:60 cover:forage) were **overturned for thermal cover** by Cook et al. 1998. In penned elk over 4 winters and 2 summers, thermal cover gave no condition benefit, and dense cover in winter was worse. Modern models use cover as **security/edge** (≥ 40% canopy) and forage quality, not as thermal value.

### Cited Findings
- Thomas et al. 1979 definitions, as cited: hiding cover = "vegetation capable of hiding 90% of a standing adult elk at 61 m or less". Optimal thermal cover = conifer or conifer-dominated mixedwood 10–12 m tall with > 70% canopy closure. A 60:40 forage-to-cover ratio was considered optimum. Thermal cover patches should be at least 4 ha (Wisdom et al. 1986) — [FRI Elk Winter Foraging HSI](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf). "Satisfactory thermal cover ... a stand of coniferous trees at least 12 m (40 ft) tall and exceeding an average of 70 percent crown closure", with a Blue Mountains optimum of 40% cover and 60% forage (search summary of the same literature) — [USFS document](https://www.fs.usda.gov/media/168576)
- **Cook et al. 1998** (Wildlife Monographs 141), Blue Mountains, NE Oregon. Four cover treatments, each replicated 3 times in 12 pens: **zero** (clearcut), **moderate** (40–70% canopy), **dense** (> 70% multilayered) and **combination** (clearcut plus dense). Winter trials ran early Dec–mid-Mar (1992–95) and summer trials late May–mid-Sep. "No significant positive effect of thermal cover was found on condition of elk during any of four winter-long experiments and two summer-long experiments." In winter, dense cover "provided the most costly energetic" environment, with significantly greater overwinter weight loss, more fat catabolism and (in one winter) mortality than the clearcut units. In summer there was "no indication that elk performance was influenced in any way by forest cover treatments, despite temperatures significantly above normal both summers". Recommendation: thermal cover emphasis "should be relatively low in most climatological settings" — [PNW Science Findings 22 (Feb 2000) on Cook et al. 1998](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/4662.pdf); [Treesearch abstract](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/4662)
- Modern canopy thresholds in models:
  - Westside (Rowland et al. 2018): cover = ≥ 40% canopy and > 2 m stand height, in ≥ 3 × 3 cell patches — [Rowland et al. 2018](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wmon.1033)
  - Montana archery study: "distance to cover" = distance to canopy ≥ 40%, and "security patches" = contiguous ≥ 30% canopy — [DeVoe et al. 2019](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/wildlife-reports/elk/devoe.etal.2019.pdf)
  - Wisconsin: escape cover = ≥ 40% canopy — [Merems et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11445449/)
  - Ranglack et al. (SW Montana, fall): selection rose with canopy but hit a **pseudothreshold at 13% canopy** on public land in archery season. Their recommended security areas were 13% canopy at ≥ 2,760 m from motorized routes (archery), and 9% canopy at ≥ 1,535 m and ≥ 5,000 acres (rifle). Their cutoffs compare with the traditional "dense forest canopy (e.g. 40%)" Hillis paradigm — [Ranglack et al. 2017 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/fall-elk-resource-selection---final-report.pdf)
  - Idaho summer ranges: selection for canopy (averaged within 2 km) "increases dramatically at 33 to 42%, plateaus and then decreases beyond 65%" — [IDFG 2016](https://fishandgame.idaho.gov/ifwis/idnhp/cdc_pdf/Elk%20Season%20Range%20in%20Idaho%20Version%202.pdf)
- Note on conflicting thresholds: a search summary of Ranglack et al. 2017 (JWM 81:778) quoted security standards of "canopy cover ≥ 23–60% and distance from motorized routes ≥ 1,846–3,679 m" (75% and 50% of locations). The MFWP final report I read gives 13% canopy / 2,760 m (archery) and 9% / 1,535 m (rifle). The journal version may report different cutoffs. I could not open it — [JWM abstract page](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jwmg.21258)
- Cook et al. 1998 suggest observed cover selection "might be related more to other habitat needs such as forage availability or security" (summary) — [USFS](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/4662)

### Inferences
- Treat canopy as **security and day-bed habitat** with a saturating curve: rising from ~10–15% and nearing full value around 40–55%. Use ≥ 40% as the "cover" class for edge distance. Do not give dense (> 70%) conifer a winter "thermal" bonus; Cook 1998 suggests it can cost energy in winter.
- The Thomas 1979 hiding-cover definition (90% concealment at 61 m) lines up with a LiDAR understorey or stem-density metric, if one exists, better than with canopy closure.

### Gaps
- I found no measured threshold for hiding cover (understorey density) linked to elk fall selection beyond the 1979 definition.
- Wildlife Monographs 141 full text (exact canopy percentages per pen, temperatures) was not retrieved. The figures above come from the PNW Science Findings summary of that work.

## 5. Water and wallows

### Takeaway
Distance to water is a weak or neutral predictor of elk fall site selection in most studies. Elk sometimes avoid riparian strips by day in summer, yet cross them often. I found almost no peer-reviewed data on wallow placement. The one study I found measured how often wallows were visited, not where they sit.

### Cited Findings
- [summer beds, SD] Distance to water did not differ between bed sites and random plots — [Millspaugh et al. 1998](https://www.originalwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/bsk-pdf-manager/2019/04/Millspaugh-et-al_1998_Summer-bed-sites-of-elk-in-the-Black-Hills-South-Dakota.pdf)
- [summer, Starkey] Elk "consistently avoided ... riparian areas" (sites ≤ 100 m from streams), with the effect varying by time and season — [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- [Manitoba, year-round] Elk were "not found within 200 m of a water feature more often than random" and positioned themselves "more commonly between 400 and 900 metres of water features". Yet **> 38% of elk travel paths** fell within 100 m of water — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)
- [summer, Manitoba forest-resident elk, May–Jun] Marsh/fen was selected (selection ratio 1.90) and water had a positive ratio (1.59) — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)
- Wallows: 39 wallows were monitored 5 Aug–14 Oct 2005, and elk visited 20. Observations: 22 male-only wallowing events and 374 further naso-oral contact events by males and females. A commonly quoted summary says wallows are "used an average of one or two times a season" (abstract) — [Conner et al. 2007, J. Wildl. Dis. 43:784](https://bioone.org/journals/journal-of-wildlife-diseases/volume-43/issue-4/0090-3558-43.4.784/Elk-Use-of-Wallows-and-Potential-Chronic-Wasting-Disease-Transmission/10.7589/0090-3558-43.4.784.full)

### Inferences
- Water: a small or zero weight. A mild positive within ~100 m for travel cells (crossings), and no bonus for bedding next to streams. In northern boreal areas water is rarely limiting in fall.
- Wallows: only seeps, springs and wet depressions in or near cover can be scored, and as a rut-period (Sep–early Oct) bonus for bull sign, not a habitat driver. The evidence is folklore-grade apart from Conner 2007's visit counts.

### Gaps
- No study found on wallow site characteristics (seeps or springs, canopy, slope position, distance to bedding) or on how far bulls travel to wallows.
- Seeps and springs as fall elk attractors: no quantitative source found.

## 6. Seasonal elevation shifts and snow

### Takeaway
Fall downslope movement is cued by **snow accumulation plus hunting pressure**, not by date. Deep snow (> 40 cm) pushes elk to south slopes and lower ground. In Montana, elk began leaving high-snowpack districts during backcountry rifle season, with SWE effects of β −0.84 and later −3.96. Migration timing is plastic: Greater Yellowstone winter-range arrival is now about 50 days later than in 2001.

### Cited Findings
- [Greater Yellowstone, > 400 GPS elk, 9 herds, 2001–2017] Departure from summer range and arrival on winter range were "both influenced by snow accumulation and exposure to hunting". Winter-range arrival dates have become "almost 50 days later since 2001" — [Rickbeil et al. 2019, Global Change Biology via USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/plasticity-elk-migration-timing-response-changing-environmental-conditions); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/06/190614141246.htm)
- [fall, western Montana] Elk "started leaving hunting districts that had higher snowpack (SWE β = −0.84)" in the backcountry rifle period, and avoided high snowpack in the rifle period (β = −3.96). In late autumn, snowpack and hunter pressure became "the primary factors" — [Snobl et al. 2024](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/16---snobl.etal.2023.pdf)
- [fall, SW Montana] Mean elevation of elk locations was 6,903 ft (~2,104 m) in archery season against 6,578 ft (~2,005 m) in rifle season. Mean SWE at rifle-season locations was 1.09 in (SD 1.13) — [Ranglack et al. 2017 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/fall-elk-resource-selection---final-report.pdf)
- "Periods of deep snow (> 40 cm) result in elk moving to habitats of high forage availability and low snow cover such as south-facing slopes" (Irwin & Peek 1983) — [FRI Elk Winter HSI](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf)
- [winter range models, Idaho, SNODAS average max depth] Bitterroot: selection rose at low depths, peaked roughly 18–50 in, and fell to no selection at 90 in. Batholith: selection peaked near 11 in and declined to none at 63 in. Elevation: selection rose from 750 to 1,500 m and fell above 2,000 m. South aspects were selected. Winter ranges are "predominantly driven by snow depth and the availability of winter forage" — [IDFG 2016](https://fishandgame.idaho.gov/ifwis/idnhp/cdc_pdf/Elk%20Season%20Range%20in%20Idaho%20Version%202.pdf). (These are long-term maxima at winter-range scale, not depth thresholds for movement.)
- Managers' observations (not peer-reviewed): "two feet [~61 cm] seems to be the threshold where large groups begin to at least consider migrating downhill". Mature elk move "without major difficulty in loose snow up to 3.3 feet [~100 cm]", and more than 4 ft hinders them. Migration can happen with < 6 in of snow if there is extreme cold and wind. Bigger bulls are often found in deeper snow than cows and calves (≤ 2 ft) — [RMEF](https://www.rmef.org/media/what-is-the-deepest-snow-an-elk-will-put-up-with/)
- [northern Montana winter] "Elk use of north slopes in deep snow was observed during portions of all three winters, and this use lasted until a hard crust formed" (search summary) — [U. Montana thesis](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=8034&context=etd)
- [summer, Starkey] Elk generally selected higher elevations, using lower elevations only by day in midsummer — [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- Eastern and parkland herds: the Kentucky herd "has not displayed any large-scale migratory tendencies"; isolated family groups moved ~9 mi between seasonal ranges — [KDFWR plan](https://fw.ky.gov/Hunt/Documents/20152030ElkManagementPlan.pdf). Duck Mountains snow cover runs typically 1 Nov–1 Apr. Elk moved from farmland into the forest in the rut and hunting season, and used farm forage crops and cropland in Nov–Dec — [Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)

### Inferences
- Snow modifier for a cell (needs a snow-depth or SWE input): neutral below ~20–25 cm. From ~40 cm, shift weight to south aspects, wind-scoured or low-snow ground and lower elevations. Heavily penalise ~60 cm+ for herds (group migration) and ~100 cm+ for all elk.
- In flat or low-relief boreal Ontario and Quebec, elevation bands matter little. Snow mostly acts through aspect, canopy interception and access to forage (and farm fields where present).

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed movement-threshold study with metric snow depths (e.g. the "40 cm avoid / 70 cm stop" figures in search summaries could not be traced). The ScienceDirect chapter on elk winter selection in severe snowpack (Yellowstone) was paywalled — [chapter](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/abs/pii/S193679610800208X)
- No region-general "September vs November elevation band" table exists; this is herd-specific.

## 7. Weather: heat, cold fronts, rain, wind, pressure, moon (evidence against folklore)

### Takeaway
**Heat** has real evidence. Elk move into canopy and north aspects by day and shift feeding to night as temperature rises. Bed microsites run about 5 °C cooler than ambient at ~30 °C. Penned elk showed no condition cost when denied cover in hot summers, though, so this is behaviour, not need. **Snow** is real (§6). **Barometric pressure, moon phase, cold fronts and rain** have no peer-reviewed elk evidence I could find. Treat them as folklore or untested.

### Cited Findings
- Elk bedded at air temperatures of 15–36 °C. Ambient air (30.3 °C) was significantly warmer than bed microsites (24.8 °C). North aspects and canopy > 54% were selected [summer] — [Millspaugh et al. 1998](https://www.originalwisdom.com/wp-content/uploads/bsk-pdf-manager/2019/04/Millspaugh-et-al_1998_Summer-bed-sites-of-elk-in-the-Black-Hills-South-Dakota.pdf)
- [Starkey] Daytime selection of closed forest grew stronger "coinciding with the progression of hot summer months". Elk "feed nocturnally in hot summer months", with the open-by-night and cover-by-day pattern attributed to thermoregulation, predation risk and/or human disturbance (not separable) — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf); [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- In an arid desert, elk "selected areas that reduced costs of thermoregulation over those that provided the highest-quality forage". In a temperate montane forest (Starkey) they selected high-quality forage over thermal savings. The poorest-condition desert elk showed the strongest thermal selection — [Long et al. 2014, Ecol. Monogr.](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/47508)
- In summer, elk lose heat mainly by sweating (cutaneous evaporation "significantly greater in elk than mule deer"), while deer rely on panting — [Parker & Robbins 1984, Can. J. Zool.](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/z84-202); [RMEF summary](https://rmef.org/elk-network/elk-sweat-pant)
- Thermal constraints on cover-type use and activity time of elk were studied by Merrill (1991, Appl. Anim. Behav. Sci.). Merrill reported elk compensating for disturbance by more nocturnal feeding — [Merrill 1991](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/016815919190252S); [as cited in Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf)
- Penned elk showed no summer performance response to cover "despite temperatures significantly above normal both summers"; "other researchers have found elk to be surprisingly tolerant of high summer temperatures" — [PNW Science Findings on Cook et al. 1998](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/4662.pdf)
- Cold and wind: older sources say "elk may take refuge in heavy timber when temperatures drop and wind-chill increases" (Beall 1974) and bed in dense stands on southern exposures in Idaho winters (Irwin & Peek 1983) — [FRI HSI](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf). But Cook 1998 found dense cover energetically **worse** in winter.
- Moon: a USDA-ARS GPS trial at Starkey compared 10 collared cow elk in 17 ha pastures over full-moon and new-moon weeks (summer and early fall 2008–09). The abstract reports **no results** — [USDA-ARS publication 246688](https://www.ars.usda.gov/research/publications/publication/?seqNo115=246688)
- Harvest vulnerability: in Utah (255 GPS cows, 2015–2017, hunts mid-Aug to 31 Jan), harvest risk depended on distance to roads, elevation (survival lower at higher elevation) and distance to trees. **Weather was not tested** — [Utah cow elk harvest vulnerability (PMC7682858)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7682858/)
- Barometric pressure and rain: claims such as "a rapidly rising barometer is one of the single best predictors of intense deer and elk movement", or that elk go to cover early in heavy rain, appear in hunting media and forums (e.g. [goHUNT](https://www.gohunt.com/content/skills/elk/how-weather-and-moon-phases-affect-elk-behavior)) with no cited elk data. In white-tailed deer, temperature beat other weather variables in one Mississippi State study (per [MeatEater](https://www.themeateater.com/wired-to-hunt/whitetail-hunting/does-barometric-pressure-affect-deer-movement)).

### Inferences
- Heat rule (from summer evidence and the documented 5 °C microsite gap): as daytime air temperature rises above roughly the mid-teens °C, raise the daytime weight on canopy ≥ 50% and north aspects, and lower the daytime weight on open cells. Move feeding further into night and late dusk. Early-September archery days in the West often hit 20–30 °C, so this matters most then. It matters little in Nov–Dec. The exact onset temperature is a modelling choice, not a measured threshold.
- Cool weather: drop the north-aspect bonus and allow sun-facing and open daytime use. Ruprecht saw daytime grassland selection in October.
- Pressure, moon, rain and fronts: keep any weight near zero, or label it "hunter lore" in the UI. No elk evidence supports a direction or size.

### Gaps
- No primary upper-critical-temperature value for elk in fall coat was retrievable. A "+20 °C operative temperature" UCT for winter-coated elk appeared in a search summary but could not be traced to a primary source.
- No elk study on wind speed effects on habitat selection or activity was reached (my web search budget ran out before this topic). The wind-driven scent and thermals part of the scoring will have to lean on general ungulate and deer work, or on the moose science already in the app.

## 8. Existing elk habitat models: variables and weights

### Takeaway
The current PNW standard (Rowland et al. 2018 Westside, and the parallel Blue Mountains models) uses **four** covariates: mean digestible dietary energy (DDE within a 350 m radius), distance to open roads, distance to the cover-forage edge, and slope. Nutrition (DDE) is the core term. It replaced the 1980s HEI models (Thomas 1979/1988; Wisdom 1986), which rested on size and spacing of cover and forage, roads, cover quality and forage. Their thermal-cover term was later discredited.

### Cited Findings
- **Westside (Rowland et al. 2018, Wildl. Monogr. 199)**: summer ranges over 11 million ha of western OR and WA. The best regional habitat-use model had 4 covariates: **DDE, distance to nearest road open to public motorized use, distance to cover-forage edge, and % slope**. Elk preferred high DDE, far from roads, close to cover-forage edges and on gentle slopes — [Rowland et al. 2018](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wmon.1033); [USFS Treesearch 57491](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/57491)
- Westside implementation details: DDE is averaged **within a 350 m radius** of each 30 m pixel. Unclassified habitat gets a default DDE of 2.825 kcal/g. Elk respond to open roads "typically not beyond ~2.5 miles" (4 km buffer). DDE classes: Poor < 2.40, Low-marginal 2.40–2.58, High-marginal > 2.58–2.75, Low-good > 2.75–2.83, High-good > 2.83–2.90, Excellent > 2.90 kcal/g (modified from Cook et al. 2004). Inputs are canopy cover, stand height and hardwood %, at 30 m (LiDAR canopy and height supported) — [Westside user guide](https://data.fs.usda.gov/research/pnw/tools/westside-elk-modeling/WestsideElkModelUserGuide.pdf)
- **Blue Mountains nutrition and habitat-use models** (successor to the 1986 Blue Mountains model): built "to measure dietary digestible energy, distance to roads, distance to cover and slope" (search summary of the project page) — [USFS PNW Blue Mountains project](https://research.fs.usda.gov/pnw/projects/bluemountainselknutritionandhabitatuse)
- **HEI (Thomas et al. 1988, PNW-GTR-218)**, Blue Mountains winter ranges: the index rests on "size and spacing of cover and forage areas, roads open to traffic per unit of area, cover quality, and quantity and quality of forage". Field application was hard because of detailed "spacing band" calculations — [Treesearch 5624](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/5624)
- 1986 Blue Mountains model (Wisdom et al. 1986) was adopted by BLM, USFS, ODFW and WDFW — [search summary, research.fs.usda.gov](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/20589.pdf)
- **Idaho seasonal-range Maxent (IDFG 2016)** ranks variables by permutation importance. Summer (Bitterroot): canopy within 2 km > non-forest NDVI sum within 2 km > deciduous shrub within 2 km > TPI > fire within 2 km > excluded land uses. Winter: average maximum snow depth > evergreen forest within 400 m > deciduous shrub within 400 m > TPI (500 m) > slope > aspect — [IDFG 2016](https://fishandgame.idaho.gov/ifwis/idnhp/cdc_pdf/Elk%20Season%20Range%20in%20Idaho%20Version%202.pdf)
- **Fall hunting-season RSF (Ranglack et al. 2017, SW Montana)**, ordered by strength. Archery: not publicly accessible land > time-integrated NDVI > canopy > distance to motorized routes > lower hunter effort. Rifle: inaccessible land > distance to motorized routes > canopy > hunter effort — [Ranglack et al. 2017 report](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/fall-elk-resource-selection---final-report.pdf)
- **Starkey RSFs** (Spitz 2018) covariates: burn/treatment, canopy cover %, cattle, eastness, northness, elevation, fir dominance, distance to open road, slope, soil depth, riparian (≤ 100 m of streams), all at 30 m with day/night interactions — [Spitz et al. 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf). Ruprecht 2023 added cosine of aspect, ln(distance to permanent water), ln(distance to admin road) and grassland — [Ruprecht et al. 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)
- **Alberta foothills winter foraging HSI**: food suitability = tree canopy (non-forest < 6% optimal; forest 6–20% optimal, 0 at 80%), deciduous share (≥ 50% optimal) and grass cover. Cover is assumed not limiting in that region — [FRI HSI 1999](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf)

### Inferences
- A Groundwind elk habitat score that mirrors the validated Westside structure would combine: a forage-quality proxy averaged within ~350 m (from stand age, deciduous share, disturbance age, openings); distance to the cover-forage edge (cover = ≥ 40% canopy and > 2 m); slope (negative); and roads (from the other researcher). Add a day/night switch and the burn and cut age curves (§2), which the Westside summer model lacks.
- Westside coefficients are not reproduced here (full text not retrieved). Weights would need refitting or hand-calibration.

### Gaps
- Coefficient values and relative weights for the Westside and Blue Mountains models were not retrieved (Wiley 403).
- The HEI functional forms (HEs, HEr, HEc, HEf curves and how they combine, often cited as a geometric mean) were not confirmed from a primary source.
- No published Ontario or Quebec elk habitat model was found.

## 9. Regional differences: Rocky Mountain West, Pacific Northwest, Canadian foothills/parkland/boreal, restored eastern herds

### Takeaway
The building blocks are the same everywhere: forage in openings or young disturbance, close to ≥ 40% cover, gentle terrain, away from roads. What drives each region differs. **Western mountains**: elevation migration cued by snow and hunting, plus security. **PNW westside**: forage quality (DDE) in closed conifer. **Parkland/boreal**: deciduous (aspen) forest plus farm fields, with cut-blocks used less than random. **Eastern restored herds**: reclaimed-mine grasslands and ridgetops, conifer and forest for cover, no migration.

### Cited Findings
- **Rocky Mountain West (MT, ID, OR interior)**: migration timing set by snow and hunting ([Rickbeil 2019](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/plasticity-elk-migration-timing-response-changing-environmental-conditions)); fall selection dominated by access, canopy and NDVI ([Ranglack 2017](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/fall-elk-resource-selection---final-report.pdf)); burns used early in fall ([Snobl 2024](https://fwp.mt.gov/binaries/content/assets/fwp/conservation/elk/research/16---snobl.etal.2023.pdf)); day/night commuting into burns and cuts ([Spitz 2018](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/journals/pnw_2018_spitz001.pdf); [Ruprecht 2023](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/66581.pdf)).
- **Pacific Northwest westside**: nutrition-limited summer range. DDE is the core model term, with cover-forage edge, slope and roads ([Rowland 2018](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wmon.1033)).
- **Arid West**: thermal costs outweigh forage in habitat choice ([Long 2014](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/47508)).
- **Canadian foothills (Alberta)**: grasslands and meadows used for winter foraging more than available. Boreal mixedwood elk lean more on browse than foothill and mountain elk (Nietfeld et al. 1985) ([FRI HSI](https://friresearch.ca/sites/default/files/null/HSP_1999_10_Rpt_ElkWinterForaging.pdf)). Wolves and linear clearings shape movement ([Frair et al.](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/277906)). Dawn activity peak ~2 h after civil twilight ([Ensing 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4160215/)).
- **Manitoba boreal-parkland (Duck Mountains)**: deciduous forest selected at all scales. In the rut (6 Sep–19 Oct) there was no significant selection, but deciduous forest had SR 1.23 and roads were avoided (SR 0.43). Elk moved from farmland into forest for the rut and hunting season. Forage crops and cropland were used in Nov–Dec. Cut-blocks were avoided at the landscape scale ([Chranowski 2009](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/09896ecc-9e0d-420c-9501-56060913bd3e/content)). Riding Mountain elk also select deciduous forest (Blood 1966; Rounds 1981; Brook 2006, as cited there).
- **Wisconsin (Great Lakes, northern hardwood/conifer)**: fall canopy avoided (β −0.39), proximity to ≥ 40% escape cover selected, S/W aspects and early-successional openings selected, wolf territory centres avoided at the landscape scale (β 0.39–0.53) ([Merems 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11445449/)).
- **Appalachia (KY, VA; PA similar)**: reclaimed mines are the selection hub all year; conifers selected; oak and mixed hardwood avoided; ridgetops and low roughness selected ([PMC13072265](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13072265/); [PMC9723100](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723100/); [KDFWR plan](https://fw.ky.gov/Hunt/Documents/20152030ElkManagementPlan.pdf)). Pennsylvania's herd of 800–900 is centred on reclaimed strip mines amid large forests (search summary of [PGC Elk Habitat Management](https://www.pa.gov/spdocsothers/Wildlife/HabitatManagement/Documents/Elk%20Habitat%20Management.pdf)).
- **Ontario**: 443 elk from Elk Island National Park, Alberta, were released at 4 central-Ontario sites in 1998–2001, giving ~800 elk province-wide by 2013. A study of the 1940s French River–Burwash herds found elk foraging on ridge, mixed-forest and grassland habitats, with **woody browse the dominant forage** (search summary) — [Rosatte 2016, Can. Field-Nat.](https://www.canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/cfn/index.php/cfn/article/download/1842/1804)

### Inferences
- For Groundwind's likely first elk areas (central and northern Ontario, possibly Quebec), the closest analogues are **Wisconsin, Manitoba and Ontario** herds, not Montana. Use deciduous and mixedwood forest as cover and browse. Use openings (fields, old farms, regen, wetlands margins) as night forage near ≥ 40% cover. Expect no elevation migration, and a snow effect through aspect and canopy. Give burns and cuts a night bonus but no daytime draw.
- Browse matters more in boreal and Ontario elk diets than in western grass-dominated systems, so deciduous shrub and young hardwood regen should count as forage (as the Alberta HSI and Idaho shrub terms do).

### Gaps
- No Ontario, Quebec or Michigan GPS resource-selection study was reached (Michigan not covered at all). Ontario-specific fall habitat effect sizes are a gap.
- A Pennsylvania vegetation-at-GPS-locations study (39 collared elk, 1,294 used against 2,509 random, Apr 2021–Aug 2022, seasonal conditional logistic regression; "elk consistently select early successional open canopy and forest habitats that provide herbaceous forage") appeared in search summaries. Its exact citation and region were not confirmed — [possibly Dryad dataset 10.5061/dryad.18931zd68](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.18931zd68)
- Yukon elk (introduced herds near Whitehorse) were not researched.

### Revisions to older findings (flagged)
- **Thermal cover** (Thomas et al. 1979: ≥ 12 m conifer, > 70% canopy; 40:60 cover:forage) was revised by **Cook et al. 1998**: no condition benefit, and dense cover was worse in winter. Later models drop thermal cover for nutrition plus cover-forage edge — [PNW Science Findings](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/4662.pdf); [Rowland 2018](https://wildlife.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wmon.1033)
- **HEI (1988)** was largely superseded in the PNW by the Westside (2018) and Blue Mountains nutrition and habitat-use models — [Westside user guide](https://data.fs.usda.gov/research/pnw/tools/westside-elk-modeling/WestsideElkModelUserGuide.pdf)
- **"Elk are crepuscular feeders"** is refined by Starkey long-term data, which show the most important foraging hours in openings are nocturnal in summer (Spitz 2018; Ruprecht 2023).
- **Short-lived burn and cut benefit** (older 1–5 yr claims) is revised: prescribed-burn nocturnal selection lasts ~15 yr (Spitz 2018), and logging benefit peaks at 14 yr and lasts 26–33 yr (Ruprecht 2023). Daytime avoidance lasts nearly as long.
- **Migration by calendar** is revised: Greater Yellowstone winter-range arrival is now ~50 days later than in 2001, driven by snow and hunting (Rickbeil 2019).
