# HuntOS: how we hunt here

The field guide behind the app. One place for what we know about hunting
moose (and the rest) out of the Pickle Lake camp: how the weather moves
them, how a hunt is laid out over the days in camp, what to do with sign
and with a bull that answers, and how the party works together. The app
draws the map and the wind; this is the thinking it is built on, written
so it reads at the kitchen table without a phone in hand.

Where a line comes from a study it is marked [S]; from guides and hunters
[H]; from our own hunts [us]. The science and the numbers the app uses
are in `HUNT-FISH-SCIENCE.md` and `MICRO-WIND.md`; this doc is the craft.

## 1. The animal, in the rut

- **When.** The Ontario moose rut runs from the last week of September to
  about the middle of October, peaking around the first week of October.
  Cows come into heat for about a day; bulls travel, call and fight for the
  whole stretch. The rut is the only time a bull will come to you. [S/H]
- **Where they are.** Cut edges meeting conifer, cut edges touching water,
  the toe of an esker where it meets a bog, beaver-pond shores and the
  inlets to shallow bays, the saddles between drainages, isthmuses and the
  ends of long narrow lakes. South and south-west slopes have the browse;
  warm days push them to north aspects and dense conifer to bed. [S]
- **Daily rhythm.** Feeding at first light and last light, bedded through
  the middle of the day in cover, and more so the warmer it is. In the rut
  bulls move at all hours, cows less so. [S]
- **What he does when called.** A bull coming to a cow call very often
  swings round to get downwind of the caller before he shows. He hangs up
  at 45 to 75 m in the timber, where he is still covered, and checks the
  wind. If he gets your scent, he is gone without a sound. If the cow call
  keeps coming and the wind is clean, he comes in. This is what happened
  on 2026-09-27: 2.5 hours of cow calls with the odd grunt back, the bull
  pulled the hunter off the calling spot, then ran to get downwind once a
  last cow call set him going. [H/us]
- **How far he hears.** On a calm cold morning a cow call carries 2 km or
  more over water and open bog; in wind, a few hundred metres. Calm and
  cold carries sound farthest. [H]
- **His nose.** A moose's nose is the best thing about him. Scent at nose
  height, about 1 m, is what counts. Count on him smelling you at 300 m
  downwind on a steady breeze, and at 100 m in any direction on a calm
  evening when the air pools. [H] The app's cone is this, drawn.

## 2. Weather and behaviour

What the forecast means for the hunt, in the order it matters.

- **Temperature is the one that is measured.** It is the only weather
  variable with a consistent effect on movement in collared deer, and the
  moose data agree: fall heat stress begins near 14 °C. Over 14 °C in the
  day they cut daytime movement and seek shade and water; over 20 °C
  little moves except at dawn and dusk. Below 10 °C they are up and
  about. [S]
- **A warm spell beds them; the first cool day after it moves them.** The
  third day or later of highs of 18 °C or more is a slow day. The first
  morning after a drop of 5 °C or more (or a high back under 14 °C) is the
  day to be in the stand before light. [S/H] The app's "recent days"
  factor is exactly this.
- **Wind.** Moose rest more as the wind rises (a weak but consistent
  effect) and calling collapses in it: under 10 km/h call freely; 10 to
  20 km/h call less and sit the edges; over 20 km/h stop calling, still-hunt the lee slopes and the dense conifer where they bed out of it. A
  windy day is a day for sign, for scouting, for the lee. [S/H]
- **Rain.** Moose are more active in rain than you would think (collar
  data). Light rain quietens the woods and is the best still-hunting
  weather there is. Heavy rain (over 3 mm/h) kills calling and visibility
  both. After rain the sign is fresh and readable. [S/H]
- **Pressure and fronts.** No evidence in the literature that the barometer
  itself moves moose or deer. What a passing front does is drop the
  temperature and clear the sky, and those matter. Hunt the day after a
  cold front for the temperature, not the pressure. [S]
- **Cloud.** An overcast cool day extends the morning feed into midday:
  stay in the stand. [S]
- **Moon.** No measured effect on deer; nothing published for moose.
  Display only. [S]
- **Snow.** The first snow is a gift: tracks say everything, and a bull in
  the open against it is a bull seen. Moose move freely in it until it is
  deep. [H]
- **The air on the ground is not the forecast wind.** On a clear evening
  the ground cools, the air at head height comes loose from the wind above
  and the cold air drains downhill into the bogs and the lake, carrying
  scent with it, however the forecast arrow points. From an hour before
  sunset through the night: scent runs downhill and pools in hollows,
  creek bottoms, low corners of cutovers and over the water. From an hour
  after sunrise until mid-afternoon on a sunny day: it rises up the slopes.
  Morning: sit level with or below the moose, across the slope. Evening:
  sit above him. Never sit in a bowl or a saddle in the evening. [S/H]
  `MICRO-WIND.md` is the whole story; the app's ground wind and cone are
  this, per spot and minute.
- **A slot in the trees channels the wind along it**, whatever the wind
  above is doing. A tree-walled bog strip running north-west took a
  southerly and sent it up the strip toward 306° on 2026-09-29. In a slot,
  expect the wind along the slot, and check it with powder. [us]

## 3. The days in camp

A week is long enough to learn the ground and short enough that every
morning counts. Lay it out rather than wander.

- **Day 0, the drive in.** Look at the week: which days are cool, which
  warm, which windy, which wet. Mark the two or three best dawns (cool,
  calm, clear or light cloud) and the best dusks. Those are the sits; the
  rest is scouting, sign, and still-hunting the lee on the windy days.
  The app's Spots tab scores the day; the strip gives the week.
- **Scout the first full day**, on foot and by boat, in the middle of the
  day when nothing is moving anyway. Walk the edges, the shore, the cut
  lines; read the sign (section 4); puff powder in the places you mean to
  sit and log it (Sharpen the wind), so the model is right there by evening.
  Sort the sits into morning sits (below the animal, across the slope)
  and evening sits (above him, out of the pooling air).
- **Each sit is a plan of three things:** where the moose is likely to be
  and to come from (browse, water, bedding, the sign), where the scent goes
  from the stand at that hour (the cone), and where the shot is (the lanes,
  section 7). If the scent goes toward where he comes from, it is not a
  sit, it is a different sit.
- **Morning:** in place half an hour before legal light, from downwind or
  cross-wind of the approach, by the route that keeps you out of the open
  and off the ridge line. Call sparingly at first light; listen more than
  you call. Sit until mid-morning on a cool day, longer under cloud.
- **Midday:** sign, scouting, the next sit's powder check, a sleep.
- **Evening:** in place two hours before dark. The last hour is the hour.
  Cow calls every twenty minutes or so, with a grunt and a thrash now and
  then, and quiet between. Stay until the end of legal light; he comes late.
- **Rest a sit.** A spot called hard for two sits with nothing is burnt
  for a few days: the bull that heard it has made up his mind. Move 500 m
  or more, or change the hunt. [H]
- **The last two days** are for the best spot found so far, hunted
  morning and evening, with the wind right. Do not scatter.
- **Keep the log.** Every sighting, sound, bit of sign and blank sit goes
  in the hunt log on the day, with where it was. Next year's plan starts
  from it. The app pulls the heat map toward fresh sign and pushes it away
  from blank sits, so the log is not just a diary.

## 4. Sign, and what to do with it

- **Tracks.** A bull's front track is 13 to 15 cm long and rounded; a
  cow's narrower and more pointed; a calf's half the size beside hers.
  Fresh in mud: sharp walls, water not yet settled in them. In the first
  hours the edges are crisp; by the next day they are rounded and dry.
  Direction of travel plus time of day is a pattern: the same trail at the
  same hour is a route, and a route is a stand. [H]
- **Droppings.** Fall pellets are firm and brown; a pile of soft, clumped
  droppings is summer feed or a cow in the rut. Warm or shiny is today. [H]
- **Rubs and thrashes.** A bull rakes brush and small trees with his
  antlers from the start of the rut: broken saplings, bark stripped at
  chest height, a smell of him. Fresh (white wood, hanging bark) means he
  is working this ground this week. A rub line along an edge is his
  circuit. [H]
- **Wallows (rut pits).** A bull paws a pit in wet ground, urinates in it
  and rolls. The smell carries 100 m. He comes back to it, and cows come
  to it. A fresh wallow is the best sign there is: a stand downwind of it
  within 60 m, with a lane, is the sit of the week. [H]
- **Beds.** Flattened ovals in cover, often on a bench or a north slope
  on a warm day, several together for a cow and calf. Where they bed says
  where they go at dusk; do not hunt the beds, hunt the way out of them.
- **Browse.** Fresh-bitten willow, red osier, birch, aspen tips at 1.5 to
  2.5 m, white and wet at the break. A browse line along a cut edge or a
  shore is the evening feed.
- **The rule.** Sign tells you where he was. Wind tells you where you can
  be. A stand is where the two agree with a lane between them. When they
  do not agree, go and read more sign rather than sit the wrong wind.
- **Log it.** Sign goes in the hunt log with a photo if it is a wallow or
  a rub line. The log's pull on the heat map is strongest for sightings
  and sounds, softer for sign; a blank sit pushes its surroundings down
  for a couple of days.

## 5. Calling, and the bull that answers

- **The calls.** The cow call: a long, nasal, rising-and-falling moan, 3 to
  8 seconds, two or three in a row, then silence; the most important call
  in the rut. The bull grunt: a short, deep "uhh", singly or in a slow
  series, the sound of a bull walking in; it tells a bull there is another
  bull on his cow, and he comes to fight or sheers off. Thrashing: antlers
  on brush, with a dry branch or a paddle; the same message. Water: a
  canoe paddle poured slowly from a height into the lake is a cow
  urinating, and it carries. [H]
- **How often.** A cow call sequence every 15 to 30 minutes at the start,
  with long listening between, more often in the last hour of light. Over-calling educates him. A bull that has answered once can be left in
  silence for ten minutes; he will come looking. [H]
- **He answers.** A grunt, a thrash, a branch breaking, a splash: put it on
  the map at once (the Heard button, tap where it was). The app joins his
  sounds up in order and draws the way he is moving, and his likeliest way
  round to wind you. Then:
  1. **Stop and read.** Which way is he moving? Where is your scent going
     right now? If his line meets your scent before it meets your lane, you
     have a few minutes.
  2. **Fix the scent problem, not the calling.** Move cross-wind, quietly,
     to put your scent where he is not going; or have the second hunter
     (section 8) take the shot from the cross-wind stand.
  3. **Give him a reason.** A soft cow call when he goes quiet, a grunt
     and a thrash if he is hung up and grunting back. A bull hung up at 60
     m in the timber wants to see a cow or a rival; a thrash from behind
     your position pulls him forward.
  4. **Hold still at the end.** The last 40 m he comes in looking. The
     movement that kills the hunt is the hunter's.
- **The cow that answers.** A cow's moan answering yours is a cow with a
  bull near her more often than not. Keep calling, softer; the bull is the
  one that moves.
- **Silence is not nothing.** A bull will come in without a sound,
  especially a mature one. Sit the full time.

## 6. Interactions and scenarios

The situations that have come up, and the move for each. Add to this
after every hunt.

- **He circles downwind (the 2026-09-27 hunt).** The bull answers from
  up-wind, works round the caller's flank, then goes hard for the downwind
  side on the last call. Counter: the caller 40 to 50 m up-wind of the
  shooter, so his swing passes the shooter in the cross-wind. One hunter:
  move to the cross-wind side of your own calling spot between calls, with
  the lane looking back at the spot. The app's red dashed swing is the line
  to watch; the second person on the scent card is the shooter's spot to
  find.
- **Angle in.** Never walk straight at a sound or a stand. Come in on an
  angle that keeps you cross-wind of where he is or will be, so your scent
  streams past him rather than at him, and so that when you stop you are
  looking along the line he will use rather than down it. Approach a stand
  by the route the app's Easiest route gives from the downwind side, and
  stop short of the open.
- **He is downwind already.** The swing says "he is on your downwind side".
  Do not call again: it tells him where the scent comes from. Move, cross-wind, 100 m or more, and start over in twenty minutes from the new spot,
  or let him go and hunt him tomorrow from the other side.
- **Two bulls answer.** A fight is coming; the cow call brings both. The
  loser leaves by the downwind side. Stay put and let them sort it; the
  winner comes to the cow.
- **A cow with a calf comes in.** Let her settle; the bull may be behind
  her, and he will come to a grunt now. Cow calls stop (she will not come
  to another cow); grunts and a thrash now and then.
- **Moving through thick bush.** The Bow view: open lanes are clear on the
  imagery, thick bush is dark. Move from opening to opening, stop at each
  edge, look and listen for a full minute. Still-hunting is nine parts
  standing.
- **A bull seen across water.** Sound carries over water: a cow call from
  the point brings him round the shore or across. Morning on a point pushes
  your scent over the water (good); afternoon lake breeze carries it
  inland (bad).
- **He runs.** A moose that has winded you runs 300 m and stops to look
  back. Do not chase. Mark the spot, note the wind, come back the next day
  from the other side; a rutting bull does not leave his ground for one
  fright.

## 7. The stand and the second lane

- **A stand is a lane first.** With a bow, 20 to 40 m of clear line to
  where he will stop; with a rifle, as far as the ground gives but with a
  backstop. The Bow view's clear ground is the lane; the dark is where the
  arrow stops.
- **Where he stops** is where the cover ends on his side: the edge of the
  timber, 45 to 75 m from the call, looking across. Put the lane where that
  edge is, not where the call is.
- **The second lane.** He will not come the way you planned. Every stand
  wants a second lane, 60 to 90° round from the first, on the downwind or
  cross-wind side: it is where he actually appears after the swing. Cut it
  (a few saplings, no more) the day before, from a stand position you can
  turn to without stepping. With two hunters, the second lane is the second
  hunter's stand.
- **Trim with the ground wind, not the forecast.** A lane that opens
  toward where the scent goes at the hour you sit is a window he looks
  through at you. Check the cone for the hour of the sit.
- **Downwind arc open.** Score a site by whether the downwind arc is
  visible and shootable, because that is where he goes to wind you. If the
  downwind side is a thicket, he will stand in it and leave.
- **Background and light.** Sit with the dark behind you and the light on
  the lane: morning facing west, evening facing east, where the ground
  allows. The sun in his eyes, not yours.
- **Mark it.** A stand found is a stand pinned (the Pin on the tap popup),
  with the lanes noted, so next year starts there.

## 8. Communal, not app-based

The app is one phone; the hunt is the party. The things that make a camp
hunt work are done at the table and on the shore, out loud.

- **The evening plan, every evening.** Who sits where in the morning, who
  calls and who shoots at each stand, the wind expected at the hour, the
  routes in, the time to be there, and the time to be back. The map is
  looked at together; the plan is said aloud and repeated back. Changes in
  the morning are said before anyone leaves.
- **Caller and shooter.** Two hunters at a stand: the caller 40 to 50 m
  up-wind of the shooter, the shooter at the cross-wind lane. Agree before
  the sit who shoots, at what, and the signal for "he is coming" (a hand,
  not a voice). The scent card with two people is for laying this out the
  evening before.
- **What goes in the log, and who.** Everyone's sounds, sightings and
  sign, on the day, by whoever saw it; one phone per party keeps the log
  if that is easier, with the others calling theirs in at supper. The wind
  checks each phone makes are shared as a file at supper too (Weather tab,
  share), so every phone's ground wind has the party's checks by morning.
- **The debrief.** After a hunt, five minutes: what was heard and where,
  what the wind did against what was expected, what the bull did. That
  is where the scenarios in section 6 come from, and where the model's
  misses get written down.
- **The radio or the satellite messenger** carries three things: a moose
  down, a change of plan, a hunter overdue. Not chat; a phone in a pocket
  stays off.
- **The young and the new.** Pair a new hunter with the caller, not the
  shooter: they learn the calls and the reading of the bull from the
  spot where it happens.
- **The camp rhythm.** Up at 4:30 on a sit morning, boat by 5, in place
  by first light. Back for a late breakfast. Out again at 3. Supper after
  dark, the plan, bed. The week is won on the mornings nobody wanted to
  get up for.

## 9. After the shot

- **Mark the spot where he stood and where you stood** (Pin, both) before
  you move, and the time. A hit moose can go 200 m; a map pin is worth
  an hour of circling.
- **Wait.** Twenty minutes on a good hit, an hour or more on a doubtful
  one, longer still with a bow. Watch the line he took and listen.
- **Track** with the blood, the broken brush and the tracks; one person
  tracks, the others stand to the side and watch ahead. Mark each blood
  find as you go (the Log, "sign") so the line can be picked up again.
- **Then it is work.** Quarters, a game cart or the boat, the tag on the
  animal, the hide and the cuts out of the sun. Plan the pack-out route
  on the map (Easiest) before the first cut.

## 10. The checklist

For each sit, in the order it is thought about:

1. Which day and hour, and why (cool, calm, after a drop).
2. Where he is likely to be and to come from (sign, browse, water, beds).
3. Where the scent goes at that hour from that spot (the cone; a powder
   check when you get there, logged).
4. The lane, and the second lane.
5. Who calls, who shoots, where each stands, the signal.
6. The route in from the downwind side, and the time to be in place.
7. The log afterwards: what happened, what the wind did, what he did.
