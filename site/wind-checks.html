Field notes on wind checks

A few puffs of powder tune the wind.

The forecast gives you the wind 10 m up, across a 2.5 km square. Your powder tells you what it's doing where you stand, right now. Groundwind weighs one against the other and works out how far the air here runs from the forecast today. Here's how many puffs that takes, where to take them and how long they hold.

By Gavan. 13 October 2026, a 7-minute read.

What a check does

Puff your powder, tap Sharpen, then the arrow the powder follows and how hard it's blowing. That one check works at three scales.

  • Right away, on your ground. The ground wind inside the check's ring turns to what you felt, and your scent cone turns with it.
  • From the second check, across the area. With two or more checks in a day, the app works out how far the air above the trees runs from the forecast: a turn and a speed. The card puts it in words, like "the air above the trees runs 20° left of the forecast at about half its speed". The streaks and cones then turn across 2 to 3 km round your checks, about the size of the forecast's own square.
  • Over the season, by kind of air. Each check is also a lesson for its kind of air: cold air draining, a lake breeze, a slot in the trees. The map carries it to the same air elsewhere and lets it go over about two weeks.

How far the forecast runs from the air

How much a puff should count depends on how far off the forecast usually is, so we measured it. At about 1,100 Environment Canada weather stations, over 29 days this fall, we compared the HD forecast (HRDPS), run through Groundwind's terrain wind, with what each station measured, in legal light: half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset. That's 330,000 station-hours (ECCC; HRDPS; Open-Meteo).

That's 10 m up in the open, which is what a forecast is made for, and it does that job well. The first and last three hours of light run the same as midday. In the mountains it runs looser, about 30°. Under the trees at head height the place adds its own turn, and that part is what the ground wind models and your checks catch.

Two things follow. In a breeze the forecast is seldom far off, and one swinging puff shouldn't overrule it. In light air the forecast's direction is a loose guide, and two puffs say more than it does.

A puff is rough too

Powder swings. At our own sits, puffs taken a few minutes apart differed by about 47°, and by more in light air. So the app reads each puff as good to about 50° either way, and lets your puffs outvote the forecast only as far as they agree with each other. Weather centres use the same arithmetic to fold measurements into a forecast: each side counts by how far off it usually runs (Kalnay 2003).

How many puffs it takes

Enough that the map goes at least half way from the forecast to what your puffs say. That depends on the wind:

The card counts for you. After each check it says how many more would settle the air where you are, or, in a steady breeze, that the forecast is seldom more than 15 to 20° off and your puffs mostly confirm it.

We tested this on 12,000 virtual sits at the same stations, all in legal light. Each puff was a station's real reading with a swing of ±60° added, rougher than our own, and the tuned wind was scored against what the station measured:

Most of the time the forecast is close and your puffs confirm it. What they're for is the bad day. In one sit in nine the forecast was more than 45° off when the puffs were taken, and eight puffs put it right in five of those six. Dawn and dusk came out the same. Steadier reads, like a puff watched for longer, do better still.

Where and when to puff

The rule

  • Two places, not one. Take the second puff 50 m or more from the first. One in the open, on a bog, a shore or a cutline, and one under the trees tells the app what the air above is doing and how much of it gets in to where you sit.
  • Spread them over the walk in and the sit. Each check is read against the forecast of its own minute, so one from the walk in still counts hours later.
  • Check again when the light changes. When the air switches from draining to rising, the old checks are about different air.
  • Wind held? Say so. Tap same as a while ago and the check is trusted twice as long.
  • Calm where you are, but the treetops moving? Tap treetops moving, calm here and say which way they lean. The treetops read the air above the trees, steadier than powder in a gusty spot.

How long a check holds

At the stations in legal light, a forecast's direction miss measured now is 60% still there an hour later, 45% after two hours, and about a quarter after six. The speed miss holds a little longer, and about a third of it belongs to the place, like shelter and how the ground lies, which lasts. Dawn and dusk change fastest: three hours on, a third of either is left.

The app fades your checks the same way. Half the turn is gone in under two hours, twice that if you said the wind has held, and a quarter of it stays until a day after your last check. The speed fades a little slower, to the third that stays.

Distance matters as well. At another station within 10 km, two-thirds of a miss holds, and past 10 km about a fifth. A check tunes your patch of bush, not the next lake over.

What it did on our own checks

At Pickle Lake, near White River, I logged 60 checks over seven days in late September and early October, 56 of them in legal light. We judged each one by the others from the same day, as if it hadn't been taken.

  • Direction: the ground wind on its own came within 45° of the powder on about 40% of checks, and about half with the day's other checks. The median miss came down from about 68° to about 52°.
  • Speed: within a factor of two on 40% of checks on its own, and nearly three in four with the others.

Each check is judged against one puff, and a single puff is rough, so no map can match every one. The day's other checks helped least at dawn and dusk. That's when light air swings the powder every way. On the lake shore one evening the wind came from the northwest, the west, the southeast and the northeast inside an hour and a half. That's the window we're working on next. Until then, puff where you'll sit, and the ring round that check does the work.

Every check teaches the map

Each check keeps what the forecast and the map said beside what you felt, and the hunt log keeps score of how often each was right. If you say yes when the app asks, your checks also come to us without your name or notes. That's how we measure all of this, and how the ground wind learns where it's wrong.

On 10 October, on the valley floor at Searchmont, the forecast had the wind from the south-southeast. My powder said it was coming from the northwest.

Tune the wind on your own ground.

Groundwind takes the HD forecast down to head height over the 1 m LiDAR ground and every stand of trees, and draws your scent cone on it. Every puff you log tunes it to the air that's really there. It works with no service.

Where the numbers come from

The station figures are our own measurements, made on public data. Here is where that data comes from, and the book on weighing measurements against a forecast.

  • Environment and Climate Change Canada. Surface weather observations (SWOB-ML) on the MSC Datamart. Free The stations' measured wind, hour by hour.
  • Environment and Climate Change Canada. High Resolution Deterministic Prediction System (HRDPS). Free The 2.5 km HD forecast.
  • Kalnay, E. 2003. Atmospheric Modeling, Data Assimilation and Predictability. Cambridge University Press. How weather centres weigh measurements against a forecast.
  • Open-Meteo. Historical Forecast API. Free Past HRDPS runs, so each hour is scored against what was forecast at the time.
